# Divider

# The Divider: A Senior Engineer's Complete Breakdown
The quietest component in the library — and the most overused. Here's when a line earns its keep, and how to build every kind correctly.

**Try it live:** the companion playground (built in a later pass) will let you tune orientation, inset, thickness, spacing tokens, and the text-label variant, and watch the semantic markup change underneath — `<hr>` for a real section break, a decorative border when the line is pure paint, a focusable `role="separator"` when it resizes a pane. For now this doc is the reference; the playground link lands when it's wired.

A Divider looks like the one component with nothing to teach. It's a line. But the line hides three genuinely different jobs — a semantic section break, a decorative rule, and an interactive resize handle — and picking the wrong one is the difference between a screen reader that navigates cleanly and one that announces "separator" fourteen times for lines that are just paint. This is a light component: no backend, and the security/performance surface is essentially nil. The weight is all in semantics and restraint.

## 1\. What a Divider Actually Is
A divider is a thin rule that separates content into groups. That's the whole job. But "a line on the screen" collapses three things that are semantically unrelated:

**Semantic separator (****`<hr>`****):** a real, meaningful break between sections of content — a thematic shift. Screen readers announce it and can navigate by it. This is a piece of _document structure_, not decoration.
**Decorative rule (a CSS border):** a line drawn purely to guide the eye — the hairline between list rows, the edge under a toolbar. It carries no meaning a screen-reader user needs; it should be invisible to assistive tech.
**Interactive separator (****`role="separator"`** **+** **`tabindex`****):** the draggable divider between two resizable panes (a code editor's sidebar, a split view). It's focusable, has a value, and responds to arrow keys. It is a _widget_, not a line.

The single most common mistake: reaching for `<hr>` (or worse, `role="separator"`) on every visual line. Most lines are decoration and should be a `border` or `box-shadow` that assistive tech never hears about. Rule of thumb: **if the line marks a genuine change in topic, it's an** **`<hr>`****; if it's there to make the layout breathe, it's a border; if you can drag it, it's a widget.** Three different things wearing the same 1px coat.
* * *

## 2\. Why It Matters
A divider carries less weight than a button, but it punches above its size in two directions — and both are invisible until they go wrong.

**It's a screen-reader landmark or noise, nothing in between.** A well-placed `<hr>` gives non-visual users the same "new section" cue white space and a line give sighted users. A wall of decorative `<hr>`s does the opposite — it clutters the accessibility tree with meaningless "separator, separator, separator" that buries the real structure. The divider is where teams accidentally _degrade_ accessibility while thinking they're adding polish.
**It's the #1 tell of an over-designed layout.** Dividers are the junk food of UI: cheap, satisfying, easy to over-consume. A screen crammed with rules reads as busy and anxious; the same screen with white space doing the separating reads calm and premium. Knowing when _not_ to draw the line is the senior skill. Most dividers a junior adds should have been eight more pixels of spacing.

For UJG's Afro-Futurist surfaces specifically, restraint reads as luxury. A single Goldenrod hairline over deep Eminence says more than five gray rules ever will.

## 3\. Anatomy of a Divider
Even a line has parts worth naming:

**The rule:** the visible line — **thickness** (usually 1px), **color** (low-contrast neutral for decoration, accent for emphasis), **style** (solid, rarely dashed).
**The spacing (the part that actually matters):** the margin above and below. A divider is 90% the space around it and 10% the line. Cramped dividers look broken; the breathing room _is_ the separation.
**Orientation:** horizontal (default, separating stacked blocks) or vertical (side-by-side items — a `Copy | Edit | Delete` row).
**Inset:** full-bleed edge-to-edge, or indented to align with content (Material's inset/middle variants).
**The label (optional):** a word floated in the middle — the "OR" between social login and an email form. Turns a plain rule into a text divider.
**The handle (interactive only):** the wider hit area and grip on a resizable split-pane separator.
* * *

## 4\. Spacing, Thickness & Tokens
A divider has no "sizes" the way a button does — it has **spacing scales** and a **thickness**, and both should be tokens, never magic numbers. The line is cheap; the rhythm is the design.

| Density | Space above/below | Thickness | Typical use |
| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| Tight | 8px (`0.5rem`) | 1px | Dense lists, menus, table rows |
| Default | 16px (`1rem`) | 1px | Most content sections |
| Comfortable | 24px (`1.5rem`) | 1px | Cards, settings groups |
| Section | 40px+ (`2.5rem`) | 1px–2px | Major thematic breaks, landing pages |

Token rules that matter:
**Drive spacing from your spacing scale**, not a hand-typed `margin: 17px`. The divider's rhythm should match everything else on the page.
**Thickness is almost always 1px.** On high-DPI screens a "1px" border is already crisp; 2px reads as heavy and is reserved for deliberate emphasis. Hairlines thinner than 1px (`0.5px`) render inconsistently across browsers — don't.
**Color is a low-contrast neutral for decoration** (think `oklch` neutrals tinted toward your brand hue) and only jumps to an accent when the divider is _meant_ to draw the eye. A decorative rule at high contrast fights the content.
**Never use color alone to carry meaning.** A divider is a visual convenience; the meaning must live in the markup (`<hr>`) or the surrounding text.

```css
:root {
  --divider-color: oklch(88% 0.01 305);   /* neutral, faint purple tint */
  --divider-thickness: 1px;
  --divider-space: 1rem;                    /* references the spacing scale */
  --divider-inset: 16px;
}
```

## 5\. States
A divider is mostly static, but the interactive variant has real states — and even a decorative rule has environmental states worth naming.

**Static divider states (decorative and semantic):**
**Visible (default)** — the line is rendered, spacing applied.
**Hidden / collapsed** — responsive breakpoints may hide a horizontal divider and replace it with spacing alone, or collapse a vertical divider on narrow screens.
**Transitioning** — if the divider animates in (e.g., a scroll-triggered reveal), it's mid-animation: fading opacity or scaling width.

**Interactive separator states (the resizable split-pane handle):**
**Rest** — the handle is visible at its default width/color, cursor `col-resize` or `row-resize`.
**Hover** — the handle widens or brightens to signal "grabbable." A common pattern: 1px visible → 4px on hover so the target is obvious.
**Focus** — keyboard focus lands on the handle (it has `tabindex="0"`); a visible focus ring appears. Arrow keys are active.
**Active / dragging** — pointer is down and moving; the handle follows the cursor. The two panes resize live. The cursor stays `col-resize`; the handle may change color to signal "engaged."
**At-min / at-max** — the handle has hit the clamp boundary. Arrow keys in that direction are no-ops; optional visual signal (a subtle bounce or color shift) to indicate the limit.
**Disabled** — rare, but if a split-pane is locked (e.g., during a loading state), the handle becomes non-interactive: `aria-disabled="true"`, cursor `default`, no drag response.

**The senior catch:** decorative dividers have no interactive states — they're paint. Giving them hover/focus effects is a bug, not a feature. Only the interactive separator earns states beyond "visible."
* * *

## 6\. Types & Variants
Same 1px line, several genuinely different components:

**Full-width (full-bleed):** runs edge-to-edge across its container. The default section break.
**Inset / middle:** indented on the leading side to align with content that's already indented (a list where text starts after an avatar) — Material's **inset**; inset on _both_ sides is **middle**.
**Vertical:** separates side-by-side items — action rows, stat groups, split headers. Needs `aria-orientation="vertical"` when semantic, and a height to exist at all (a vertical border has no intrinsic height).
**Text / label divider:** a word centered on the line — the classic **"OR"** between social login and an email form, or a "Today" separator in a chat log. Flexbox and two rule segments, not a background hack.
**Decorative rule:** pure styling — the hairline under a sticky header, grid lines between cards. A `border` or `box-shadow`, hidden from assistive tech, never an `<hr>`.
**Semantic separator (****`<hr>`****):** a genuine thematic break. The only variant that _should_ appear in the accessibility tree by default.
**Interactive / focusable separator:** the draggable divider between two resizable panes — `role="separator"`, `tabindex="0"`, `aria-valuenow/min/max`, arrow-key resize. A widget with state, covered in §9.5.

The distinction that trips everyone: **most "dividers" in a design are decorative and semantically invisible; a few are real** \*\*`<hr>`\*\***s; a rare one is an interactive widget.** Pick deliberately per line.
* * *

## 7\. When to Use One (and When Not To)
Use a divider when a group boundary needs to be _explicit_ and white space alone isn't reading clearly — a settings page with distinct sections, a menu separating "Edit" actions from "Delete," a form splitting "Account" from "Billing."

Reach for **white space instead** when:
Spacing already communicates the grouping. If 24px of margin reads as "these are separate," the line is redundant noise. Whitespace is the senior default; the divider is the exception you justify.
You're tempted to divide _every_ row. A rule between every single item looks like a 2003 spreadsheet. Use zebra striping, hover states, or just spacing; reserve rules for real group boundaries.
The container already has an edge — a card border, a background change, an elevation shadow. Two separators doing one job is one too many.

Placement heuristics: a rule under an `<h2>` as ornamentation is the heading's `border-bottom` (decorative), not an `<hr>`; give the line real breathing room on both sides; and draw one divider between two groups, not a fence around each — you want a rhythm, not a grid.

The through-line: **the best divider is often no divider.** Name the grouping with space first; add the line only when space isn't enough.

## 8\. Dividers Across Design Systems
Same line, different philosophies and prop names.

**Material Design (Google):** a first-class `Divider` with **full-width**, **inset**, and **middle** variants plus a dedicated **vertical** one. Inset aligns with content that starts after an icon/avatar. Material is explicit: use them sparingly, spacing first.
**Apple HIG (iOS/macOS):** `Divider` (SwiftUI) and table-row separators. Apple leans on thin hairlines and inset list separators, and on whitespace over rules elsewhere — `List` draws them for you.
**Fluent (Microsoft):** a `Divider` that can carry **content** (a centered label) natively, plus alignment props. The labeled divider is a built-in, not a hack.
**Ant Design / enterprise:** a rich `Divider` with `orientation` for the label, `dashed`, `plain`, and vertical `type`. Built for dense admin UIs where explicit grouping earns its place.
**Tailwind / utility-first:** no component — a `<hr class="border-0 h-px bg-neutral-200 my-4">` or a `border-t` on a wrapper, plus the responsibility to remember which lines should be semantic.
**shadcn/Radix:** a `Separator` primitive with a `decorative` prop that flips between `role="separator"` and `aria-hidden` for you. The model to copy.
**Bootstrap:** a framework-styled `<hr>` plus the `.vr` vertical-rule utility. No component API; markup + utilities.

The pattern worth stealing is Radix's: **one prop (****`decorative`****) that decides whether the line is announced or silent.** That single boolean is the whole accessibility story.

## 8b. Visual Styles (the same divider, eleven aesthetics)
A divider is nearly all skin, so the styles change it more than you'd expect — but the semantics (`<hr>` vs decorative vs widget), keyboard behavior, and the a11y contract never move.

**Flat:** a single crisp hairline, one neutral color, no shadow. The honest default.
**Material:** a 1px rule in the system neutral, full-width or inset, sometimes with a subtle elevation edge where a scrolled surface meets a bar.
**Glassmorphism:** a translucent low-opacity hairline over a blurred surface. Watch contrast — a glass divider can vanish entirely.
**Liquid Glass (the 2026 trend):** Apple's iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe material — a refractive seam with a faint specular rim rather than a flat line, catching light where two glass panes meet. Web = honest approximation; SwiftUI's material handles it natively.
**Neumorphism:** the "engraved" line — paired light-top / dark-bottom inset shadow pressed into the surface. Contrast-poor, accent only.
**Skeuomorphism:** the classic 3D groove — a dark line above a light one, mimicking a bevel. The `<hr>` of 2008.
**Neo-Brutalism:** a thick, hard, black rule — 2–4px, zero softness, high contrast. The divider as a slab.
**Claymorphism:** a soft, rounded, slightly inset channel with a gentle glow; a puffy seam, not a hairline.
**Aurora / Gradient:** fades from transparent → color → transparent, or a subtle multi-hue gradient. Premium; honor `prefers-reduced-motion` if it animates.
**Minimal / Swiss:** often _no_ line at all — whitespace and the grid separate. When a rule appears, it's a precise, faint hairline.
**UJG Brand:** a Goldenrod hairline (or a transparent→Goldenrod→transparent fade) over deep Eminence/Night, occasionally with a warm glow. One confident line, not a fence.

**Rule that never changes:** style is skin, behavior is the skeleton. A decorative divider stays out of the accessibility tree no matter how ornate; a semantic `<hr>` stays an `<hr>`; the interactive separator keeps its role, `tabindex`, and arrow-key resize in every skin. Guard glass/liquid-glass/neumorphism for contrast (an invisible divider has failed at its one job), and aurora/liquid-glass for reduced-motion. Full guidance in the 🎨 Design Styles (visual languages) ([https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-81211/838qa-200551](https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-81211/838qa-200551)).

## 9\. The Code
Real, production-shaped snippets. A divider is light, so this section is proportionate — the honest surface is `<hr>`, a decorative border, a text divider, a vertical divider, one genuinely interesting interactive widget (the resizable split-pane separator), framework one-liners, and tests.

### 9.1 HTML (the foundation)

```plain
<!-- Semantic: a real thematic break. Announced by screen readers. -->
<section>…first topic…</section>
<hr>
```

```plain
<!-- Decorative rule: pure paint. Keep it OUT of the a11y tree. -->
<div class="rule" role="presentation"></div>
```

```plain
<!-- Vertical divider between inline actions -->
<div class="toolbar">
  <button type="button">Copy</button>
  <div class="vr" role="separator" aria-orientation="vertical"></div>
  <button type="button">Delete</button>
</div>
```

Key HTML rules:
**`<hr>`** **is the semantic separator, and it means** **_thematic break_** — a genuine shift in topic, not "I want a line here." It carries an implicit `role="separator"` and is announced. Don't sprinkle it for decoration.
**Decorative lines shouldn't be** \*\*`<hr>`\*\***s.** Use a `border` on an adjacent element, or if you must have an element, hide it (`aria-hidden` / `role="presentation"`). Every decorative `<hr>` is one more "separator" your screen-reader users hear for no reason.
**`<hr>`** **is a void element** — no closing tag, no children. A text divider ("OR") is therefore _not_ an `<hr>`; it's a labeled construct (see §9.3).

### 9.2 CSS (the decorative rule, done right)
The overwhelming majority of "dividers" are this: a border, not an element. Cheaper, lighter, and silent to assistive tech by default.

```css
/* The senior move: the line lives on the content, not as its own element. */

  border-top: var(--divider-thickness) solid var(--divider-color);
```

```css
/* A styled <hr> when you DO want it semantic */
hr {
  border: 0;                          /* reset the ugly default 3D groove */

  background: var(--divider-color);
  margin-block: var(--divider-space); /* logical prop = RTL-safe */
```

}

```css
/* Inset variant (Material-style) */

```


### 9.3 The Text / Label Divider ("OR")
The "OR" between login options. Built with flexbox and two rule segments — never with a background-image hack or a single `<hr>` you try to write text on.

```plain
<div class="divider-text" role="separator" aria-label="or">
  <span aria-hidden="true">or</span>
</div>
```

```css
.divider-text {
  display: flex;
  align-items: center;
  gap: 12px;
  color: var(--divider-label-color);
  font: 500 12px/1 system-ui, sans-serif;
  text-transform: uppercase;
  letter-spacing: 0.08em;
}
.divider-text::before,
.divider-text::after {
  content: "";
  flex: 1;                                   /* each rule takes half the space */
  height: var(--divider-thickness);
  background: var(--divider-color);
}
```

Accessibility note: the visible "or" is `aria-hidden`, and the label rides on the container's `aria-label`, so the separator is announced once with its label rather than as loose text. If the "or" is meaningful running text rather than a separator, drop the `role`/`aria` entirely and let it be a normal element — don't over-annotate.

### 9.4 Vertical Divider
A vertical rule needs a height (a border on a zero-height element draws nothing) and, when semantic, an explicit orientation.

```plain
<div class="stats">
  <span>1.2k posts</span>
  <span class="v-divider" role="separator" aria-orientation="vertical"></span>
  <span>340 following</span>
</div>
```

```css
.stats { display: flex; align-items: center; gap: 12px; }
.v-divider {
  width: var(--divider-thickness);
  align-self: stretch;                 /* inherit the row's height */
  min-height: 1em;                     /* fallback if the row has no height */
  background: var(--divider-color);
}
```

If the vertical divider is purely decorative (the usual case between inline stats), drop `role="separator"` and add `aria-hidden="true"` — the same "is this meaningful?" test as everywhere else.

### 9.5 The Interactive Separator (resizable split-pane, React + TS)
The one genuinely hard divider: the draggable handle between two resizable panes (a sidebar, a code/preview split). It is a **widget**, not a line — `role="separator"`, focusable, with a value and keyboard resize. This is the block worth reading closely.

```typescript
  children: [React.ReactNode, React.ReactNode];
  label?: string;
```

```typescript
  min = 15,
  max = 85,
  defaultSize = 50,
  step = 2,

  const containerRef = useRef<HTMLDivElement>(null);
```

const clamp = (n: number) => Math.min(max, Math.max(min, n));

```typescript
// Pointer drag → convert cursor X to a percentage of the container.
  const onPointerDown = useCallback((e: React.PointerEvent) => {
    e.preventDefault();
    const el = containerRef.current;
    if (!el) return;
    (e.target as HTMLElement).setPointerCapture(e.pointerId);

      const pct = ((ev.clientX - rect.left) / rect.width) * 100;
      setSize(clamp(pct));
    };
    const up = () => {
      window.removeEventListener("pointermove", move);
      window.removeEventListener("pointerup", up);
    };
    window.addEventListener("pointermove", move);
    window.addEventListener("pointerup", up);
```

}, \[min, max\]);

```typescript
      case "ArrowLeft":  setSize((s) => clamp(s - step)); break;
      case "ArrowRight": setSize((s) => clamp(s + step)); break;
      case "Home":       setSize(min); break;
      case "End":        setSize(max); break;
```

Why this is the senior version:
**`role="separator"`** **with** **`aria-valuenow/min/max`** turns a drag handle into a widget a screen reader can report ("separator, 50 percent"). Without the value attributes it's an unlabeled mystery.
**`tabIndex={0}`** **\+ arrow keys** is the whole accessibility story — a divider you can only _drag_ is unusable by keyboard and touch-switch users. Home/End snap to the limits.
**The hit area (****`flex: 0 0 8px`****) is wider than the visible 1px line.** Nobody can grab a 1px target; the widget is 8px+ even though it looks like a hairline.
**`touchAction: "none"`** **\+ pointer capture** make the drag work on touch without the page scrolling out from under it.
**Clamping** keeps the panes within `min`/`max` so a user can't drag one pane to zero and lose it.

Note the split: a plain divider (`<hr>`) has an implicit `role="separator"` but is **not focusable and has no value** — it's static structure. Adding `tabindex` + `aria-valuenow` is exactly what promotes it from "line" to "widget." Never put those on a decorative rule.

### 9.7 shadcn/ui (Radix Separator — the model to copy)
Not a line you draw; a primitive that gets the semantics right for you.

```typescript
import * as SeparatorPrimitive from "@radix-ui/react-separator";
```

import { cn } from "@/lib/utils";

```typescript
export function Separator({
  className,
  orientation = "horizontal",
  decorative = true,          // default true — most dividers are decoration
  ...props
}: React.ComponentProps<typeof SeparatorPrimitive.Root>) {
  return (
    <SeparatorPrimitive.Root
      decorative={decorative}   // true → aria-hidden; false → role="separator"
      orientation={orientation}
      className={cn(
        "shrink-0 bg-border",
        orientation === "horizontal" ? "h-px w-full" : "w-px self-stretch",
        className
      )}
      {...props}
    />
  );
}
```

The `decorative` prop is the entire accessibility contract in one boolean: `decorative={true}` renders `aria-hidden` (silent), `decorative={false}` renders `role="separator"` with the right `aria-orientation` (announced). Defaulting it to `true` encodes the correct assumption — most dividers are decoration.

### 9.9 Testing the Divider
A divider is mostly structure, so tests are short — but the interactive separator earns real behavioral tests. Skip testing colors and pixel thickness (implementation detail); test **semantics** and, for the widget, **keyboard resize**.

```typescript
import { render, screen } from "@testing-library/react";
```

### 9.10 Backend, Security & Performance — the short, honest version
A divider **never reaches the server**, holds no state a backend cares about, carries no user input, and is not an attack surface. There is no endpoint, no validation, no CSRF story. Saying so plainly is more useful than inventing a section to match the Button doc.

Performance is equally quiet, with two real notes:
**Prefer a** **`border`** **over an extra element.** In long lists (thousands of rows), a `border-top` on each row — or Tailwind's `divide-y` — adds zero DOM nodes, while a `<hr>` per row adds one node each. Fewer nodes = less memory and faster layout. This is the only divider perf decision that ever matters.
**Don't animate a divider's** **`height`****/****`width`** if you ever transition one in — animate `opacity` or `transform: scaleX()` (compositor-only, no reflow). But most dividers don't animate at all, and that's correct.

## 10\. Accessibility (the whole game for this component)
For a divider, accessibility _is_ the component. Three rules cover almost everything:

**Decorative lines must be invisible to assistive tech.** If the line is pure styling, use a `border`/`box-shadow`, or if it's an element, `aria-hidden="true"` / `role="presentation"`. The failure mode — a screen reader announcing "separator" a dozen times for lines that mean nothing — is the single most common divider bug.
**Semantic breaks use** **`<hr>`** **(implicit** **`role="separator"`****). Use it only for genuine thematic shifts, so each announcement** **_means_** **something. One meaningful** **`<hr>`** **beats ten decorative ones.Vertical semantic dividers need** **`aria-orientation="vertical"`****.**
**The interactive separator is a widget:** `role="separator"` **+** `tabindex="0"` **+** `aria-valuenow`/`aria-valuemin`/`aria-valuemax`, an `aria-label`, and full arrow-key (plus Home/End) resize. A drag-only handle is inaccessible — keyboard operation is mandatory, and the hit target must be ≥ the usual touch minimum even though the visible line is 1px.
**Contrast:** a divider is a non-text element; WCAG's 3:1 non-text contrast target applies if the line is _conveying meaning_ (a real boundary). Purely aesthetic hairlines can be fainter, but a divider so faint it's invisible has failed at its only job — if it needs to be seen, it needs enough contrast to be seen.
**Never rely on the line alone to convey grouping to non-visual users.** The grouping should also live in structure — headings, landmarks, list semantics — with the divider as the visual reinforcement, not the sole signal.

## 11\. Innovative & Emerging Ideas
Even the humble line is evolving, mostly at the edges:
**Liquid Glass seams:** on Apple's 2026 platforms, the boundary between two glass surfaces becomes a refractive seam with a light-catching rim rather than a drawn line — the divider as a property of the material, not a separate element.
**Scroll-aware dividers:** a header's bottom rule that fades in only once content scrolls under it (a `border` toggled on scroll state), so the line appears exactly when it's needed and not before.
**Resizable-everything:** split-pane separators are spreading beyond code editors — dashboards, email clients, comparison tools — which makes the accessible interactive separator (§9.5) a library staple, not a one-off.
**Content-carrying dividers as a primitive:** Fluent and Ant already ship the labeled divider natively; expect the "OR" / date-separator variant to standardize as a real component rather than a per-project flexbox recipe.

## 12\. Conversion & UX Killers (the quiet divider mistakes)
None of these throw an error. They just make a screen feel cluttered, anxious, or inaccessible — and nobody files a bug for "too many lines."

**Over-dividing.** A rule between every single list row, every card, every section — the screen reads as a grid of fences instead of flowing content. Dividers signal _group boundaries_; if every item is its own group, nothing is grouped. Pull back to one rule per _real_ section break; let spacing handle the rest.

**Semantic** **`<hr>`** **spam.** Dropping an `<hr>` on every visual line means screen readers announce "separator" fourteen times on a page where two section breaks exist. The accessibility tree is cluttered with meaningless landmarks. Decorative lines must be borders or `aria-hidden` elements, not `<hr>`s.

**Invisible dividers.** A divider so faint it can't be seen has failed at its one job. If a decorative rule carries a real grouping signal (users would be confused without it), it must meet the 3:1 non-text contrast threshold. Glass/neumorphic styles are the usual offenders — beautiful mockups, invisible production.

**Dividers instead of spacing.** The #1 misuse. White space already communicates the separation; the line is redundant noise. Every divider should pass the "would 8px more margin do this job?" test before it earns its pixel.

**Interactive separator with no keyboard.** A drag-only resize handle is inaccessible to keyboard and switch users. If the handle exists, it must be `tabindex="0"` with arrow-key resize and `aria-valuenow` — or it's excluding a large user population silently.

**Vertical dividers with no height.** A CSS border on a zero-height element draws nothing; a `<span>` without `align-self: stretch` or a `min-height` is invisible. The divider "exists" in the DOM but renders as nothing — a ghost bug.

## 13\. Advanced Patterns
**Scroll-triggered reveal.** A header's bottom rule that fades in only once content scrolls beneath it — the line appears when it's _needed_ (scrolled content would otherwise merge with the header) and is absent when it's not. Driven by a scroll-state class or `IntersectionObserver`, not a scroll listener per frame. CSS: `border-bottom-color: transparent` → `var(--divider-color)` on `.header--scrolled`, transition on `opacity` or `color`.

**Animated reveal dividers.** A divider that `scaleX(0)` → `scaleX(1)` when a section enters the viewport. GPU-composited (transform), not a width animation (reflow). Always honor `prefers-reduced-motion` by snapping instead of animating.

**Compound dividers (icon or ornament on the line).** A decorative flourish (a diamond, a logo mark, a leaf) centered on the rule — the "ornamental break" pattern. Built like the text divider (§9.3): two rule segments with a centered icon between them, the icon `aria-hidden`.

**Responsive divider collapse.** A vertical divider between side-by-side stats that becomes a horizontal divider (or disappears into spacing) when the layout stacks on mobile. Controlled by `flex-direction` or a media query that swaps `width` ↔ `height` and `align-self` direction.

**Token-driven density switching.** Wire `--divider-space` to the app's density token so a "compact mode" toggle tightens every divider's breathing room in one swap — no per-instance overrides. The divider's spacing _is_ the rhythm token expressed visually.

**The infinite-line pattern.** A divider that bleeds full-width out of a padded container (`margin-inline: calc(-1 * var(--container-pad))`) to run edge-to-edge across the viewport. The "section break" vs "content-level" distinction made visual: full-bleed = major thematic shift, indented = sub-group.
* * *

## 14\. Performance & Bundle Cost
A divider is the lightest component in the library — but at scale, choices add up.

**Prefer a** **`border`** **over an element.** In long lists (thousands of rows), a `border-top` on each row — or Tailwind's `divide-y` — adds zero DOM nodes. A `<hr>` or `<div>` per row adds one node each. Fewer nodes = less memory, faster layout. This is the only divider performance decision that ever matters at scale.

**Don't animate** **`height`** **or** **`width`****.** If a divider transitions in, animate `opacity` or `transform: scaleX()` (compositor-only, no reflow). Animating dimensional properties triggers layout on every frame. Most dividers don't animate, and that's correct.

**`contain: paint`** **on the interactive separator.** A resize handle's pointer-move updates the pane widths every frame. Applying `contain: layout paint` to the split container isolates its reflow from the rest of the page — the browser doesn't relayout siblings on every drag frame.

**No library needed.** A divider never justifies a package dependency. It's an `<hr>`, a border, or at most a ~30-line React component for the interactive case. If your bundle includes a "divider library," something went wrong.
* * *

## 15\. Security
A divider has effectively zero attack surface. It renders no user input, accepts no data, reaches no server, and carries no state. Stating this plainly is more useful than inventing threats.

**The one real note: the interactive separator doesn't write to a server.** A split-pane handle changes local layout state (`aria-valuenow`). If you persist the split position (e.g., saving the user's sidebar width to a preference), that write endpoint needs the same auth and validation as any preference-save — but that's a "save preference" concern, not a divider concern.

**Dividers that render CMS content (the text/label variant).** If the "OR" or section label in a text divider comes from a CMS or user input, render it as text (escaped), not innerHTML. This is the same rule as every other component — framework interpolation handles it by default.

**No CSRF, no injection, no BOLA.** A divider is paint. It doesn't reach the server. It doesn't accept input. Ship it and move on.

## 16\. Senior-Level Checklist (ship-ready)
Before a divider is "done":
Asked "does whitespace already do this?" before adding the line at all.
Decorative lines are `border`/`box-shadow` (or `aria-hidden`), **not** `<hr>` — silent to AT.
Semantic breaks use `<hr>`, reserved for genuine thematic shifts.
The default 3D-groove `<hr>` is reset (`border: 0`) and restyled.
Spacing and thickness are tokens, not magic numbers; margins use logical properties (RTL-safe).
Vertical semantic dividers carry `aria-orientation="vertical"` and have a height.
Text/label dividers use flexbox + two rule segments; the label is announced once, not as loose text.
Interactive separators are focusable, arrow-key resizable (Home/End), report `aria-valuenow/min/max`, and have a ≥ real hit target.
Not fencing every row/section — a rhythm, not a grid.
Line has enough contrast to be seen if it conveys a boundary; reduced-motion respected if it animates.
