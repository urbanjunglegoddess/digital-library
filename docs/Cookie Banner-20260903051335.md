# Cookie Banner

# Cookie Banner
The most legally-loaded component on the web, and the one teams ship most carelessly. Here's the full breakdown: what the law actually asks for, how consent is enforced (not just shown), and production code that gates scripts instead of decorating them.

_Informational only — this documents engineering and UX practice around consent, not legal advice. Privacy obligations vary by jurisdiction and change; confirm specifics with counsel before you ship to regulated markets._
* * *

## 1\. What It Actually Is
A cookie banner (consent banner / consent management interface) is the control surface a site shows to collect, record, and honor a user's decision about non-essential cookies and tracking technologies before those technologies run. It is the visible front-end of a Consent Management Platform (CMP): the part that presents categories and choices, and the part that must _actually block_ scripts until the user opts in.

Four things get lumped together and shouldn't be:
*   **Cookie banner (consent):** asks permission for non-essential storage/tracking and gates it. The subject of this page. Its whole job is a legally-meaningful yes/no _before_ the tracker fires.
*   **Cookie** **_notice_** **(informational bar):** a passive "we use cookies" strip with only a dismiss/close. This is the pre-2018 pattern and is non-compliant on its own under GDPR/ePrivacy — dismissing is not consenting, and it usually sets cookies anyway.
*   **Privacy policy / privacy center:** a static document or a standing preferences page. The banner _links_ to these; it is not one of them. Consent must be re-accessible after the banner is gone (the persistent "Cookie settings" link).
*   **Age gate / geo gate / paywall:** other "interrupt before entry" overlays. They block content; a compliant cookie banner must _not_ block content just to coerce a choice (that's a dark pattern and, in the EU, "consent or pay" is heavily contested).

**Rule of thumb:** if it merely _tells_ you about cookies, it's a notice and it's not enough. If it collects a free, specific, informed, revocable choice **and prevents the tracker from running until you make it**, it's a consent banner. If the "Reject" path is harder than "Accept," it isn't compliant no matter what it's called.
* * *

## 2\. Why It Matters
This component is where privacy law, trust, and revenue collide, and where the failure modes are silent and expensive.

*   **Legal exposure is real and enforced.** ePrivacy (the "cookie law") + GDPR govern the EU/UK; CCPA/CPRA governs California; a growing patchwork (Colorado, Virginia, Connecticut, Quebec's Law 25, Brazil's LGPD) governs the rest. Regulators have fined banners specifically — for pre-ticked boxes, for "Accept" being one click while "Reject" was buried levels deep, and for setting analytics/ad cookies _before_ any interaction. The CNIL (France) alone has issued hundreds of millions in fines over exactly these banner patterns. The banner is often the single most-audited element on a site.
*   **A broken banner poisons every downstream metric.** If your banner sets cookies before consent, or fires GA/Meta Pixel regardless of choice, your "compliance" is theater and your analytics are legally tainted. Worse: if it _over_\-blocks or mis-implements Consent Mode, you lose real conversion data and can't tell why revenue reporting drifted.
*   **It's the first interaction and it sets the trust tone.** The banner is frequently the very first thing a visitor touches. A manipulative one (giant glowing "Accept All," a gray ghost "Reject") reads as adversarial and measurably raises bounce. A clean, honest one — equal buttons, one-click reject — signals a company that respects the person on the other end.
*   **Accessibility stakes are unusually high.** Because it appears on load and can be modal, a badly-built banner can trap keyboard and screen-reader users out of the entire site on their first visit — a total lockout, not a degraded experience. It is one of the few components whose accessibility bug is "the site is unusable," not "this control is awkward."
*   **Performance tax on the critical path.** The CMP loads before almost everything and decides what else is allowed to load. A heavy, render-blocking CMP delays first paint for _every_ visitor on _every_ page — the worst possible place to be slow.
* * *

## 3\. Anatomy
A compliant consent banner has far more parts than the visible bar. The invisible half (the store and the script gate) is where compliance actually lives.

*   **Container / surface:** the banner region itself. Commonly a bottom or corner sheet (non-modal) or a centered modal with backdrop (first-layer "consent wall"). Needs `role="dialog"` (or `alertdialog` if truly blocking) with an accessible name.
*   **Purpose statement:** the short, plain-language explanation of what's collected and why. Must be specific enough to make consent "informed" — not "we use cookies to improve your experience."
*   **Category toggles (the granular layer):** the heart of compliance. Minimum four categories: **Necessary/Strictly-Essential** (always on, non-togglable, exempt from consent), **Functional/Preferences**, **Analytics/Performance**, **Marketing/Advertising**. Each is a labeled switch with a description and, ideally, a vendor list.
*   **Primary actions — equal weight:** **Accept All** and **Reject All (Reject Non-Essential)** must be the same visual prominence and reachable at the same layer. **Save Preferences** confirms a custom mix.
*   **Manage Preferences / second layer:** expands the granular toggles. It is legitimate to hide the toggles behind one click, but Reject must remain at the _first_ layer next to Accept.
*   **Policy links:** cookie policy + privacy policy, inline and reachable by keyboard.
*   **Persistent re-entry affordance:** a standing "Cookie settings" link/floating icon so consent is revocable at any time _after_ the banner closes. Withdrawal must be as easy as granting.
*   **The consent store (invisible):** the persisted record — a first-party cookie and/or `localStorage` entry holding the per-category decision, a **consent string/version**, a **timestamp**, and an **expiry** for re-consent. This is what every gated script reads.
*   **The script gate (invisible, the real engine):** the mechanism that prevents non-essential tags from executing until the store says yes — `type="text/plain"` script swapping, a tag-manager consent trigger, or Google **Consent Mode v2** signal defaults. Without this, everything above is decoration.
*   **Signal listeners:** readers for **Global Privacy Control (****`navigator.globalPrivacyControl`****)** and legacy **DNT**, which pre-set or override the marketing/sale-of-data categories.
* * *

## 4\. Sizes / Scale / Density
"Size" for a banner is really _layer strategy_ and footprint. Three canonical footprints, each with real placement and dimension rules.

| Tier | Footprint | Height / Width | Token | Use |
| ---| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| Bar (non-modal) | Full-width bottom/top strip | `min-height: 72px`, auto-grow; `inset-inline: 0` | `--cb-bar-minh: 72px` | Low-friction sites; content stays interactive; Reject + Accept + Settings in one row on desktop |
| Box / Corner card | Anchored corner sheet | `width: clamp(320px, 92vw, 440px)`; `inset: auto 24px 24px auto` | `--cb-box-w: 440px` | Default for most sites; unobtrusive, still non-modal |
| Modal wall | Centered dialog + backdrop | `width: min(560px, 92vw)`; `max-height: min(640px, 88vh)`, internal scroll | `--cb-modal-w: 560px` | Strict-consent jurisdictions where you choose to block until decision. Only when a true modal is intended |
| Preferences layer | Expanded panel (any tier grows into it) | `+240px to +360px` height, or full modal body | `--cb-panel-gap: 16px` | Second layer holding the four category toggles |

Rules that matter:
*   **Every interactive control ≥ 44×44px** hit target (Accept, Reject, each toggle, each link). Toggles cramped into a dense list are the most common target-size failure here.
*   **Buttons driven by spacing tokens, not magic numbers**, so the banner scales with the design system. Accept and Reject share one size token — never size Reject smaller.
*   **Reserve layout space / avoid CLS.** The banner appears on load; render it with a fixed footprint or animate only `transform`/`opacity` so it doesn't shove content and tank Cumulative Layout Shift.
*   **Mobile:** the bar becomes a bottom sheet; stack Accept/Reject as equal full-width buttons — never Accept full-width and Reject a tiny text link.
*   **Internal scroll on the modal**, not page scroll, so long vendor lists don't break the trap or the layout.
* * *

## 5\. States
A consent banner has a lifecycle most teams under-build. Each state is distinct and must be handled.

1. **First visit / no stored consent (visible):** banner shown, **no non-essential cookies set yet**. This is the compliant default — silence is not consent, so nothing but strictly-necessary storage exists until the user acts.
2. **Preferences-collapsed (default view):** Accept All / Reject All / Manage Preferences visible; toggles hidden behind Manage.
3. **Preferences-expanded:** the four category switches are visible; Necessary is on and disabled, others reflect current/stored choice.
4. **Toggle states:** each category switch has **on**, **off**, and **locked-on** (Necessary) — the locked switch is visibly on, `aria-disabled`, and explained ("Always active").
5. **Accepted-all (dismissed):** store written with every category true; banner hidden; gated scripts now allowed to load.
6. **Rejected-all (dismissed):** store written with non-essential categories false; banner hidden; **gated scripts must NOT load**; only necessary storage remains.
7. **Custom-saved (dismissed):** a partial mix persisted; only the opted-in categories' scripts load.
8. **Saving / in-flight (optional):** if consent is logged server-side, a brief pending state on the confirm button; keep width stable, block double-submit.
9. **Error:** the consent write failed (storage blocked, network error logging). Fail **closed** — treat as no-consent, keep non-essential scripts off, surface a retry. Never silently "assume accept."
10. **Signal-preset (GPC/DNT):** GPC or DNT detected → marketing/sale category pre-set to off and the UI reflects that the browser already spoke.
11. **Re-prompt / re-consent:** stored consent has **expired** (typically 6–13 months) or the **consent version changed** (policy/vendor update) → banner reappears with prior choices as defaults.
12. **Withdrawn:** user reopened settings and revoked a category → store updated, and any already-loaded tag for that category is disabled/cleared going forward.

## 6\. Types / Variants
By layer strategy and by how much choice is surfaced up front.

*   **Notice-only bar (non-compliant, documented for contrast):** message + "Got it." No reject, no gating. Legal in almost no GDPR context; included so you can recognize and reject it.
*   **Two-button bar (Accept / Reject):** both equal, plus a "Manage" link to the second layer. The compliant minimum for many sites.
*   **Three-choice bar (Accept All / Reject All / Manage):** the common standard; granular toggles one click away.
*   **Granular-first (categories visible immediately):** the four toggles shown up front with per-category descriptions. Highest transparency; used by privacy-forward brands and strict interpretations.
*   **Modal consent wall:** centered dialog + backdrop that blocks the page until a choice is made. Legitimate _only_ when you intend a true modal; must still offer an equal-weight Reject and must not be used purely to coerce Accept.
*   **Corner sheet (non-modal):** anchored card that leaves the page usable — the friendliest default that's still compliant when it gates correctly.
*   **TCF / IAB Transparency & Consent Framework variant:** ad-tech-heavy sites use an **IAB TCF v2.2** CMP that presents the full vendor/purpose taxonomy and emits a standardized **TC string** downstream partners read. Heavier, purpose-built for programmatic advertising. (Covered lightly here — it's a specialized superset of the granular pattern.)
*   **Region-adaptive variant:** the banner's behavior changes by detected jurisdiction — opt-**in** (block until consent) in the EU/UK, opt-**out** ("Do Not Sell/Share" link + honor GPC) for California. One component, geo-aware defaults.
* * *

## 7\. When to Use (and When Not To)
**Use a consent banner when:**
*   You set or read **any non-essential cookie or storage** (analytics, ad pixels, A/B testing, embedded YouTube/Maps, session-replay, chat widgets that profile) and you have EU/UK/EEA visitors, or visitors under any opt-in regime.
*   You run **third-party tags** (GTM tags, Meta/TikTok/LinkedIn pixels, Hotjar, Intercom) that drop cookies or fingerprint.
*   You sell/share personal data or serve **California/US-state** consumers (here it may be an opt-**out** "Do Not Sell or Share" flow + GPC honoring, not a blocking banner).

**Don't use one — or use something else — when:**
*   You genuinely set **zero non-essential cookies** (truly essential-only: auth/session, CSRF, load-balancing, cart). Strictly-necessary cookies are **exempt** — adding a banner you don't need trains users to reflex-dismiss and can itself look like a dark pattern. Consider a small **privacy-policy link** instead of a banner.
*   Your analytics are **cookieless and server-side / privacy-first by design** (e.g. aggregate, no personal identifiers, no cross-site tracking) — in many readings no consent prompt is required; a transparency note in the privacy policy suffices. Verify per jurisdiction.
*   You need to interrupt for **age or geography** — that's an **age gate / geo gate**, a different overlay. Don't overload the cookie banner with unrelated gating.
*   You'd be tempted to block content purely to force Accept — don't. That's a **content wall / forced-consent** anti-pattern, not a legitimate banner.

**Placement heuristics:**
*   **Bottom sheet / corner card is the default** — visible, dismissible by choice, doesn't hijack the whole viewport.
*   **Reject lives at the first layer, beside Accept** — never only inside a second "Manage" screen.
*   **Persistent re-entry** ("Cookie settings" footer link or floating button) so consent is revocable forever after.
*   **Never cover the privacy-policy link or core nav** with the banner — the escape hatch must stay reachable.

## 8\. Across Design Systems
Consent UI is rarely a first-class design-system component (it usually ships from a CMP), but the primitives it's built from — dialog, switch, button — are, and each system's take shapes the banner.

*   **Material Design (Google):** builds it from the **Banner** pattern (a top/inline surface with up to two text actions) for the lightweight case, and **Dialog** + **Switch** for the granular layer. Consent Mode v2 is Google's own runtime contract the banner is expected to signal into. Elevation and a scrim distinguish modal from non-modal.
*   **Apple HIG (iOS/macOS):** no cookie-banner component per se, but the analog is the **App Tracking Transparency (ATT)** system sheet — a modal, equal-choice ("Ask App Not to Track" / "Allow") consent prompt. HIG's lesson: system-level, unavoidable, and _symmetrical_ choices. On web, Safari's ITP also caps cookie lifetimes, which affects your consent-expiry math.
*   **Fluent (Microsoft):** composes from **Dialog/ContentDialog** + **Toggle** + **Button**, using acrylic/mica for the surface. Microsoft's own consent banner (the "MSCC" pattern) is a widely-copied reference for equal Accept/Reject bars.
*   **Ant Design:** `Modal` or `Drawer` + `Switch` + `Button`, with `danger`/`type` props for emphasis. Enterprise/admin bias makes the granular multi-category layout natural.
*   **Tailwind / utility-first:** no prescribed banner; you compose the surface, toggles, and buttons from utilities and (usually) wrap it so Accept/Reject share one button component and can't drift apart in weight.
*   **shadcn / Radix:** the strong modern choice — `Dialog` (with proper focus trap + `Escape`), `Switch`, and `Button` from Radix primitives give you accessibility for free; you own the styling. The right foundation for a hand-rolled compliant banner.
*   **Bootstrap 5:** `Toast`/`Modal` + form `switch` + `btn` classes. Bootstrap's docs literally ship a cookie-consent example, which is why so many small sites' banners look identical.

### 8b. Visual Styles / Trends
The banner wears any skin (see §17), but 2026 trends specifically touch consent UI:
*   **Liquid Glass consent sheets** (Apple iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe): translucent, refractive corner cards over the page. Beautiful, but consent text over a blurred, moving backdrop is a **contrast trap** — pin the text on an opaque plate.
*   **Regulator-driven "symmetry" as a design constraint:** the strongest 2026 trend isn't aesthetic, it's legal — equal-weight buttons are now effectively mandatory (EDPB guidance, CNIL enforcement), killing the era of the ghost "Reject."
*   **One-click "Reject all" at the top layer** is becoming the default even for ad-tech CMPs after enforcement actions.
*   **Consent Mode v2 + server-side tagging** shifting the runtime from "block the script" to "signal-gated tags," so the banner's job is increasingly to emit correct _signals_, not just hide `<script>`s.
*   **Global Privacy Control honoring** moving from optional to expected (California treats GPC as a legally-binding opt-out signal).

```html
        <span>Strictly necessary</span>
      </label>
      <p id="cb-nec-d" class="cb-hint">Always active. Required for the site to function.</p>

      <label class="cb-row">
        <input type="checkbox" name="functional" aria-describedby="cb-fun-d">
        <span>Functional</span>
      </label>
      <p id="cb-fun-d" class="cb-hint">Remembers preferences like language and region.</p>

      <label class="cb-row">
        <input type="checkbox" name="analytics" aria-describedby="cb-ana-d">
        <span>Analytics</span>
      </label>
      <p id="cb-ana-d" class="cb-hint">Helps us understand aggregate usage.</p>

      <label class="cb-row">
        <input type="checkbox" name="marketing" aria-describedby="cb-mkt-d">
        <span>Marketing</span>
      </label>
      <p id="cb-mkt-d" class="cb-hint">Used to personalize and measure advertising.</p>
    </fieldset>
  </div>

  <!-- Equal-weight actions. Reject is a real, prominent button, not a link. -->
  <div class="cb-actions">
    <button type="button" data-cb="reject" class="btn btn--primary">Reject non-essential</button>
    <button type="button" data-cb="accept" class="btn btn--primary">Accept all</button>
    <button type="button" data-cb="manage" class="btn btn--ghost" aria-expanded="false" aria-controls="cb-panel">
      Manage preferences
    </button>
    <button type="button" data-cb="save" class="btn btn--secondary" hidden>Save preferences</button>
  </div>
</section>

<!-- Persistent re-entry so consent is revocable forever after -->
<button type="button" id="cookie-settings-link">Cookie settings</button>
```

Key HTML rules:
*   **`aria-modal="false"`** on a non-modal corner banner — lying about modality makes screen readers announce a trap that doesn't exist. Use `alertdialog` + `aria-modal="true"` **only** for a true blocking wall.
*   **Reject is a** **`<button>`** **of the same class as Accept.** The markup itself must encode equal weight.
*   Necessary is `checked disabled` and described as "Always active" — never a togglable illusion.
*   The banner is `hidden` by default and only revealed by JS _after_ confirming no valid stored consent exists, so it never flashes for returning users.

### 9.3 React + TypeScript (full reusable component + gate)

```tsx
import { createContext, useCallback, useContext, useEffect, useRef, useState } from "react";

export type Category = "necessary" | "functional" | "analytics" | "marketing";
export type Consent = Record<Category, boolean>;

interface StoredConsent {
  choices: Consent;
  version: number;      // bump to force re-consent on policy change
  ts: number;           // epoch ms, for expiry
}

const CONSENT_KEY = "ujg_consent";
const CONSENT_VERSION = 3;
const EXPIRY_MS = 1000 * 60 * 60 * 24 * 180; // 180 days
const DEFAULT: Consent = { necessary: true, functional: false, analytics: false, marketing: false };

function readStored(): StoredConsent | null {
  try {
    const raw = localStorage.getItem(CONSENT_KEY);
    if (!raw) return null;
    const parsed = JSON.parse(raw) as StoredConsent;
    if (parsed.version !== CONSENT_VERSION) return null;        // policy changed → re-consent
    if (Date.now() - parsed.ts > EXPIRY_MS) return null;        // expired → re-consent
    return parsed;
  } catch {
    return null; // corrupt store → fail closed, re-ask
  }
}

interface ConsentCtx {
  consent: Consent;
  ready: boolean;
  save: (c: Consent) => void;
}
const Ctx = createContext<ConsentCtx | null>(null);
export const useConsent = () => {
  const v = useContext(Ctx);
  if (!v) throw new Error("useConsent must be used within <ConsentProvider>");
  return v;
};

export function ConsentProvider({ children }: { children: React.ReactNode }) {
  const [consent, setConsent] = useState<Consent>(DEFAULT);
  const [ready, setReady] = useState(false);

  useEffect(() => {
    const stored = readStored();
    if (stored) setConsent(stored.choices);
    setReady(true);
  }, []);

  const save = useCallback((c: Consent) => {
    const next: Consent = { ...c, necessary: true };
    setConsent(next);
    const record: StoredConsent = { choices: next, version: CONSENT_VERSION, ts: Date.now() };
    try { localStorage.setItem(CONSENT_KEY, JSON.stringify(record)); } catch { /* private mode: keep in memory */ }
    // Signal Google Consent Mode v2 + log server-side (see 9.6 / 9.13)
    window.gtag?.("consent", "update", {
      analytics_storage: c.analytics ? "granted" : "denied",
      ad_storage: c.marketing ? "granted" : "denied",
      ad_user_data: c.marketing ? "granted" : "denied",
      ad_personalization: c.marketing ? "granted" : "denied",
    });
    void fetch("/api/consent", {
      method: "POST",
      headers: { "Content-Type": "application/json" },
      body: JSON.stringify({ choices: next, version: CONSENT_VERSION }),
      keepalive: true,
    }).catch(() => {/* logging is best-effort; local store is source of truth */});
  }, []);

  return <Ctx.Provider value={{ consent, ready, save }}>{children}</Ctx.Provider>;
}

export function CookieBanner() {
  const { ready, save } = useConsent();
  const [open, setOpen] = useState(false);
  const [showPanel, setShowPanel] = useState(false);
  const [draft, setDraft] = useState<Consent>(DEFAULT);
  const firstBtn = useRef<HTMLButtonElement>(null);

  useEffect(() => {
    if (!ready) return;
    const stored = readStored();
    // Honor Global Privacy Control: pre-deny marketing if the browser opted out.
    if ((navigator as any).globalPrivacyControl) {
      setDraft((d) => ({ ...d, marketing: false }));
    }
    if (!stored) setOpen(true);
  }, [ready]);

  // Move focus INTO the banner without stealing it abusively: focus the first action, once.
  useEffect(() => { if (open) firstBtn.current?.focus(); }, [open]);

  if (!open) return null;

  const decide = (c: Consent) => { save(c); setOpen(false); };

  return (
    <section role="dialog" aria-modal="false" aria-labelledby="cb-title" aria-describedby="cb-desc" id="cookie-banner">
      <h2 id="cb-title">Your privacy choices</h2>
      <p id="cb-desc">We use necessary cookies to run the site, and optional ones with your consent.</p>

      {showPanel && (
        <fieldset>
          <legend>Cookie categories</legend>
          {(["necessary", "functional", "analytics", "marketing"] as Category[]).map((cat) => (
            <label key={cat} className="cb-row">
              <input
                type="checkbox"
                checked={cat === "necessary" ? true : draft[cat]}
                disabled={cat === "necessary"}
                onChange={(e) => setDraft((d) => ({ ...d, [cat]: e.target.checked }))}
              />
              <span>{cat[0].toUpperCase() + cat.slice(1)}</span>
            </label>
          ))}
        </fieldset>
      )}

      <div className="cb-actions">
        <button ref={firstBtn} className="btn btn--primary" onClick={() => decide({ ...DEFAULT })}>
          Reject non-essential
        </button>
        <button className="btn btn--primary" onClick={() => decide({ necessary: true, functional: true, analytics: true, marketing: true })}>
          Accept all
        </button>
        <button className="btn btn--ghost" aria-expanded={showPanel} aria-controls="cb-panel" onClick={() => setShowPanel((s) => !s)}>
          Manage preferences
        </button>
        {showPanel && <button className="btn btn--secondary" onClick={() => decide(draft)}>Save preferences</button>}
      </div>
    </section>
  );
}
```

Gate a component behind consent:

```tsx
export function ConsentGate({ category, children }: { category: Category; children: React.ReactNode }) {
```

```js
const KEY = "ujg_consent", VERSION = 3, EXPIRY = 1000 * 60 * 60 * 24 * 180;
const DEFAULT = { necessary: true, functional: false, analytics: false, marketing: false };

function readConsent() {
  try {
    const r = JSON.parse(localStorage.getItem(KEY) || "null");
    if (!r || r.version !== VERSION || Date.now() - r.ts > EXPIRY) return null;
    return r.choices;
  } catch { return null; }
}

// Activate only the scripts whose category is granted. Runs after every save.
function applyConsent(consent) {
  document.querySelectorAll('script[type="text/plain"][data-cc]').forEach((tag) => {
    if (!consent[tag.dataset.cc]) return;             // category denied → leave inert
    const live = document.createElement("script");
    for (const { name, value } of tag.attributes) {
      if (name === "type" || name === "data-cc") continue;
      live.setAttribute(name, value);
    }
    if (!tag.src) live.textContent = tag.textContent;  // inline snippet
    tag.replaceWith(live);                             // now it executes
  });
}

function saveConsent(consent) {
  const next = { ...consent, necessary: true };
  try { localStorage.setItem(KEY, JSON.stringify({ choices: next, version: VERSION, ts: Date.now() })); } catch {}
  applyConsent(next);
  navigator.sendBeacon?.("/api/consent", JSON.stringify({ choices: next, version: VERSION }));
}

function initBanner() {
  const stored = readConsent();
  if (stored) { applyConsent(stored); return; }        // returning user: no banner, apply choice
  const gpc = navigator.globalPrivacyControl === true; // honor GPC as opt-out of marketing
  const banner = document.getElementById("cookie-banner");
  banner.hidden = false;
  banner.querySelector('[data-cb="reject"]').focus();

  banner.addEventListener("click", (e) => {
    const action = e.target.closest("[data-cb]")?.dataset.cb;
    if (!action) return;
    if (action === "accept") saveConsent({ necessary: true, functional: true, analytics: true, marketing: true });
    else if (action === "reject") saveConsent({ ...DEFAULT });
    else if (action === "manage") { const p = document.getElementById("cb-panel"); p.hidden = !p.hidden; e.target.setAttribute("aria-expanded", String(!p.hidden)); return; }
    else if (action === "save") {
      const read = (n) => banner.querySelector(`input[name="${n}"]`).checked;
      saveConsent({ necessary: true, functional: read("functional"), analytics: read("analytics"), marketing: !gpc && read("marketing") });
    }
    banner.hidden = true;
  });
}

document.addEventListener("DOMContentLoaded", initBanner);
```

```python
import hashlib, json, time
from fastapi import FastAPI, Form, Request, Response, HTTPException
from pydantic import BaseModel

app = FastAPI()
CONSENT_VERSION = 3
EXPIRY_S = 60 * 60 * 24 * 180

class ConsentRecord(BaseModel):
    functional: bool
    analytics: bool
    marketing: bool
    version: int
    ts: int

def hash_ip(ip: str) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256(f"{ip}::ujg-salt".encode()).hexdigest()  # minimize: store a hash, never raw IP

@app.post("/consent")
async def record_consent(request: Request, response: Response,
                         decision: str = Form(...), csrf_token: str = Form(...)):
    if not verify_csrf(request, csrf_token):
        raise HTTPException(403, "Invalid CSRF token")
    if decision not in ("accept", "reject"):
        raise HTTPException(422, "Unknown decision")

    grant = decision == "accept"
    record = ConsentRecord(functional=grant, analytics=grant, marketing=grant,
                           version=CONSENT_VERSION, ts=int(time.time()))

    # Durable audit log: who consented to what, when, under which policy version.
    await db.consent_log.insert({
        **record.model_dump(),
        "ip_hash": hash_ip(request.client.host if request.client else ""),
        "ua": request.headers.get("user-agent", "")[:256],
    })

    # First-party cookie the server reads on later requests to decide `consent_valid`.
    response.set_cookie("ujg_consent", json.dumps(record.model_dump()),
                        max_age=EXPIRY_S, httponly=False, secure=True, samesite="lax")
    return {"ok": True}
```

## 10\. Accessibility
STANDALONE. The banner appears on load and can lock the whole site out — its accessibility is the difference between "usable" and "the site is a wall." The governing tension: **move focus into the banner without stealing it abusively, and never trap the entire page unless it is a true modal.**

**Roles & ARIA:**
*   Non-modal corner/bar: `role="dialog"`, `aria-modal="false"`, `aria-labelledby` (title) + `aria-describedby` (purpose text). The page stays operable behind it.
*   True modal wall: `role="alertdialog"` (or `dialog`) + `aria-modal="true"` **and** an actual focus trap + backdrop. Only when you genuinely block the page.
*   Category toggles: real `<input type="checkbox">`/switch with a `<label>`; Necessary is `checked disabled` + `aria-describedby="…always active…"`.
*   "Manage preferences" uses `aria-expanded` + `aria-controls` on the panel it discloses.

**Keyboard map (interactive):**

| Key | Action |
| ---| --- |
| `Tab` / `Shift+Tab` | Move between Reject, Accept, Manage, links, and toggles. In a modal wall, focus is trapped within the banner; in a non-modal banner, Tab may leave into the page (that's correct — don't force a trap). |
| `Enter` / `Space` | Activate the focused button; toggle the focused switch (`Space`). |
| `Arrow` keys | Move within a switch group if implemented as a `radiogroup`/roving-tabindex list. |
| `Escape` | In a modal wall, does NOT dismiss to "accept" — either no-op or treat as "reject non-essential" (dismissing must never be coerced consent). In a non-modal banner, `Escape` should not silently grant consent. |

**Focus management:**
*   On appear, move focus to the **first action** (Reject/Accept) once — enough for screen-reader and keyboard users to find it, without yanking focus repeatedly or on every re-render.
*   In a modal wall, trap focus and **return focus** to the triggering element (or a sensible landmark) on close. For the standing "Cookie settings" re-entry, return focus to that link after the preferences dialog closes.
*   Do **not** auto-focus deep inside the panel or steal focus away from a screen reader mid-announcement.

**Announcement:** because it renders after load, ensure it's announced — a `role="dialog"` with a name is announced on focus; for non-modal bars that don't take focus immediately, an `aria-live="polite"` companion or moving focus makes it discoverable. Never leave it silent.

**Contrast & non-text:**
*   Body/purpose text ≥ 4.5:1; large headings ≥ 3:1. On glass/translucent skins this is the #1 failure — pin text on an opaque plate.
*   Toggle tracks/thumbs and button borders are non-text UI: ≥ 3:1 against their surroundings. Don't signal on/off by color alone — position + a check/label too.

**Target size:** every button, link, and toggle ≥ 44×44px. Category rows crammed to save vertical space are the usual violation.

**Reduced motion:** under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, drop the slide/scale entrance and any animated backdrop; render the banner in place.

**Common failures specific to this component:**
*   Focus trap on a **non**\-modal banner — announces a trap that isn't real and blocks the page needlessly.
*   Modal wall with **no** trap — Tab escapes behind the scrim to an inert page; keyboard users get lost.
*   "Reject" as a low-contrast text link while "Accept" is a big button — an accessibility _and_ compliance failure at once.
*   Focus never enters the banner, so screen-reader users don't know it exists and can't act.
*   `Escape` mapped to "Accept" — coerced consent, and a WCAG-hostile surprise.

## 11\. Innovative / Emerging Ideas
*   **Global Privacy Control as a first-class input:** read `navigator.globalPrivacyControl` and treat it as a legally-binding opt-out (California already does), pre-denying marketing/sale and shrinking the banner to a confirmation rather than an interruption. The 2026 direction is "the browser already told you — honor it."
*   **Consent Mode v2 + server-side tagging:** instead of physically blocking `<script>`s, ship denied-by-default consent signals and let tags run in a cookieless, modeled mode until granted — recovering conversion insight without dropping identifiers pre-consent. The banner becomes a signal emitter.
*   **Storage Access API & partitioned cookies (CHIPS):** as third-party cookies die, embeds (YouTube, Maps) move to `requestStorageAccess()` and partitioned (`; Partitioned`) cookies — the banner increasingly gates _storage-access requests_, not just cookie writes.
*   **Federated Credential Management / Privacy Sandbox:** Topics, Protected Audience, and Attribution Reporting reshape what "marketing consent" even means; forward-looking banners map categories onto Sandbox APIs, not just classic pixels.
*   **`granted`****/****`denied`** **design-token theming of consent state** so an embed placeholder ("Accept marketing cookies to load this video") is generated from the same category model, not hand-authored per embed.
*   **On-device / edge consent records** signed and timestamped for tamper-evident audit trails, reducing the raw PII a consent log has to store.

## 12\. Conversion / UX Killers
None throws an error. Each quietly costs trust, compliance, or data.

*   **Setting cookies before consent.** The cardinal sin: analytics/ad tags fire on load and _then_ the banner asks. It's non-compliant, it taints your data, and any regulator's first test catches it. Fix: default-deny and gate scripts (§9.4), so nothing non-essential runs until the click.
*   **Asymmetric buttons (the ghost Reject).** A glowing "Accept All" next to a faint "Reject" link is a dark pattern regulators fine specifically. It also erodes trust on first contact. Fix: equal size, equal weight, both at the first layer — enforce it in CSS/tests.
*   **Reject buried behind "Manage → scroll → uncheck all → Save."** Making rejection multi-step while accept is one click is the most-litigated banner pattern. Fix: one-click "Reject all" beside "Accept all."
*   **The full-page consent wall used to coerce.** Blocking all content until Accept (with no real Reject) reads as hostage-taking and spikes bounce. Fix: non-modal corner/bar with a genuine reject, or a modal that still offers equal reject.
*   **No re-entry / no withdrawal.** Once dismissed, the user can never change their mind — which is itself non-compliant (withdrawal must be as easy as consent). Fix: a persistent "Cookie settings" link/floating button.
*   **Re-prompting on every page or every visit.** Not persisting the choice (or persisting it wrong) nags users into rage-accepting. Fix: honor the stored, versioned, non-expired record; only re-ask on version bump or expiry.
*   **Layout shift on load.** The banner shoves the page down as it appears, users mis-tap, and Core Web Vitals (CLS) tank. Fix: fixed footprint + `transform`/`opacity`\-only entrance.
*   **Consent theater.** A banner that stores a choice but whose scripts ignore it entirely — the worst outcome, because it _looks_ compliant while providing zero protection and full liability. Fix: prove enforcement with the Playwright "no trackers before consent" test.

## 13\. Advanced Patterns
*   **TypeScript-enforced consent shape (necessary is unforgeable).** Model consent so `necessary` can only ever be `true` and every save is total — the type system prevents "forgot a category" and "accidentally togglable necessary" bugs:

```typescript
type Category = "functional" | "analytics" | "marketing";
// necessary is a literal true; the optional categories are exhaustive and required.
type Consent = { readonly necessary: true } & { [K in Category]: boolean };

// A save can't compile if it omits a category or tries to disable necessary:
function save(c: Consent): void { /* ... */ }
// save({ necessary: true, functional: false, analytics: false, marketing: false }); // ok
// save({ necessary: false, ... });  // ❌ Type 'false' is not assignable to 'true'
// save({ necessary: true, functional: false, analytics: false }); // ❌ missing 'marketing'
```

*   **Category → token gate for embeds.** Drive both the "load this iframe?" decision and its placeholder from one token/category map, so every third-party embed is gated consistently and a new category is one config entry, not N hand-wired checks:

```typescript
const EMBED_CATEGORY: Record<string, Category> = {
  "youtube.com": "marketing", "player.vimeo.com": "functional", "maps.google.com": "functional",
};
export function canLoadEmbed(src: string, consent: Consent): boolean {
  const host = new URL(src).hostname.replace(/^www\./, "");
  const cat = Object.entries(EMBED_CATEGORY).find(([h]) => host.endsWith(h))?.[1];
  return !cat || consent[cat]; // unknown host → treat as functional-safe or block per policy
}
```

*   **Design-token consent surface.** Express the banner entirely in three-tier tokens (primitive → semantic → component) so a client rebrand or dark mode is a token swap, and the locked "necessary" switch, focus ring, and equal-weight buttons stay consistent across every skin:

```css
:root {
  --eminence-800: oklch(38% 0.13 305); --gold-500: oklch(78% 0.135 82);   /* primitive */
  --color-action: var(--eminence-800); --color-focus: var(--gold-500);     /* semantic */
  --cb-btn-bg: var(--color-action); --cb-ring: var(--color-focus);         /* component */
}
.cb-actions .btn { background: var(--cb-btn-bg); }
#cookie-banner :focus-visible { outline: 3px solid var(--cb-ring); }
```

*   **Cross-tab consent sync.** Broadcast a consent change so a decision in one tab immediately gates/ungates scripts in every open tab, via the `storage` event or `BroadcastChannel("consent")` — otherwise a user who rejects in one tab is still tracked in another.

## 14\. Performance & Bundle Cost
The CMP loads before nearly everything and decides what else loads — the worst place to be heavy or slow.

*   **The CMP is on the critical path.** A render-blocking, multi-hundred-KB consent script delays first paint for _every_ page and visitor. Load the banner logic itself as first-party, inline-critical, and defer the heavy vendor-list/TCF machinery until "Manage" is opened. Never let the consent tool be your largest render-blocking resource.
*   **Animate only compositor properties.** The entrance uses `transform` + `opacity`, never `height`/`top`/`margin`, so appearing on load doesn't trigger reflow on the whole page or spike CLS. Reserve the footprint so content doesn't jump.
*   **Tree-shake the category logic; lazy-load the second layer.** Import only the store + gate on initial load (a few KB). Code-split the granular preferences panel, vendor list, and any TCF/IAB string encoder so they download only when the user clicks "Manage preferences" — most users never do.
*   **Virtualize / defer long vendor lists.** TCF banners can list _hundreds_ of vendors. Render that list virtualized (windowed) and only when the panel opens; never mount 400 toggle rows on page load.
*   **Gate = performance win, not just compliance.** Because non-essential tags don't load until consent (and never for users who reject), rejecting users get a dramatically lighter page. Correct enforcement is also your biggest third-party-script performance lever.
*   **One delegated listener, not one per control.** Bind a single click handler on the banner and read `data-cb` (§9.4) rather than wiring each button/toggle — cheaper setup, works if the panel is injected later.

## 15\. Security
STANDALONE. A consent banner's security story is unusual: its biggest risks aren't classic exploits but **enforcement integrity** and **injection through the tag it controls.**

**Attack surface & enforcement integrity (the core issue).** The banner's entire value is that non-essential scripts are _actually gated_. If the store is client-side (it must be, to gate client scripts), a technical user can flip `localStorage` to "accept everything" — that's acceptable for _their own_ tracking, but it means the client store is **not** a trust boundary. The real security failures are: (1) **banners that set cookies/fire tags anyway** regardless of the stored choice — "consent theater" that is both a privacy violation and an integrity failure, and (2) **visually gated but not functionally gated** scripts, where the tag is hidden or `display:none`'d but still executes and phones home. Enforce by making the tag genuinely inert until consent (`type="text/plain"` swap, real Consent Mode denial, or a GTM consent trigger) and **verify it with an automated "no requests before consent" test** (§9.17), not by eyeballing the UI.

**XSS via injected tags.** A consent banner is, by design, a machine that **injects and executes third-party** **`<script>`****s** on approval — that is a deliberate code-execution path, so treat the allowed-tag list as trusted input only. Never build the gated script's `src` or inline body from URL params, `postMessage`, or any user-controllable value; a banner that reads a category or vendor id from the query string and reflects it into a `<script>` is a self-inflicted XSS. Keep the category→tag mapping server-controlled/static, and apply a **Content-Security-Policy** (`script-src` allowlist) so even a compromised CMP can't load an arbitrary origin.

**Server-side validation (the consent log).** When consent is logged to an endpoint (§9.6/§9.13), validate the payload (`necessary` must be `true`, categories are booleans, version is an int), attach a **CSRF token** on the form-post variant, rate-limit the endpoint, and **minimize what you store** — hash the IP, truncate the UA, never log more PII than the proof-of-consent requires. The log is evidence; keep it tamper-evident (append-only, timestamped, versioned) so it holds up under audit.

**Authorization / gating content.** The banner gates _tracking_, not protected content — it is **not** an access-control mechanism. Never use "accepted cookies" as a gate for authenticated or paid content; that's what auth/session and server-side authorization are for. Treat the consent decision as a privacy signal, never as a permission grant.

**Clickjacking & tampering.** For a modal consent wall, ship `Content-Security-Policy: frame-ancestors 'none'` (and legacy `X-Frame-Options: DENY`) so your banner can't be framed under a decoy that harvests "Accept" clicks. And because the client store isn't a trust boundary, any _server_ decision that depends on consent (e.g. "may we email this user") must be re-checked server-side against the durable log, never inferred from a cookie the client can forge.

## 17\. Visual Styles
The same compliant banner — equal buttons, four toggles, a real gate — wears any skin. Here it is across the 11 core visual languages, described for _this_ component specifically.

1. **Flat:** solid Night surface, crisp 1px border, no shadow; Accept and Reject as flat equal rectangles, toggles as simple two-state pills. Reads instantly, cheapest to render on the critical path.
2. **Material:** the banner as an elevated Surface (dp-shadow) sliding up from the bottom; toggles are Material Switches with a ripple, buttons are filled/tonal with an ink ripple on press. Consent Mode is Google's native contract underneath.
3. **Glassmorphism:** translucent frosted corner card with `backdrop-filter: blur()` over the page; gorgeous but the consent text must sit on an opaque plate or it fails contrast — the one skin where readability actively fights the look.
4. **Liquid Glass (2026, Apple iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe):** a refractive pill-shaped sheet that bends the content behind it with a specular rim and moving sheen; toggles glisten as glass capsules. Pin all consent copy on solid backing and honor reduced-motion, or the sheen becomes a legibility and motion hazard.
5. **Neumorphism:** the card and toggles extrude softly from a same-color surface with dual light/dark shadows; the "necessary" switch reads as a pressed-in inset. Pretty but contrast-poor — risky for a component that legally must be readable; accent use only.
6. **Skeuomorphism:** a "real" cookie-jar-lid card with beveled edges and gradient buttons; toggles look like physical rocker switches, Accept a glossy raised key. Tactile and friendly, heavy on decoration.
7. **Neo-Brutalism:** thick black border, hard offset drop-shadow, zero radius, clashing Spanish-Orange Accept vs. Platinum Reject — but rendered at the _same_ size so symmetry survives the loud styling. High personality, still compliant.
8. **Claymorphism:** big-radius puffy card with inner top-light and soft glow; toggles are squishy clay capsules, buttons rounded pillows. Warm and approachable — good for consumer brands that want the banner to feel un-scary.
