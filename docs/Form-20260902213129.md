## 1\. What a Form Actually Is
A form is the **`<form>`** **element itself** — the container that groups a set of controls, owns their collective state, and turns a user's intent into a single, validated, submittable transaction. That's the distinction that trips people up: a form is _not_ its inputs. The Input, Select, Checkbox, and Button docs cover the individual controls. **This doc is about the orchestrator** — the thing that composes those controls, coordinates their validation, decides when and how to submit, and manages what happens after.

Three things people conflate, and the boundary that matters:
*   **The form (orchestrator):** the `<form>`. Owns `method`, `action`, `enctype`, submission, the validation _strategy_, the error summary, dirty-state, and the request that leaves the browser. This is the subject of this doc.
*   **The fields (controls):** individual `<input>`, `<select>`, `<textarea>`, `<button>`. Each owns its own label, its own value, its own single-field validity. Covered in the sibling docs.
*   **The field group (the layer between):** a labeled control + its help text + its error message + its wiring (`<label>`, `aria-describedby`, `aria-invalid`). The form arranges these; it doesn't _be_ them.

The single most common architecture mistake: building a "form" as a pile of controlled inputs wired to one big `useState` object with a hand-rolled `onSubmit`, and never rendering a real `<form>` element at all. That throws away implicit submission, native validation, progressive enhancement, and half of accessibility. **The form is a real HTML element with real semantics — start there, then enhance.** The `<form>` is the skeleton; React state is a skin over it.
* * *

## 2\. Why It's So Important
The form is where the user _commits_. Everything before it is browsing; the form is the transaction. That makes it the highest-stakes surface in most apps.
*   **Conversion lives and dies here.** Every field you add measurably drops completion. Checkout, signup, lead capture, application — these are the exact screens the business is trying to move people through, and a form's friction is a direct tax on revenue. A form that loses the user's typed data on an error is a refund waiting to happen.
*   **It's the accessibility deep end.** A button is one control; a form is a dozen controls, their labels, their errors, their focus order, and the announcement of what went wrong. Screen-reader users, keyboard-only users, and voice-control users experience your app _as a sequence of forms_. Get the form's labeling and error handling right and you've cleared the hardest a11y bar in the product.
*   **It's the primary attack surface.** Every state-changing form is a doorway into your backend. CSRF, mass-assignment, injection, SSRF via URL fields, abuse of file uploads — they all enter through a form submit. The UI is never the security boundary; the server is (see §9.9 and §15).
*   **It sets the trust tone.** A form that validates as you leave a field, preserves your input on error, tells you _exactly_ what's wrong and _where_, and shows a real pending state on submit feels trustworthy. One that clears itself, blocks the button with no explanation, or reloads into a generic "something went wrong" feels broken even when it works.
* * *

## 3\. Anatomy of a Form
A well-built form is a predictable stack of parts. The form owns all of them; it delegates the innermost to the field controls.
*   **The** **`<form>`** **element:** the container. Carries `action`, `method`, `enctype`, `novalidate`, and the submit event. The orchestrator.
*   **Form heading + intro:** an `<h1>`/`<h2>` naming the task and, ideally, an honest sense of length ("Takes about 2 minutes").
*   **Error summary region:** a top-of-form, focus-managed, `aria-live` block that lists every error with in-page links to the offending fields. The single highest-leverage accessibility feature a form has (see §9 and §10).
*   **Field groups:** each a `<label>` + control + optional help text + error slot, wired with `aria-describedby` and `aria-invalid`. Related groups nest in a `<fieldset>` with a `<legend>`.
*   **Layout container:** the grid or stack that arranges field groups — column count, gap, and density (see §4).
*   **Progress indicator (multi-step only):** "Step 2 of 4," ideally with named steps.
*   **Action row:** the real submit `<button type="submit">`, plus any secondary actions (Back, Save draft, Cancel). One primary per step.
*   **Submission feedback:** pending state on the submit button, plus a post-submit success or error announcement.
*   **Hidden machinery:** CSRF token, honeypot, `enctype`, and any `formaction`/`formmethod` overrides that route a given submit differently.

## 4\. Layout, Scale & Density
Form layout is not decoration — it's completion rate. The research here is unusually settled.

| Decision | Recommendation | Why |
| ---| ---| --- |
| Column count | Single column for almost everything | Multi-column forms cause skipped fields and ambiguous reading order. Exceptions: short logically-paired fields (City / State / ZIP, First / Last). |
| Label placement | Top-aligned | Fastest to complete, wraps cleanly on mobile, gives the label full width for long/translated text. Left-aligned labels save vertical space but slow scanning. |
| Label-to-field gap | 4–8px | Tight enough to bind label to field; the group-to-group gap must be clearly larger. |
| Group-to-group gap | 16–24px | Must exceed the intra-group gap or the eye can't parse where one field ends. |
| Field height | 44–48px | Meets the 44px touch target and reads as tappable. |
| Max line/field width | ~`60ch` / 480–640px | Full-bleed inputs on desktop look broken and hurt scanning. Constrain the column. |
| Required/optional marking | Mark the rarer one | If most fields are required, mark the optional ones ("(optional)"). Never rely on the `*` alone — spell it out for screen readers. |
| Primary action position | Bottom-left or bottom-full-width, aligned to the field column | The eye ends at the last field; the button belongs directly under it, not floated right away from the column. |

Density scale (match your product's baseline):

| Density | Field height | Vertical rhythm | Use |
| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| Comfortable | 48px | 24px | Consumer signup, checkout, marketing lead capture |
| Default | 44px | 20px | Most app forms, settings |
| Compact | 36px | 12px | Data-entry-heavy admin, internal tools, tables of inputs |

Two rules that carry more weight than they look: **one idea per row** (don't cram unrelated fields side by side), and **group with** **`<fieldset>`****/****`<legend>`** so "Billing address" is announced as a heading over its cluster, not five orphan fields.

## 5\. States (the form-level ones juniors forget)
Individual fields have their own states (that's the Input doc). The _form_ has its own states on top, and missing them is the tell of a shallow build.
1. **Pristine / untouched** — nothing edited yet. Don't show errors here. Premature validation on an untouched form is hostile.
2. **Dirty** — the user has changed at least one field from its initial value. Drives the unsaved-changes guard (§9) and the "Save" enablement decision.
3. **Validating** — a field or the whole form is running async validation (e.g. "is this username taken?"). Show it; don't freeze silently.
4. **Invalid (with errors)** — one or more fields failed. The form surfaces an error summary, marks fields `aria-invalid`, and moves focus to the summary or first error.
5. **Valid / submittable** — passes client validation. Note: valid ≠ enabled-only. Prefer an _always-clickable_ submit that surfaces errors over a disabled one (see §12).
6. **Submitting / pending** — the request is in flight. Disable the submit (to block double-submit), show a spinner/label change, keep field values, ideally disable inputs to prevent mid-flight edits.
7. **Submit success** — the transaction landed. Announce it, redirect, or replace the form with a confirmation. Don't leave a stale form sitting there.
8. **Submit error (server-side)** — the request failed. Two flavors: **field errors** the server found (re-render with them mapped back to fields) and **form-level errors** (network, 500, rate-limited) shown in a top banner, with the user's input preserved.
9. **Partially saved / autosaved** — for long forms: "Saved 12:04pm," draft persisted, resumable.

The through-line: a form is a small state machine — `pristine → dirty → validating → (invalid | valid) → submitting → (success | error)`. Model it explicitly. The amateur version tracks only "did I click submit."
* * *

## 6\. Types & Variants
Forms vary by _shape of the transaction_, not by color.
*   **Single-step form:** the default. Everything on one screen, one submit. Most forms should be this — don't split a 5-field form into a wizard.
*   **Multi-step / wizard:** one logical task broken into sequential screens with a progress indicator. Right for genuinely long flows (onboarding, applications, checkout) where chunking reduces abandonment. Each step should validate before advancing; state persists across steps (§9).
*   **Inline / edit-in-place:** a field or small group becomes editable in context, saves on blur or on a local Save. Common in settings and tables.
*   **Search / filter form:** often `method="get"` so the query lives in the URL (shareable, bookmarkable, back-button-friendly). Frequently submits on change with debounce rather than a button.
*   **Login / auth form:** small but high-stakes — autocomplete tokens, password-manager compatibility, rate-limited endpoint, no username-vs-password enumeration leaks.
*   **Multipart / file-upload form:** carries binary data, needs `enctype="multipart/form-data"`, progress UI, and server-side type/size validation (§9).
*   **Autosaving form:** no explicit submit for the main body; changes persist continuously (docs, settings). Still needs a dirty-state model and conflict handling.
*   **Multi-intent form:** one form, multiple submit buttons with different `name`/`value` or `formaction` (Save draft vs Publish). The no-JS branching pattern (§9).
* * *

## 7\. When to Use One (and When Not To)
Use a `<form>` whenever the user submits a **set of related inputs as one transaction** — even a single search box belongs in a form, because it earns you implicit submit (Enter), native semantics, and progressive enhancement for free.

Don't reach for a full form when:
*   **A single setting toggles instantly.** A standalone switch that autosaves on change isn't a form-with-submit; it's a control with an `onChange` (still wrap in a `<form>` if you want Enter/reset semantics, but no submit button needed).
*   **The action is one button, no inputs.** That's just a button (see the Button doc) — though a state-changing button should _still_ live in a `<form>` for CSRF and progressive enhancement.
*   **You're tempted to split a short form into steps.** Wizards help long flows and hurt short ones. Below ~6–8 fields, keep it single-step.
*   **You'd block the submit until perfect.** If you find yourself disabling submit and hiding why, the pattern is wrong (§12) — let them submit and surface errors.

Placement heuristics: keep the primary submit **under the field column** and reachable without hunting; on long forms, a sticky action bar keeps "Continue" in view; put destructive form actions (Delete account) far from the primary submit and gate them behind confirmation.

## 8\. Forms Across Design Systems
Same `<form>` underneath, very different opinions about fields, spacing, and validation ergonomics.
*   **Material Design (Google):** filled/outlined text fields with floating labels, helper + error text baked into the field's own layout, prominent required indicators. Strong opinions about spacing and the error-text slot.
*   **Apple HIG (iOS/macOS):** grouped inset lists for forms (`Form` in SwiftUI), labels leading, minimal chrome, validation usually deferred to submit or inline on commit. Restraint over decoration.
*   **Fluent (Microsoft):** dense, label-on-top, strong support for complex enterprise data-entry, inline validation with subtle iconography. Built for long admin forms.
*   **Ant Design / enterprise:** the most form-centric of the majors — a full `Form` model with `Form.Item`, per-field rules, layouts (horizontal/vertical/inline), and a built-in validation engine. Great for dense CRUD.
*   **Tailwind / utility-first:** no prescribed form look; you compose spacing and states from utilities, usually behind a wrapper so density stays consistent. Freedom + drift risk.
*   **shadcn/Radix + React Hook Form:** the 2024+ web default. Radix primitives for accessible fields, a `Form` wrapper that binds React Hook Form's context, and Zod for schema validation. Accessibility and error wiring come baked in; you own the code.
*   [**GOV.UK**](http://GOV.UK) **Design System:** the accessibility gold standard worth stealing from — one-thing-per-page flows, an error-summary component with focus management and in-page links, and question-first labeling. If you copy one system's form patterns, copy this one.

## 9\. The Code
The biggest section. The form is the orchestrator, so most of this is about _composition, submission, validation strategy, and the server_ — not styling a box.

### 9.1 HTML (native form semantics — master this first)
Everything else is an enhancement over this. If your form doesn't work here, it doesn't work.

```plain
<form action="/signup" method="post" enctype="application/x-www-form-urlencoded">
  <h1>Create your account</h1>

  <!-- Error summary: empty until submit fails, then focus moves here -->
  <div id="error-summary" role="alert" tabindex="-1" hidden>
    <h2>There is a problem</h2>
    <ul><!-- links to each invalid field injected here --></ul>
  </div>

  <div class="field">
    <label for="email">Email address</label>
    <input id="email" name="email" type="email" autocomplete="email"
           required aria-describedby="email-hint" />
    <p id="email-hint" class="hint">We'll send a confirmation link.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="field">
    <label for="password">Password</label>
    <input id="password" name="password" type="password"
           autocomplete="new-password" required minlength="12"
           aria-describedby="password-hint" />
    <p id="password-hint" class="hint">At least 12 characters.</p>
  </div>

  <!-- CSRF token: verified server-side (see 9.9) -->
  <input type="hidden" name="csrf_token" value="{{ csrf }}" />

  <!-- A REAL submit button. Non-negotiable. -->
  <button type="submit">Create account</button>
</form>
```

The rules that separate a real form from a `<div>` pile:
*   **`method`** **and** **`action`** **are the contract.** `method="post"` for anything that changes state; `method="get"` only for searches/filters (the values go in the URL — never put a password in a GET). `action` is where it posts. Set both even if JS will intercept — that's your progressive-enhancement floor.
*   **`enctype`** **matters the moment you upload a file.** Default is `application/x-www-form-urlencoded`. For `<input type="file">` you _must_ use `multipart/form-data` or the file never arrives. `text/plain` exists and is essentially never correct.
*   **Keep a real** **`<button type="submit">`****\*\*\*\*.** This is the one juniors delete. A real submit button gives you: **implicit submission** (Enter in a text field submits the form via the first submit button), native form validation firing, correct semantics for assistive tech, and the form working before JS loads. A `<div onClick>` that calls `fetch` throws all of that away.
*   **Every control has a** **`name`****\*\*\*\*.** No `name`, no data in the submission — the field is decorative. The `name` is the key the server reads.
*   **Wire the accessibility inline:** `<label for>` bound to `id`, hints via `aria-describedby`, `required` on required fields. This is the field group's job but the form must enforce it.
*   **`novalidate`** **on the** **`<form>`** turns off the browser's built-in validation bubbles — use it when you're providing your own richer validation UI, but only if you actually replace it.

### 9.2 Controlled vs Uncontrolled (the decision that shapes everything)
Before any framework, decide who owns the field values.
*   **Uncontrolled:** the DOM owns the value; you read it at submit time (via `FormData`, refs, or `defaultValue`). Less code, fewer re-renders, closest to native. React Hook Form is built on this.
*   **Controlled:** React state owns every keystroke (`value` + `onChange`). Needed when the UI must react to input _as you type_ (live formatting, dependent fields, char counters, instant cross-field validation). Costs a re-render per keystroke.

The senior default: **uncontrolled-first, controlled only where you need reactivity.** A 20-field form fully controlled re-renders the whole tree on every keystroke; the same form uncontrolled (or with RHF's subscription model) barely re-renders at all. Reach for controlled state per-field, not form-wide.

`FormData` is the native bridge — it reads an uncontrolled form in one line and is exactly what Server Actions receive:

```javascript
form.addEventListener("submit", (e) => {
  e.preventDefault();
  const data = Object.fromEntries(new FormData(e.currentTarget));
  // data = { email: "...", password: "...", csrf_token: "..." }
});
```

### 9.3 Validation Strategy (the real orchestration problem)
This is the heart of form engineering. Two mechanisms, and you almost always want _both_.

**Native Constraint Validation API** — free, built into the browser, zero dependencies. `required`, `minlength`, `maxlength`, `pattern`, `type="email"`, `min`/`max`/`step` all validate without JS, and the API exposes it:

```javascript
const email = form.email;
email.validity.valueMissing;   // true if required and empty
email.validity.typeMismatch;   // true if type="email" and malformed
email.checkValidity();         // runs constraints, returns bool
email.setCustomValidity("That email is already taken"); // your own message
form.checkValidity();          // whole-form validity
```

Use it as the **floor**: it works before JS, it's accessible, and it's the progressive-enhancement baseline. Its ceiling is low — messages are hard to style/position, cross-field rules ("passwords must match") don't fit, and async rules ("is this taken?") aren't supported.

**Schema libraries (Zod / Yup / Valibot)** — the ceiling. Define the shape once, get validation _and_ types, run it on client and server from the same source:

```typescript
import { z } from "zod";

export const SignupSchema = z.object({
  email: z.string().email("Enter a valid email"),
  password: z.string().min(12, "At least 12 characters"),
  confirm: z.string(),
  age: z.coerce.number().int().min(13, "You must be 13 or older"),
}).refine((d) => d.password === d.confirm, {
  message: "Passwords don't match",
  path: ["confirm"],            // cross-field rule, attached to a field
});

export type SignupInput = z.infer<typeof SignupSchema>;  // types for free
```

The non-negotiable rule: **client-side validation is UX; server-side validation is truth. Do both, and share the schema.** Client validation gives instant feedback and saves a round-trip; it is _not_ security. An attacker skips your React entirely and posts to the endpoint with `curl`. The server must re-run the same schema on every request (§9.9). One Zod schema, imported in both places, is how you keep them in lockstep.

**Timing** — when to validate matters as much as how:
*   **On submit:** validate everything, show the summary. Always do this.
*   **On blur (after first submit attempt):** the humane default — don't yell while they're mid-typing, but once they've left a field _and_ tried to submit, validate on blur so they get fast feedback on fixes.
*   **On change:** reserve for live constraints (password strength meter, char counter). Validating every keystroke from the first character is hostile.

The GOV.UK-tested pattern: **validate on submit first; after the first failed submit, re-validate individual fields on blur/change so the user sees errors clear as they fix them.**

### 9.4 React Hook Form + Zod (the modern web default, longest block)
The 2024+ standard: uncontrolled performance, schema validation, and accessible error wiring in one composition. This is the form architecture most new React apps should reach for.

```tsx
"use client";
import { useForm } from "react-hook-form";
import { zodResolver } from "@hookform/resolvers/zod";
import { useRef } from "react";
import { SignupSchema, type SignupInput } from "@/lib/schemas";

export function SignupForm() {
  const summaryRef = useRef<HTMLDivElement>(null);
  const {
    register,
    handleSubmit,
    setError,
    formState: { errors, isSubmitting, isDirty },
  } = useForm<SignupInput>({
    resolver: zodResolver(SignupSchema),
    mode: "onSubmit",         // validate on submit...
    reValidateMode: "onBlur", // ...then on blur once errored (see 9.3)
  });

  async function onSubmit(data: SignupInput) {
    const res = await fetch("/api/signup", {
      method: "POST",
      headers: { "Content-Type": "application/json" },
      body: JSON.stringify(data),
    });
    if (!res.ok) {
      const body = await res.json();
      // Map server field errors back onto the form (never trust client alone)
      for (const [field, message] of Object.entries(body.errors ?? {})) {
        setError(field as keyof SignupInput, { message: String(message) });
      }
      // Move focus to the summary so SR users hear the problem
      summaryRef.current?.focus();
      return;
    }
    window.location.assign("/welcome");
  }

  // On invalid client submit, focus the error summary
  function onInvalid() {
    summaryRef.current?.focus();
  }

  const errorList = Object.entries(errors);

  return (
    <form onSubmit={handleSubmit(onSubmit, onInvalid)} noValidate>
      <h1>Create your account</h1>

      {errorList.length > 0 && (
        <div ref={summaryRef} tabIndex={-1} role="alert" className="error-summary">
          <h2>There is a problem</h2>
          <ul>
            {errorList.map(([field, err]) => (
              <li key={field}>
                <a href={`#${field}`}>{err?.message as string}</a>
              </li>
            ))}
          </ul>
        </div>
      )}

      <div className="field">
        <label htmlFor="email">Email address</label>
        <input
          id="email" type="email" autoComplete="email"
          aria-invalid={!!errors.email}
          aria-describedby={errors.email ? "email-error" : undefined}
          {...register("email")}
        />
        {errors.email && (
          <p id="email-error" className="field-error">{errors.email.message}</p>
        )}
      </div>

      <div className="field">
        <label htmlFor="password">Password</label>
        <input
          id="password" type="password" autoComplete="new-password"
          aria-invalid={!!errors.password}
          aria-describedby={errors.password ? "password-error" : undefined}
          {...register("password")}
        />
        {errors.password && (
          <p id="password-error" className="field-error">{errors.password.message}</p>
        )}
      </div>

      <button type="submit" disabled={isSubmitting}>
        {isSubmitting ? "Creating account…" : "Create account"}
      </button>
    </form>
  );
}
```

Why this is the senior version:
*   **`register`** **is uncontrolled** — RHF subscribes to inputs instead of re-rendering the tree per keystroke. A 30-field form stays fast.
*   **`zodResolver`** runs the _same schema_ the server uses. One source of truth for shape, messages, and types.
*   **Errors are wired for assistive tech:** `aria-invalid` on the field, `aria-describedby` pointing at the message, and a focus-managed `role="alert"` summary with in-page links. This is the pattern §10 demands.
*   **`isSubmitting`** drives the pending state and blocks double-submit; **`isDirty`** feeds the unsaved-changes guard.
*   **Server errors map back via** **`setError`** — because client validation passing means nothing about server truth (unique email, business rules).

### 9.5 Next.js Server Actions + `useActionState` (progressive enhancement done right)
Next's App Router lets a form post to a server function with **no API route and no client JS required** — and enhance up when JS is present. This is the strongest progressive-enhancement story on the web today.

```tsx
// app/actions.ts
"use server";
import { redirect } from "next/navigation";
import { SignupSchema } from "@/lib/schemas";

export type FormState = { errors?: Record<string, string>; message?: string | null };

export async function signup(_prev: FormState, formData: FormData): Promise<FormState> {
  // Server-side validation — the real one. Never trust the client.
  const parsed = SignupSchema.safeParse(Object.fromEntries(formData));
  if (!parsed.success) {
    const errors: Record<string, string> = {};
    for (const issue of parsed.error.issues) errors[issue.path[0] as string] = issue.message;
    return { errors };
  }
  // ...authz check, dedupe, create user...
  redirect("/welcome");
}
```

```tsx
// app/signup/SignupForm.tsx
"use client";
import { useActionState } from "react";
import { useFormStatus } from "react-dom";
import { signup, type FormState } from "@/app/actions";

function SubmitButton() {
  const { pending } = useFormStatus(); // reads the enclosing form's pending state
  return (
    <button type="submit" disabled={pending}>
      {pending ? "Creating account…" : "Create account"}
    </button>
  );
}

export function SignupForm() {
  const [state, formAction] = useActionState<FormState, FormData>(signup, { errors: {} });
  const errors = Object.entries(state.errors ?? {});

  return (
    <form action={formAction} noValidate>
      {errors.length > 0 && (
        <div role="alert" tabIndex={-1} className="error-summary">
          <h2>There is a problem</h2>
          <ul>{errors.map(([f, m]) => <li key={f}><a href={`#${f}`}>{m}</a></li>)}</ul>
        </div>
      )}
      <div className="field">
        <label htmlFor="email">Email address</label>
        <input id="email" name="email" type="email" autoComplete="email"
               aria-invalid={!!state.errors?.email}
               aria-describedby={state.errors?.email ? "email-error" : undefined} />
        {state.errors?.email && <p id="email-error" className="field-error">{state.errors.email}</p>}
      </div>
      <SubmitButton />
    </form>
  );
}
```

The point people miss: because the form uses `<form action={formAction}>` with real `name` attributes, **it submits and validates on the server even with JavaScript disabled or still loading.** `useActionState` and `useFormStatus` are the _enhancement_ — pending UI and inline errors without a full reload — layered on a form that already works. That's progressive enhancement as an architecture, not an afterthought.

### 9.6 CSS (form-level layout + state, done right)
The form owns layout and rhythm; fields own their own box. Drive it with tokens.

```css
.form {
  max-width: 640px;
  display: grid;
  gap: 20px;                 /* group-to-group rhythm */
}
.field {
  display: grid;
  gap: 6px;                  /* label-to-control, must be < form gap */
}
.field label { font-weight: 600; }
.field input, .field select, .field textarea {
  min-height: 44px;          /* touch target */
  padding: 0 12px;
  border: 1px solid var(--field-border, #6b6473);
  border-radius: 8px;
}
.field input:focus-visible {
  outline: 2px solid var(--focus, #DCA424);
```

### 9.7 Vanilla JS (submission + native validation, no framework)
The building block frameworks abstract. Progressive enhancement in raw form.

```javascript
const form = document.querySelector("#signup");
const summary = document.querySelector("#error-summary");

form.addEventListener("submit", async (e) => {
  e.preventDefault();

  // 1. Native constraint validation first
  if (!form.checkValidity()) {
    buildSummary();               // read validity, list errors, link them
    summary.hidden = false;
    summary.focus();              // move focus to the summary (a11y)
    return;
  }

  // 2. Submit — disable to block double-submit, keep values
  const btn = form.querySelector("button[type=submit]");
  btn.disabled = true;
  btn.textContent = "Creating account…";
  try {
    const res = await fetch(form.action, {
      method: form.method,
      body: new FormData(form),   // multipart-ready; no manual serialization
    });
    if (!res.ok) throw await res.json();
    window.location.assign("/welcome");
  } catch (serverErrors) {
    mapServerErrors(serverErrors); // paint field errors, focus summary
    summary.focus();
  } finally {
    btn.disabled = false;
    btn.textContent = "Create account";
  }
});

function buildSummary() {
  const list = summary.querySelector("ul");
  list.innerHTML = "";
  for (const el of form.elements) {
    if (el.willValidate && !el.checkValidity()) {
      const li = document.createElement("li");
      const a = document.createElement("a");
      a.href = `#${el.id}`;
      a.textContent = el.validationMessage;
      el.setAttribute("aria-invalid", "true");
      li.appendChild(a);
      list.appendChild(li);
    }
  }
}
```

### 9.8 Multi-Step / Wizard Forms
The orchestration problem in its hardest form: state must survive across steps, each step validates before advancing, and the whole thing must stay accessible and resumable.

```tsx
"use client";
import { useState } from "react";
import { z } from "zod";

const StepSchemas = [
  z.object({ name: z.string().min(1), email: z.string().email() }),
  z.object({ street: z.string().min(1), zip: z.string().regex(/^\d{5}$/) }),
  z.object({ plan: z.enum(["free", "pro"]) }),
] as const;

export function Wizard() {
  const [step, setStep] = useState(0);
  const [data, setData] = useState<Record<string, unknown>>({});
  const [errors, setErrors] = useState<Record<string, string>>({});
  const isLast = step === StepSchemas.length - 1;

  function next(formData: FormData) {
    const values = Object.fromEntries(formData);
    const parsed = StepSchemas[step].safeParse(values);
    if (!parsed.success) {
      const e: Record<string, string> = {};
      for (const i of parsed.error.issues) e[i.path[0] as string] = i.message;
      setErrors(e);
      return; // block advance until this step is valid
    }
    setErrors({});
    const merged = { ...data, ...parsed.data };
    setData(merged);
    if (isLast) submitAll(merged);
    else setStep((s) => s + 1);
  }

  return (
    <form action={next} noValidate aria-label={`Step ${step + 1} of ${StepSchemas.length}`}>
      <p role="status">Step {step + 1} of {StepSchemas.length}</p>
      {/* render fields for `step`, seeded with values from `data` */}
      <div className="actions">
        {step > 0 && <button type="button" onClick={() => setStep((s) => s - 1)}>Back</button>}
        <button type="submit">{isLast ? "Finish" : "Continue"}</button>
      </div>
    </form>
  );
}
```

Wizard rules that matter:
*   **Validate each step before advancing** — but never lose entered data when going Back.
*   **Persist across steps** (state, `sessionStorage`, or server-side draft) so a refresh doesn't nuke progress.
*   **Announce step changes** (`role="status"`, move focus to the new step's heading) so SR users know they advanced.
*   **One idea per step** beats cramming — the whole reason to use a wizard is to reduce per-screen load.
*   **Let users go back and edit** without redoing everything; consider a review step before final submit.

### 9.9 The Backend (the endpoint the form actually hits)
The visible form is the tip. The submit fires a request that must be engineered defensively — this is where most form security lives.

**Node / Express:**

```typescript
import express from "express";
import rateLimit from "express-rate-limit";
import { SignupSchema } from "./schemas"; // the SAME schema the client used

const router = express.Router();
const limiter = rateLimit({ windowMs: 60_000, max: 10 }); // per IP

router.post("/api/signup", limiter, verifyCsrf, async (req, res) => {
  // 1. Re-validate on the server — client validation is UX, not truth
  const parsed = SignupSchema.safeParse(req.body);
  if (!parsed.success) {
    const errors = Object.fromEntries(
      parsed.error.issues.map((i) => [i.path[0], i.message])
    );
    return res.status(422).json({ errors });
  }

  // 2. Allow-list fields — NEVER spread req.body into the model (mass assignment)
  const { email, password } = parsed.data;

  // 3. Business rules the client can't be trusted to enforce
  if (await db.users.exists({ email })) {
    return res.status(422).json({ errors: { email: "That email is already taken" } });
  }

  // 4. Create with explicit, allow-listed fields only
  const user = await db.users.create({ email, passwordHash: await hash(password) });
  return res.status(201).json({ id: user.id });
});

export default router;
```

**Security subsection — the form is an attack surface (read this twice):**
*   **CSRF (Cross-Site Request Forgery).** A malicious page auto-submits a form to _your_ endpoint using the victim's session cookie. Defenses: a **per-session anti-CSRF token** rendered into the form and verified server-side (`verifyCsrf` above), plus `SameSite=Lax`/`Strict` cookies so the browser won't send auth on cross-site posts. Every state-changing form needs this. Server Actions in Next include origin checks, but a raw endpoint does not — add it.
*   **Never trust the client.** The disabled submit, the `required` attributes, the Zod schema in the browser — all trivially bypassed. Re-run validation and authorization on every request. "The UI didn't allow it" is not a control.
*   **Mass assignment.** The #1 form-to-backend bug: `db.users.create(req.body)` lets an attacker POST `{ email, password, role: "admin", isVerified: true }` and self-promote. **Allow-list the fields you accept** (destructure `parsed.data`), or use a schema that strips unknown keys. Never spread the raw body into a model.
*   **SSRF (Server-Side Request Forgery).** If a form field is a URL your server later fetches (avatar-by-URL, webhook, "import from link"), an attacker points it at `http://169.254.169.254/` (cloud metadata) or internal services. Validate the scheme, resolve and block private/loopback/link-local IP ranges, and never fetch user-supplied URLs without an allow-list.
*   **Rate limiting & abuse.** Login, signup, password-reset, and contact forms are brute-force and spam targets. Rate-limit per IP _and_ per account, add exponential backoff, and gate suspicious bursts behind a challenge (CAPTCHA / proof-of-work / a hidden honeypot field bots fill in).
*   **Injection.** Validated input still gets parameterized — SQL via prepared statements/ORM, never string-concatenated queries; escape on output to block stored XSS from a form field that's later rendered.
*   **File uploads (multipart).** Validate type by _content_ (magic bytes), not just extension or client-sent MIME; cap size at the server; store outside the web root with a generated name; scan if you can; and serve back with a restrictive `Content-Type` and `Content-Disposition: attachment` so an uploaded `.html` can't run as your origin.
*   **Idempotency.** A double-submitted form (impatient user, retry) must not create two records. Accept an idempotency key or dedupe on a natural key; this is the server-side backstop to the client disabling the button.

**Python / FastAPI** — same defenses, different syntax:

```python
from fastapi import FastAPI, Form, HTTPException, Request, Depends
from pydantic import BaseModel, EmailStr, constr

app = FastAPI()

class Signup(BaseModel):
    email: EmailStr
    password: constr(min_length=12)   # mirrors the client schema

@app.post("/api/signup", status_code=201)
async def signup(request: Request, body: Signup, _=Depends(verify_csrf), __=Depends(rate_limit)):
    # Pydantic already validated + coerced; unknown keys are ignored (no mass-assignment)
    if await users.exists(email=body.email):
        raise HTTPException(422, detail={"errors": {"email": "That email is already taken"}})
    user = await users.create(email=body.email, password_hash=hash(body.password))
    return {"id": user.id}
```

Python note: even in a Python or Rails or Laravel stack, the form still lives in HTML. The framework's job is to render it, verify CSRF, re-validate, allow-list fields, and defend the endpoint — the exact same list as Node.

### 9.10 Autosave, Dirty-State & the Unsaved-Changes Guard
Long forms lose work; users close tabs. Two mechanisms protect them.

**Dirty-state + unsaved-changes guard** — warn before navigating away from edits:

```tsx
"use client";
import { useEffect } from "react";

export function useUnsavedGuard(isDirty: boolean) {
  useEffect(() => {
    if (!isDirty) return;
    const handler = (e: BeforeUnloadEvent) => {
      e.preventDefault();
      e.returnValue = ""; // browsers show their own generic prompt
    };
    window.addEventListener("beforeunload", handler);
    return () => window.removeEventListener("beforeunload", handler);
  }, [isDirty]);
}
// In-app navigation (router) needs its own guard — beforeunload only covers
// full page unloads, not client-side route changes.
```

**Autosave** — persist changes as they happen, so there's nothing to lose:

```typescript
function autosave(getValues: () => object, save: (v: object) => Promise<void>) {
  let timer: ReturnType<typeof setTimeout>;
  return () => {
    clearTimeout(timer);
    timer = setTimeout(async () => {
      await save(getValues());        // debounce ~800ms so you don't spam the server
      status.textContent = `Saved ${new Date().toLocaleTimeString()}`; // announce
    }, 800);
  };
}
```

Guidance: **debounce autosave** (don't POST per keystroke); **announce the save** in an `aria-live` region so it's not silent; handle **conflicts** (someone else edited the same record — last-write-wins is a data-loss bug in collaborative tools); and keep a **manual save as a fallback** so users who don't trust autosave have a lever. Autosave and an unsaved-changes guard are two answers to the same fear — pick one deliberately per form; running both is contradictory.

## 10\. Accessibility (non-negotiable, and this is the deep end)
A form is the single hardest accessibility surface in most products. Get these right:
*   **Every control has a real, programmatic label.** `<label for="id">` bound to the control, or `aria-label`/`aria-labelledby`. Placeholder text is _not_ a label — it vanishes on focus and fails contrast. This is the #1 form a11y failure.
*   **Group related fields** with `<fieldset>` + `<legend>` (e.g. a set of radios, or "Shipping address"). The legend is announced as context for each control inside.
*   **Errors are wired, not just colored.** Each invalid field gets `aria-invalid="true"` and an `aria-describedby` pointing at its visible error text. Color alone fails colorblind users and WCAG.
*   **The error summary is the load-bearing pattern.** On failed submit: render a summary at the top of the form in a focus-managed container (`tabindex="-1"`, then `.focus()`), mark it `role="alert"` (or move focus into an `aria-live` region), list each error, and **link each summary item to its field** (`href="#field-id"`). This is the [GOV.UK](http://GOV.UK) pattern and it's the difference between an accessible form and a compliant-looking one.
*   **Move focus deliberately.** On error → focus the summary (or the first invalid field). On step change in a wizard → focus the new step's heading. On success → move focus to the confirmation. Never leave the user's focus stranded after a submit.
*   **Announce async and submit states** in an `aria-live="polite"` region: "Checking username…", "Saved", "Submitting…". A visual spinner is invisible to screen readers.
*   **Keyboard:** Tab order must follow visual order; Enter submits from a text field (implicit submission — which is why you keep a real submit button); Escape should cancel inline edits. Every control reachable and operable by keyboard alone.
*   **`autocomplete`** **tokens** (`email`, `name`, `street-address`, `new-password`) let browsers and password managers fill correctly — an accessibility _and_ conversion win, and a WCAG 2.1 requirement (Identify Input Purpose).
*   **Don't disable the submit as your error strategy** (see §12) — a disabled button is unfocusable and gives assistive tech nothing to explain _why_ the user is stuck.
*   **Contrast ≥ 4.5:1** for label, input, and error text; focus ring ≥ 3:1 against its background; every field ≥ 44px target.
* * *

## 11\. Innovative & Emerging Ideas (2026-aware)
The form looks solved and keeps evolving:
*   **Server Actions as the default data path:** `<form action={serverAction}>` with `useActionState`/`useFormStatus` is collapsing the form-endpoint-fetch stack into one progressively-enhanced primitive. Remix/React Router pushed the same model. Forms that work without JS are back in fashion — as an architecture, not a compromise.
*   **`useOptimistic`** **\+ streaming validation:** the form updates instantly and reconciles when the server answers, with field errors streaming back inline. Perceived latency drops to near zero.
*   **AI-assisted fields:** "clean up my address," "extract fields from this uploaded PDF/photo," and generative autofill turning a paragraph into structured form data. The form becomes a target for an agent, not just a human.
*   **Passkeys / WebAuthn** replacing password fields entirely — the highest-leverage form-conversion change of the decade is deleting the password field.
*   **Native** **`<selectlist>`****\*\*\*\*/customizable select** and the popover + anchor-positioning APIs are eliminating a class of custom-JS field widgets, shrinking the form's JS footprint.
*   **Spatial / voice form-fill:** the same form exposed as fields and as a conversational intent, sharing one validation schema.
*   **Zod-first full-stack types:** one schema drives client validation, server validation, TypeScript types, _and_ the API contract — the form's shape defined exactly once.

## 12\. Conversion Killers (the silent form profit-leaks)
None of these throw an error. They just quietly drop completion, which makes them the dangerous ones.
*   **The disabled-submit anti-pattern.** Graying out "Continue" until the form is "perfect," with no explanation, strands users who can't tell what's missing — and gives screen readers nothing to announce. **Let them click, then show the errors.** A disabled submit is a dead end; an always-clickable submit with a clear error summary is a path forward.
*   **Losing input on error.** The user fills 12 fields, one's wrong, and the form reloads _empty_. This is a rage-quit generator. Always preserve entered values through a validation failure — client and server.
*   **No pending state on submit.** They click "Pay," nothing visibly changes, so they click again (double charge) or leave thinking it broke. Disable + label change + spinner on submit is mandatory.
*   **Too many fields.** Every field measurably drops completion. Ask for the minimum now; defer the rest to after conversion. The highest-converting form is the shortest honest one.
*   **Validating too eagerly.** Throwing a red error on a field the user hasn't finished typing (or hasn't even reached) feels like being scolded. Validate on blur/submit, not on the first keystroke.
*   **Placeholder-as-label.** Looks clean, tests terribly: the label vanishes on focus, contrast fails, and users forget what the field was. Use real labels.
*   **Vague errors.** "Invalid input" tells the user nothing. Name the field, the problem, and the fix: "Enter a valid email like [name@example.com](mailto:name@example.com)."
*   **Errors far from fields, no summary.** A single error at the top with no link, or errors only next to fields with no summary — either way the user hunts. Do both: a linked summary _and_ inline messages.
*   **No autocomplete / password-manager hostility.** Blocking paste in password fields, missing `autocomplete` tokens, or breaking autofill adds friction to every single submit.

**The through-line:** these all fail _quietly_ — no log, no crash, just a lower completion number that's easy to blame on "the traffic." Audit your forms before you audit your funnel.
* * *

## 13\. Advanced Patterns
Senior moves that lean on the platform and the type system.
*   **Multi-intent submits, no JS.** Two submit buttons, same form, different `formaction` or `name`/`value` — the server branches on which was clicked (Save draft vs Publish). Degrades gracefully with JS off.

```html
<form action="/posts" method="post">
  <button type="submit" formaction="/posts/draft">Save draft</button>
  <button type="submit" formaction="/posts/publish">Publish</button>
</form>
```

*   **One schema, four consumers.** A single Zod schema feeds client validation, server validation, TypeScript types, and (via `zod-to-json-schema`) your API docs. The form's shape defined once, enforced everywhere.
*   **Token-driven density.** Expose field height, gaps, and radius as CSS custom properties so "comfortable → compact" is a token swap, not a rewrite — the same three-tier token architecture the Button doc describes.
*   **`FormData`** **\+ Server Actions as the universal wire format.** Uncontrolled inputs → `FormData` → Server Action means you never hand-serialize state, and file uploads ride the same path as text.
*   **Type-enforced accessibility.** A discriminated union that _requires_ an error-message id whenever a field is `aria-invalid`, so the wiring can't be forgotten at compile time — the same technique the Button doc uses to force `aria-label` on icon-only buttons.
*   **Field-level async validation with cancellation.** "Is this username taken?" fires a debounced, abortable request per field; a stale response never overwrites a newer one (`AbortController`).
*   **Progressive enhancement as the default posture.** Build the form to work with `action`/`method`/submit and no JS; layer client validation, inline errors, and optimistic UI on top. The floor is always a working form.
* * *

## 14\. Performance & Bundle Cost
A form feels free; at scale it isn't.
*   **Uncontrolled beats controlled for large forms.** A fully-controlled 30-field form re-renders the whole subtree on every keystroke. React Hook Form's subscription model (or plain uncontrolled + `FormData`) keeps re-renders local. This is the single biggest form-perf lever.
*   **Validation library weight.** Zod is convenient but not free (~13kb+ min+gz depending on usage); Valibot is a lighter, tree-shakeable alternative when bundle size is tight. For simple forms, the native Constraint Validation API costs zero bytes.
*   **Code-split wizard steps.** Don't ship step 4's fields (and their heavy widgets — date pickers, rich text) in the initial bundle. Lazy-load each step.
*   **Debounce everything expensive.** Autosave, async field validation, and search-as-you-type must be debounced/throttled or they hammer the server and thrash the UI.
*   **Server Actions cut client JS.** Posting via `<form action={serverAction}>` moves logic server-side and can eliminate a client-side data-fetching layer entirely — less JS shipped, and it works before hydration.
*   **Don't animate layout on validation.** Inserting an error message that shifts every field below it (layout thrash) is jarring; reserve space or animate `transform`/`opacity` only.
* * *

## 15\. Security (consolidated — the form is a doorway into your backend)
Fully detailed in §9.9; the checklist view, because it's the part that bites hardest:
*   **CSRF token** on every state-changing form + `SameSite` cookies.
*   **Re-validate server-side** with the same schema. Client validation is UX, never a control.
*   **Allow-list fields** — never spread the raw body into a model (mass assignment).
*   **Guard URL fields against SSRF** — block private/loopback/metadata ranges, allow-list schemes.
*   **Rate-limit** auth and contact endpoints; add honeypots/CAPTCHA for spam.
*   **Parameterize queries** (no string-built SQL) and escape on output (no stored XSS).
*   **Validate uploads by content**, cap size, store outside web root, serve inertly.
*   **Idempotency** so a double-submit can't create duplicates.
*   **Don't leak** in errors — a login form must not reveal whether the email or the password was wrong (enumeration).

## 16\. Senior-Level Checklist (ship-ready)
Before a form is "done":
- [ ] It's a real `<form>` with `method`, `action`, and a real `<button type="submit">`.
- [ ] Works with JavaScript disabled (progressive-enhancement floor), enhanced with JS.
- [ ] Every control has a programmatic `<label>`; no placeholder-as-label.
- [ ] Related fields grouped in `<fieldset>`/`<legend>`.
- [ ] `autocomplete` tokens set; password managers and autofill work.
- [ ] Validation runs client-side (UX) **and** server-side (truth) from a shared schema.
- [ ] Errors wired with `aria-invalid` + `aria-describedby`; not color-only.
- [ ] Focus-managed error summary at the top, `role="alert"`, links to each field.
- [ ] Submit shows a pending state and blocks double-submit; server is idempotent.
- [ ] Input is preserved through a validation failure (client and server).
- [ ] Submit is not disabled-as-error-strategy; users can click and see what's wrong.
- [ ] Single column, top labels, one idea per row, ≤~640px column, 44px targets.
- [ ] CSRF token, field allow-listing, rate limiting, SSRF/upload defenses on the endpoint.
- [ ] Dirty-state guard or autosave for long forms; wizard steps persist across Back.
- [ ] `axe` clean; keyboard-only completion works; contrast ≥ 4.5:1.

## 17\. Visual Styles (the same form, eleven skins)
Style is skin; the skeleton never changes. Across all eleven, the form keeps its labels, focus rings, error wiring, 44px targets, and keyboard behavior — only the surface treatment moves. See them live on the [Design Styles (visual languages)](https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-81211/838qa-200551) page.
*   **Flat:** solid field fills, 1px borders, crisp corners, flat error text. Fast, legible, the safe default for dense data entry.
*   **Material:** filled or outlined text fields with floating labels, helper/error text in the field's own slot, a colored underline/focus state. Strong, opinionated spacing.
*   **Glassmorphism:** translucent panel with `backdrop-filter: blur()` over a vivid background; fields as frosted cards. Guard label/error contrast hard — glass loses text legibility fast.
*   **Liquid Glass (the 2026 trend):** Apple's iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe material — the form panel and inputs as refractive translucent surfaces with a specular rim and a subtle moving sheen. Gorgeous over a dark saturated backdrop; guard for contrast _and_ `prefers-reduced-motion` (kill the sheen), and never let the refraction eat the error text.
*   **Neumorphism:** fields as soft extruded/inset wells on a same-color surface. Pretty, but contrast-poor by nature — accent only, and force a real visible border + focus ring so fields don't disappear.
*   **Skeuomorphism:** inset field wells with inner shadow, beveled edges, a "carved paper form" feel. Tactile and old-school-trustworthy for certain brands.
*   **Neo-Brutalism:** thick black borders, hard offset shadows on fields, zero radius, loud error colors. High personality; keep the error summary just as loud so it still reads as the priority.
*   **Claymorphism:** big radius, soft inner top-light / bottom-shadow on fields, puffy friendly inputs. Warm, approachable — good for consumer signup.
*   **Aurora / Gradient:** animated multi-hue gradient framing a dark form; accent CTAs glow. Premium; honor `prefers-reduced-motion` and keep field interiors calm so inputs stay readable.
*   **Minimal / Swiss:** typography and grid do all the work — hairline underlined fields, generous whitespace, near-zero decoration, sharp focus ring. Ages the best.
*   **UJG Brand:** Afro-Futurist house style — fields on deep Eminence/Night surfaces, Goldenrod focus rings and primary submit, Platinum label/body text, a confident radius and a warm glow on the active field. Error text in a legible Spanish-Orange-adjacent danger that still clears 4.5:1 on the dark field. Liquid Glass accents over the Eminence backdrop for hero/marketing forms; Flat/Swiss for the dense internal and data-entry forms.

**Rule that never changes:** the form's semantics, labels, focus management, error announcement, and 44px targets are identical across all eleven skins. If a style forces you to drop a label, hide a focus ring, or bury an error, the style is wrong — not the accessibility.
* * *

**Bottom line:** a form is not a pile of inputs — it's the orchestrator that composes controls, owns submission, runs validation on both sides of the wire, announces what went wrong, and defends the endpoint behind it. Master the native `<form>` semantics, the pristine → dirty → submitting → success/error state machine, the client-UX-plus-server-truth validation split, the focus-managed error summary, and the CSRF/mass-assignment/SSRF defenses on the backend, and you've mastered the highest-stakes, highest-blast-radius surface in the product. Everything the user actually _commits_ to, they commit through a form.
