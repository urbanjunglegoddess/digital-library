## 1\. What a Radio Group Actually Is
A radio button is a control for choosing **exactly one option from a small, mutually exclusive set**. But here's the thing juniors miss on day one: _the radio button is not the component._ The **group** is. A lone radio button is a nonsense object — it can be selected but never \_un\_selected by the user, and "one of one" is not a choice. The real unit of engineering is the **radio group**: two-or-more radios that share a single `name`, so selecting one clears the others.

The name comes from car radios — the row of mechanical preset buttons where pushing one popped the others out. Push AM, FM pops out. That physical mutual-exclusion is the whole mental model.

Four controls get lumped together and confused constantly. Getting these distinctions right is the difference between a senior and a junior build:

*   **Radio group (one-of-many, all visible):** pick exactly one from a set you can _see all at once_. Best when the number of options is small (≤5-ish) and comparing them side by side matters. "Shipping speed: Standard / Express / Overnight."
*   **Checkbox (many-of-many, independent):** each box is its own on/off, unrelated to its neighbors. A checkbox says "this one thing, yes or no." A group of checkboxes lets you pick _zero, one, or several_. The #1 confusion: using checkboxes where only one answer is valid, or radios where multiple should be. **Radio = one. Checkbox = any number.**
*   **Select / dropdown (one-of-many, collapsed):** same "pick one" semantics as a radio group, but the options are _hidden_ behind a collapsed control until you open it. Trades visibility for vertical space. Right when the list is long (states, countries, 20+ options).
*   **Segmented control (one-of-many, horizontal toggle):** the same radio semantics wearing a button-bar skin. iOS's Day/Week/Month toggle. Under the hood it should be `role="radiogroup"` with `role="radio"` children — it _is_ a radio group, just visually a joined button row.

**The senior heuristic — radios vs. a select:** reach for a radio group when the options are **≤5 and visible**, when **comparison matters** (the user benefits from seeing all choices at once), and when the choice is **important enough to deserve the screen space**. Reach for a select when the list is long, when options are self-explanatory (no comparison needed), or when vertical space is precious and the field is secondary. A radio group makes the options _loud_; a select makes them _quiet_. Shipping speed at checkout is loud — show radios. Country of residence is quiet — use a select. Getting this one call right is worth more conversion than any amount of styling.

## 2\. Why It Matters
The radio group is where a user commits to a _fork in the road_. Unlike a button (which fires an action) it captures a decision that shapes everything downstream — the plan they'll pay for, the shipping they'll wait for, the answer that routes the rest of the form.

*   **Conversion lives in the framing.** A radio group _is_ the choice architecture. Which option is pre-selected (or whether any is), the order, the labels, the descriptions — these move revenue directly. A pricing selector shown as three radio cards versus a cramped `<select>` is a measurable difference in plan mix. The default option is the single most powerful nudge in all of UX, and it lives in a radio group.
*   **It's an accessibility set-piece.** The radio group is one of the most-tested patterns in every screen-reader audit because its correct behavior is _non-obvious_: one Tab stop for the whole group, arrow keys to move _and select_, a group label announced once. Build it from real `<input type="radio">` and you inherit all of it. Build it from styled `<div>`s and you own a pile of ARIA you'll get wrong.
*   **It sets the trust tone.** A radio group where the whole label isn't clickable — where only the tiny 16px circle responds — feels broken and cheap. A group with no visible selected state, or where the selection ring vanishes on your dark background, makes users second-guess what they picked. Precision here reads as competence.
* * *

## 3\. Anatomy of a Radio Group
Every well-built radio group is composed of predictable parts. Note that some belong to the _group_ and some to each _option_ — keeping that straight is half the battle.

**Group-level parts:**
*   **Fieldset / group container:** the semantic wrapper that binds the options together. Native: `<fieldset>`. Custom: `role="radiogroup"`.
*   **Legend / group label:** the question being answered ("Choose a plan"). Native: `<legend>`. This is announced to screen readers when focus enters the group — it's _not_ optional decoration.
*   **Shared** **`name`****\*\*\*\*:** the invisible thread. Every radio in the group carries the _same_ `name` attribute; that's the browser-native mechanism that enforces mutual exclusion and groups them for keyboard nav. Get this wrong and you have N independent, all-selectable radios.
*   **Help/error text:** optional group-level description or validation message, wired with `aria-describedby`.

**Per-option parts:**
*   **Control (the circle):** the visual `<input type="radio">` or its custom stand-in. Two visual states: empty ring and filled dot.
*   **Option label:** the text for this choice. Must be wired to the input (via `<label for>` or wrapping) so **clicking the text selects the option** — this is the part cheap builds skip.
*   **Optional description:** a secondary line under the label ("Best for teams up to 10").
*   **Optional icon / price / badge:** in card/tile radios, richer content sits inside the clickable label.
*   **Selection indicator:** the filled dot, plus (in custom builds) a border/background change on the whole tile.
*   **Focus ring:** shown on the _currently focused_ radio during keyboard navigation.
*   **Hit target:** the actual clickable area — should be the **entire label row/tile**, minimum 44×44px, never just the circle.
* * *

## 4\. Sizes & Density
The control circle is small by convention, but the _hit target_ and _row rhythm_ are what actually matter. A typical scale:

| Size | Control ø | Font | Row height | Gap (control→label) | Use |
| ---| ---| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| SM | 16px | 13px | 32px | 8px | Dense settings, admin forms |
| MD | 20px | 14px | 40px | 10px | Default for most UI |
| LG | 24px | 16px | 48px | 12px | Mobile-first, primary choices |
| Card | 20px + tile | 15px | auto (padding 16px) | — | Plan pickers, rich options |

Rules that matter:
*   **The visible circle can be 16px; the tap target must not.** Expand the clickable area to the full label row (min 44px tall) with padding — never leave a user aiming at a 16px dot on a phone.
*   **Vertical stacking beats horizontal for scannability.** One option per row, left-aligned, lets the eye run straight down the list and compare. Horizontal layouts (or segmented controls) are for 2-4 short options only.
*   **Consistent gap** between control and label is a token, not a magic number, so the whole system scales with density.
*   **Dot size scales with the ring**, not independently — the filled dot is typically ~40-50% of the ring diameter.
* * *

## 5\. States (the part juniors forget)
A radio has both _per-option_ states and a _group-level_ selection model. Missing states are the #1 tell of an amateur build. Per option:

1. **Unselected (rest)** — empty ring, the baseline.
2. **Hover** — pointer over the option row. Subtle ring/label shift. (Doesn't exist on touch, never rely on it alone.)
3. **Focus** — reachable by keyboard; must show a visible ring via `:focus-visible`. In a radio group, focus and selection often move _together_ with arrow keys — but they can be decoupled (see §10).
4. **Selected (checked)** — the filled dot. The one non-negotiable visual: it must be obvious _at a glance_ which option is chosen, using more than color alone (the dot's presence is the signal).
5. **Selected + focused** — the chosen option while keyboard-focused; both the fill and the focus ring show.
6. **Active (pressing)** — the moment of click/tap. Slight scale or fill flash. Sells tactility.
7. **Disabled** — this option isn't currently allowed. Reduced opacity, `cursor: not-allowed`, `aria-disabled` or native `disabled`. A single option can be disabled while the rest of the group works.
8. **Error / invalid** — the _group_ failed validation ("Please select a shipping method"). Styled at the group level, wired with `aria-describedby` and announced.
9. **Read-only-ish** — radios have no true `readonly` (see §7); you emulate it by disabling the unselected options.

**The group-level state that trips everyone up: no selection.** A radio group can legitimately start with _nothing_ selected. Whether it should is a real debate (§7). The states above must all render correctly in that "zero selected" starting condition — including keyboard entry, which behaves differently when nothing is checked yet.
* * *

## 6\. Types & Variants
Same semantics, different presentations. Pick by content richness and space.

*   **Standard vertical list:** native circle + label, one per row. The default. Scannable, accessible, boring in the good way.
*   **Horizontal / inline:** 2-4 short options in a row. Space-efficient; breaks down past a few options or with long labels.
*   **Segmented control:** joined button-bar skin over radio semantics. iOS-style. Great for 2-4 mutually exclusive views/filters. Still `role="radiogroup"` underneath.
*   **Card / tile radios:** the _whole card_ is the label and the control. Each tile holds an icon, a title, a description, maybe a price. The selected card gets a border + background + a check/dot. This is the plan-picker pattern (§9). The senior detail: the entire tile is one clickable `<label>` wrapping a visually-hidden `<input>` — not a div with an onClick.
*   **Image / swatch radios:** color swatches, avatar picks, thumbnail choices. Same tile pattern; the "label" is an image. Needs an accessible text name per option regardless of the visual.
*   **Radio + reveal:** selecting an option expands a dependent field ("Other ▸ \[text input\]"). The revealed input is described by / associated with its radio.
*   **Custom-styled radios:** the native input is visually hidden or restyled with `accent-color`, but the underlying `<input type="radio">` stays for behavior and a11y. This is how you get brand looks without losing keyboard support (§9.2).
* * *

## 7\. When to Use One (and When Not To)
Use a radio group when the user must **pick exactly one** from a **small, comparable, visible set**. Don't use one when:
*   **Multiple answers are valid** → checkboxes. If a user could ever reasonably want two, radios are wrong.
*   **The list is long (roughly >5-7 options)** → a `<select>` or combobox. Ten radios is a wall; a dropdown collapses it.
*   **It's a binary on/off for a single setting** → a single checkbox or a switch, not a two-radio Yes/No group (usually).
*   **The options are actions, not choices** → buttons. "Save / Cancel" are actions; "Small / Medium / Large" is a choice.

**The radios-vs-select decision, expanded** (this is the call seniors are paid to make):
*   **≤5 options, comparison matters, choice is consequential → radios.** Shipping speed, subscription tier, payment method. Seeing them all at once _is_ the value.
*   **Long list, self-explanatory, secondary field → select.** Country, state, time zone, "how did you hear about us."
*   **Mobile with scarce vertical space and a secondary choice → select** (native mobile select is a great picker). But a _primary_ choice still deserves radios even on mobile — just go full-width tiles.

**The no-default-selection debate** (settle this per field, deliberately):
*   **Pre-select a default when** there's a genuinely safe/common/recommended option and you want to reduce friction. The default carries enormous weight — most users accept it. Use that power responsibly (don't pre-select the most expensive plan and call it UX).
*   **Start with nothing selected when** the choice is consequential and you need a _deliberate_ answer — legal consent, "how should we contact you," anything where a silent default could be an unfair or wrong assumption. Forcing an explicit pick prevents "I didn't realize it defaulted to X."
*   **The accessibility catch:** an empty group and a pre-selected group behave _differently_ under the keyboard. With a selection, Tab lands on the checked radio. With nothing selected, Tab lands on the _first_ radio and it's focusable but not yet checked — arrow keys then move-and-select. Test both paths.
*   **The anti-pattern:** never use a radio group with a "None"/"No preference" option _and_ no default as a lazy substitute for deciding — if "none" is valid, make it an explicit, labeled option.

Placement heuristics: label above the group, options stacked below, error text directly under the group. Keep the group visually bounded so it reads as one unit, not scattered controls.

## 8\. Radio Groups Across Design Systems
Same control, different philosophies. Fluency here means you can drop into any codebase.
*   **Material Design (Google):** `<md-radio>` / Radio components with a ripple on the control, a clear filled-dot selected state, and built-in `RadioGroup` handling. Labels are part of the touch target. Material 3 leans into larger targets and expressive motion on selection.
*   **Apple HIG (iOS/macOS):** On macOS, classic vertical radio lists are standard for settings. On iOS, Apple largely _replaces_ radios with **table-view checkmarks** (a list where the selected row shows a ✓) and **segmented controls** for 2-4 inline choices. The pattern survives; the widget changes.
*   **Fluent (Microsoft):** `RadioGroup` + `Radio`, compact, neutral, with clear grouping and label association. Built for dense settings surfaces.
*   **Ant Design / enterprise:** `Radio.Group` with both classic radios and `Radio.Button` (segmented-style). Heavy support for option arrays, disabled options, and controlled value binding — built for data-dense admin forms.
*   **Tailwind / utility-first:** no prescribed component; you compose from `accent-color` or a peer-based custom control. Usually wrapped in a `RadioGroup` component to enforce consistency.
*   **shadcn/Radix (modern web standard):** Radix `RadioGroup` provides headless, fully-accessible roving-tabindex behavior; shadcn skins it with Tailwind. This is the current default for serious React apps — you get correct keyboard semantics for free and own the looks (§9.7).
*   **Bootstrap:** `.form-check` with `.form-check-input`/`.form-check-label`, plus `.btn-check` + `.btn` for a segmented "button radio" look. Ready-made, accessible, instantly recognizable.

## 8b. Visual Styles note
The radio group wears all eleven house skins (§17). The rule that never changes: **the skin is cosmetic; the shared** **`name`****\*\*\*\*, the one-Tab-stop, the arrow-key move-and-select, the visible focus ring, and the 44px target stay identical across every style.** A glassmorphic radio and a neo-brutalist radio are the same skeleton in different clothes. Full guidance lives in the [Design Styles (visual languages)](https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-81211/838qa-200551) doc.

### What's trending in 2026
Radios are inheriting the same forces as everything else: **Liquid Glass** selected-tiles on Apple platforms (the filled state refracts the backdrop with a specular rim), **Expressive Material 3** springy dot-fill motion, and card/tile radios becoming the default for any consequential choice because they give room for a description and a price. AI-native forms increasingly _pre-fill_ a recommended radio and explain _why_ inline ("we suggest Express based on your address") — which raises the stakes on the no-default debate: a suggested default must be honest, reversible, and clearly labeled as a suggestion. **For UJG:** card-style radios on deep Eminence with a Goldenrod selected-ring and dot are the house default for plan/tier pickers; keep flat vertical radios for dense settings; respect `prefers-reduced-motion` on any dot-fill animation.

## 9\. The Code
### 9.1 HTML (the foundation everyone should master first)
The whole component, done right, in pure HTML. Notice what does the work: the shared `name`, the `fieldset`/`legend`, and label association.

```plain
<!-- The group is a fieldset; the question is the legend -->
<fieldset>
  <legend>Choose a shipping speed</legend>

  <!-- Every input shares the SAME name — that's what makes them one group -->
  <div class="radio-row">
    <input type="radio" id="ship-standard" name="shipping" value="standard" checked>
    <label for="ship-standard">Standard <span>3–5 business days · Free</span></label>
  </div>

  <div class="radio-row">
    <input type="radio" id="ship-express" name="shipping" value="express">
    <label for="ship-express">Express <span>2 business days · $9</span></label>
  </div>

  <div class="radio-row">
    <input type="radio" id="ship-overnight" name="shipping" value="overnight">
    <label for="ship-overnight">Overnight <span>Next business day · $25</span></label>
  </div>
</fieldset>

<!-- A disabled single option inside a working group -->
<div class="radio-row">
  <input type="radio" id="ship-drone" name="shipping" value="drone" disabled>
  <label for="ship-drone">Drone <span>Not available in your area</span></label>
</div>
```

Key HTML rules — this is where most bugs are born:
*   **The shared** **`name`** **is the entire mechanism.** Same `name` = one mutually-exclusive group + one Tab stop + arrow-key nav, all native. Different `name`s = N independent radios that can all be checked at once. This is the single most common radio bug.
*   **Every input needs a unique** **`id`****\*\*\*\*, and every** **`<label>`** **a matching** **`for`****\*\*\*\*.** That's what makes _clicking the text_ select the option and what gives screen readers the accessible name. Alternatively, wrap the input inside the `<label>` (implicit association) — no `for` needed then.
*   **`<fieldset>`** **+** **`<legend>`** **are required for a real group**, not optional polish. The legend is the group's accessible name; without it a screen reader announces three orphan radios with no context.
*   **`value`** **is what the server receives** — the label text is for humans, the `value` is for the backend. Only the checked radio's `name=value` pair is submitted.
*   **`checked`** **sets the default.** Omit it on every option for a no-default group. There is no `checked` on more than one — the last one wins if you try.
*   **Radios have no real** **`readonly`****\*\*\*\*.** `readonly` is ignored on radios; to lock a group, `disabled` the options you don't want changed.

### 9.2 CSS (custom-styled, accessible)
The senior way to style a radio: keep the real `<input type="radio">` for behavior, and either use `accent-color` (one line, huge win) or visually hide the input and draw your own control that stays in sync via `:checked`.

```css
/* Option A: the one-liner. Brand the native control, keep ALL behavior/a11y. */
.radio-row input[type="radio"] {
  accent-color: #5F2C82;   /* Eminence — themes the checked dot + ring */
  width: 20px;
  height: 20px;
}

/* Layout: the whole row is a comfortable, aligned hit target */
.radio-row {
  display: flex;
  align-items: flex-start;
  gap: 10px;
  min-height: 44px;
  padding: 8px;
  border-radius: 10px;
}
.radio-row:hover { background: rgba(95, 44, 130, .06); }
.radio-row label { cursor: pointer; line-height: 1.3; }
.radio-row label span { display: block; color: #6b6b6b; font-size: 13px; }

/* Keyboard focus only — draw the ring on the control */
.radio-row input[type="radio"]:focus-visible {
  outline: 2px solid #DCA424;   /* Goldenrod */
  outline-offset: 2px;
}
.radio-row input[type="radio"]:disabled { opacity: .5; }
.radio-row:has(input:disabled) { cursor: not-allowed; opacity: .6; }

/* Option B: fully custom control (when accent-color isn't enough).
   Visually hide the input but keep it operable; draw a control that
   mirrors :checked. NEVER display:none the input — that kills focus + a11y. */
.radio-custom input[type="radio"] {
  position: absolute;
  opacity: 0;               /* invisible but present, focusable, in the a11y tree */
  width: 20px; height: 20px; margin: 0;
}
.radio-custom .control {
  width: 20px; height: 20px; border-radius: 50%;
  border: 2px solid #8a7fa0; display: inline-grid; place-content: center;
  transition: border-color .15s, box-shadow .15s;
}
.radio-custom .control::after {
  content: ""; width: 10px; height: 10px; border-radius: 50%;
  background: #5F2C82; transform: scale(0); transition: transform .15s;
}
.radio-custom input:checked + .control { border-color: #5F2C82; }
.radio-custom input:checked + .control::after { transform: scale(1); }
/* Drive the focus ring from the real input onto the fake control */
.radio-custom input:focus-visible + .control {
  outline: 2px solid #DCA424; outline-offset: 2px;
}

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  .radio-custom .control, .radio-custom .control::after { transition: none; }
}
```

The rule that separates senior from junior custom radios: **the real** **`<input>`** **never leaves the DOM.** You hide it with `opacity: 0` (still focusable, still in the accessibility tree, still keyboard-operable), _not_ `display: none` or `visibility: hidden` (which remove it from tab order and screen readers). The visible circle is pure decoration driven by `:checked` and `:focus-visible`.

### 9.3 React + TypeScript (a proper reusable, controlled RadioGroup)
The real-world pattern: a controlled group component that owns the shared `name`, wires the fieldset/legend, enforces one-of-many at the type level, and threads `aria-describedby` for errors. This is usually the longest block for a reason.

```plain
import { forwardRef, useId, type ReactNode } from "react";

export interface RadioOption<T extends string> {
  value: T;
  label: ReactNode;
  description?: ReactNode;
  disabled?: boolean;
}

interface RadioGroupProps<T extends string> {
  /** Group question, rendered as the <legend>. */
  legend: ReactNode;
  /** The one shared name for the whole group (mutual exclusion). */
  name: string;
  options: RadioOption<T>[];
  /** Controlled selected value. `null` = nothing selected yet. */
  value: T | null;
  onChange: (value: T) => void;
  /** Group-level validation message; wired to every input via aria-describedby. */
  error?: string;
  required?: boolean;
}

export function RadioGroup<T extends string>({
  legend, name, options, value, onChange, error, required,
}: RadioGroupProps<T>) {
  const errorId = useId();
  return (
    <fieldset
      role="radiogroup"
      aria-invalid={error ? true : undefined}
      aria-describedby={error ? errorId : undefined}
      aria-required={required || undefined}
      className="radio-group"
    >
      <legend>{legend}</legend>

      {options.map((opt) => {
        const id = `${name}-${opt.value}`;
        return (
          <div className="radio-row" key={opt.value}>
            <input
              type="radio"
              id={id}
              name={name}                       /* shared name = one group */
              value={opt.value}
              checked={value === opt.value}      /* controlled */
              disabled={opt.disabled}
              onChange={() => onChange(opt.value)}
            />
            <label htmlFor={id}>
              {opt.label}
              {opt.description && <span className="desc">{opt.description}</span>}
            </label>
          </div>
        );
      })}

      {error && (
        <p id={errorId} className="radio-error" role="alert">
          {error}
        </p>
      )}
    </fieldset>
  );
}
```

Usage — note the state is a single value, never an array (that's the checkbox shape):

```plain
function ShippingPicker() {
  const [speed, setSpeed] = useState<"standard" | "express" | "overnight" | null>(null);
  return (
    <RadioGroup
      legend="Choose a shipping speed"
      name="shipping"
      value={speed}
      onChange={setSpeed}
      error={speed === null ? "Please select a shipping method" : undefined}
      required
      options={[
        { value: "standard",  label: "Standard",  description: "3–5 days · Free" },
        { value: "express",   label: "Express",   description: "2 days · $9" },
        { value: "overnight", label: "Overnight", description: "Next day · $25" },
      ]}
    />
  );
}
```

Why this is the senior version, not the junior one:
*   **Generic** **`<T extends string>`** ties the selected value, the options, and `onChange` to the _same_ string-literal union — a typo'd value won't compile, and the state is provably one-of-the-allowed.
*   **`value: T | null`** models the no-default-selection case honestly instead of pretending something's always chosen.
*   **The group owns** **`name`** so callers can't accidentally split the group by passing different names per option.
*   **`aria-describedby`** **+** **`role="alert"`** on the error means the validation message is _announced_, not just shown — the difference between passing and failing an audit.
*   **Native** **`<input>`** **under the hood** means arrow-key roving, mutual exclusion, and form submission all come free from the browser. We're not reimplementing a radiogroup; we're labeling one.

### 9.4 Card / Tile Radios (the whole tile is the label)
The plan-picker pattern. The senior detail: it's a real `<input type="radio">` visually hidden inside a `<label>` that _is_ the whole card. Clicking anywhere on the tile selects it; keyboard and screen-reader support come free because the control is a native radio.

```plain
function PlanCards({ value, onChange }: {
  value: string | null; onChange: (v: string) => void;
}) {
  const plans = [
    { value: "starter", name: "Starter", price: "$0",  blurb: "For trying things out" },
    { value: "pro",     name: "Pro",     price: "$29", blurb: "For growing teams" },
    { value: "scale",   name: "Scale",   price: "$99", blurb: "For heavy usage" },
  ];
  return (
    <fieldset className="plan-cards" role="radiogroup" aria-label="Choose a plan">
      <legend className="sr-only">Choose a plan</legend>
      {plans.map((p) => (
        <label key={p.value} className="plan-card" data-selected={value === p.value}>
          <input
            type="radio" name="plan" value={p.value}
            checked={value === p.value}
            onChange={() => onChange(p.value)}
            className="sr-only"                 /* visually hidden, still real */
          />
          <span className="plan-card__dot" aria-hidden="true" />
          <span className="plan-card__name">{p.name}</span>
          <span className="plan-card__price">{p.price}</span>
          <span className="plan-card__blurb">{p.blurb}</span>
        </label>
      ))}
    </fieldset>
  );
}
```

```css
  position: absolute; width: 1px; height: 1px; padding: 0; margin: -1px;
  overflow: hidden; clip: rect(0 0 0 0); white-space: nowrap; border: 0;

```

The anti-pattern this replaces: a `<div onClick>` card with a decorative radio circle that isn't a real input. That version has no keyboard support, no arrow-key nav, no form submission, and no screen-reader role — every one of which the native version gives you for free. **The whole tile is a** **`<label>`****\*\*\*\*; the label wraps a real radio. That's the pattern.**

### 9.5 Vanilla JavaScript (reading + reacting)
Reading a group's value and reacting to changes without a framework. Bind one listener to the group, not one per radio.

```plain
const group = document.querySelector('fieldset');

// Read the current value at any time:
function selectedShipping() {
  const checked = group.querySelector('input[name="shipping"]:checked');
  return checked ? checked.value : null;   // null = nothing chosen yet
}

// React to changes with ONE delegated listener on the group:
group.addEventListener('change', (e) => {
  if (e.target.name !== 'shipping') return;
  updateOrderTotal(e.target.value);        // e.target is the newly-checked radio
});

// Programmatically set a value (and fire change for listeners):
function selectShipping(value) {
  const radio = group.querySelector(`input[value="${value}"]`);
  if (radio && !radio.disabled) {
    radio.checked = true;
    radio.dispatchEvent(new Event('change', { bubbles: true }));
  }
}
```

Notes: the `change` event fires on the radio that becomes checked (never on the one that becomes unchecked — the browser handles the deselect silently). One delegated listener on the fieldset beats N listeners on N radios. And you _never_ need to manually uncheck siblings — the shared `name` does it.

```plain
// app/checkout/actions.ts
"use server";
import { z } from "zod";

const Schema = z.object({ speed: z.enum(["standard", "express", "overnight"]) });

export async function setShipping(_prev: unknown, formData: FormData) {
  const parsed = Schema.safeParse({ speed: formData.get("speed") });
```

### 9.9 Node.js (the endpoint behind the group)
Only _one_ value arrives from a radio group (the checked option), and it must be validated against a fixed allow-list — a radio value is fully attacker-controllable, so "it was in my UI" is not a guarantee.

```plain
import express from "express";
import { z } from "zod";

const router = express.Router();

// Server-side truth: the enum, not whatever the client sent.
const Checkout = z.object({
  speed: z.enum(["standard", "express", "overnight"]),
  plan:  z.enum(["starter", "pro", "scale"]),
});

router.post("/api/checkout", requireAuth, async (req, res) => {
  const parsed = Checkout.safeParse(req.body);
  if (!parsed.success) return res.status(422).json(parsed.error.issues);

  // Even valid enums can be unauthorized: can THIS user pick this plan?
  if (parsed.data.plan === "scale" && !req.user.canUseScale)
    return res.sendStatus(403);

  const order = await db.orders.create({ ...parsed.data, userId: req.user.id });
  return res.status(201).json(order);
});

export default router;
```

The through-line with Button's backend chapter: the UI restricting choices to three radios is UX, not security. `curl` can POST `speed=free_teleport`. The `z.enum` allow-list is the actual boundary. Radio-specific note: if _nothing_ was selected and the field was required, the key is simply _absent_ from the body — validate for presence, don't assume an empty string.

### 9.11 Testing the Radio Group
You test _behavior_: the group is one Tab stop, arrow keys move and select, only one is ever checked, clicking a label selects it, and the required-but-empty case is caught. Query `byRole("radio", { checked })` so the tests prove the accessibility state.

```plain
import { render, screen } from "@testing-library/react";
import userEvent from "@testing-library/user-event";
import { axe } from "jest-axe";
import { describe, it, expect, vi } from "vitest";
import { RadioGroup } from "./RadioGroup";

const opts = [
  { value: "a", label: "Alpha" },
  { value: "b", label: "Beta" },
  { value: "c", label: "Gamma" },
];

describe("RadioGroup", () => {
  it("selects an option when its LABEL is clicked", async () => {
    const onChange = vi.fn();
    render(<RadioGroup legend="Pick" name="g" value={null} onChange={onChange} options={opts} />);
    await userEvent.click(screen.getByText("Beta"));   // click text, not circle
    expect(onChange).toHaveBeenCalledWith("b");
  });

  it("keeps exactly one selected (mutual exclusion)", () => {
    render(<RadioGroup legend="Pick" name="g" value="a" onChange={() => {}} options={opts} />);
    const checked = screen.getAllByRole("radio").filter((r) => (r as HTMLInputElement).checked);
    expect(checked).toHaveLength(1);
  });

  it("is one Tab stop; arrow keys move AND select", async () => {
    const onChange = vi.fn();
    render(<RadioGroup legend="Pick" name="g" value="a" onChange={onChange} options={opts} />);
    await userEvent.tab();                       // focus lands on the checked radio
    expect(screen.getByRole("radio", { name: "Alpha" })).toHaveFocus();
    await userEvent.keyboard("{ArrowDown}");     // moves to next AND selects it
    expect(onChange).toHaveBeenCalledWith("b");
  });

  it("has no axe violations (fieldset/legend wired)", async () => {
    const { container } = render(
      <RadioGroup legend="Pick" name="g" value="a" onChange={() => {}} options={opts} />

    expect(await axe(container)).toHaveNoViolations();
  });

```

What to assert (and skip):
*   **Do test:** clicking the label selects, only one checked at a time, one Tab stop, arrow-keys move-and-select, the group has an accessible name (legend), required-empty is flagged, found `byRole("radio")`.
*   **Don't test:** exact class names, the dot's pixel size, hover colors. Implementation details that break tests without catching bugs.
*   **Bonus:** `axe` catches the classics — missing legend, orphan radios (mismatched `name`), unlabeled inputs.

### 9.15 Web Components (framework-agnostic)
A radio group that works anywhere, delegating the hard part (mutual exclusion) to native inputs inside the shadow DOM.

```plain
// Escape everything going into innerHTML. `options` comes from an attribute and is
// attacker-controllable, so raw ${o.label}/${o.value} would be an XSS/injection hole.
const esc = (s) => String(s).replace(/[&<>"']/g, (c) => (
  { "&": "&", "<": "<", ">": ">", '"': """, "'": "&#39;" }[c]
));

class UIRadioGroup extends HTMLElement {
  connectedCallback() {
    const name = this.getAttribute("name") || "group";
    const legend = this.getAttribute("legend") || "";
    const opts = JSON.parse(this.getAttribute("options") || "[]");
    this.attachShadow({ mode: "open" });
    this.shadowRoot.innerHTML = `
      <style>/* scoped styles */ .row{display:flex;gap:10px;min-height:44px;align-items:center}</style>
      <fieldset role="radiogroup">
        <legend>${esc(legend)}</legend>
        ${opts.map((o) => `
          <label class="row">
            <input type="radio" name="${esc(name)}" value="${esc(o.value)}">${esc(o.label)}
          </label>`).join("")}
      </fieldset>`;
    this.shadowRoot.addEventListener("change", (e) => {
      this.dispatchEvent(new CustomEvent("select", { detail: e.target.value, bubbles: true }));
    });
  }
}
customElements.define("ui-radio-group", UIRadioGroup);
// <ui-radio-group name="ship" legend="Speed" options='[{"value":"a","label":"A"}]'></ui-radio-group>
```

### 9.16 The Selection Lifecycle & Keyboard Model
The engine under the group. This is the part that separates a real radio group from three checkboxes wearing circles.

**Native keyboard behavior (the reference implementation):**
*   **Tab** moves focus _into_ the group and lands on the **checked** radio (or the **first** radio if none is checked). Tab again moves _out_ of the group entirely — the whole group is **one Tab stop**.
*   **Arrow keys (↓/→ and ↑/←)** move focus to the next/previous radio **and select it in the same motion**. This is the defining behavior: in a radio group, moving _is_ selecting. (Contrast a toolbar of buttons, where arrows move focus but you still press Enter/Space to activate.)
*   **Space** selects the focused radio (mostly relevant in the no-default case, where arrowing hasn't happened yet).
*   Selection **wraps** at the ends (arrowing past the last goes to the first).

**Roving tabindex vs. native radiogroup — know the difference:**
*   With **native** **`<input type="radio">`** **sharing a** **`name`**, the browser implements all of the above _for you_. You write nothing. This is why native radios are the right default.
*   When you build a **custom radiogroup from non-input elements** (`role="radiogroup"` + `role="radio"` divs), _you_ must implement **roving tabindex** by hand: exactly one item has `tabindex="0"`, all others `tabindex="-1"`, and your arrow-key handler moves both the real focus and the `tabindex="0"` while flipping `aria-checked`. Radix does this for you; hand-rolled, it's the #1 place custom radios go wrong.

```plain
// Roving tabindex for a CUSTOM radiogroup (only needed when NOT using <input>)
const items = [...group.querySelectorAll('[role="radio"]')];
let idx = Math.max(0, items.findIndex((el) => el.getAttribute("aria-checked") === "true"));

function select(i) {
  items.forEach((el, n) => {
    const on = n === i;
    el.setAttribute("aria-checked", String(on));
    el.tabIndex = on ? 0 : -1;          // roving: only the selected item is tabbable
  });
  items[i].focus();                      // move REAL focus, not just visual
}
select(idx);

group.addEventListener("keydown", (e) => {
  if (["ArrowDown", "ArrowRight"].includes(e.key)) { e.preventDefault(); select((idx = (idx + 1) % items.length)); }
  if (["ArrowUp", "ArrowLeft"].includes(e.key))   { e.preventDefault(); select((idx = (idx - 1 + items.length) % items.length)); }
});
```

The lesson: **if you can use native** **`<input type="radio">`****\*\*\*\*, do — the entire keyboard model is free.** Only reach for `role="radio"` + roving tabindex when you genuinely can't (e.g. the "radio" is a complex composite the browser can't render as an input), and then implement the full pattern, not half of it.

## 10\. Accessibility (non-negotiable)
The radio group is a canonical accessibility set-piece. The rules, precisely:

*   **Use real** **`<input type="radio">`** **in a** **`<fieldset>`** **with a** **`<legend>`****\*\*\*\*.** That single choice buys you the role, the keyboard model, mutual exclusion, and the group name — all free. Everything below is what you _lose_ if you fake it with divs.
*   **The shared** **`name`** **is an a11y requirement, not just a behavior one.** It's what tells assistive tech "these three are one group; you're on option 2 of 3."
*   **`<legend>`** **is the group's accessible name.** Screen readers announce it when focus enters the group ("Shipping speed, radio group, Standard, selected, 1 of 3"). No legend = orphan radios with no context. If you must use `role="radiogroup"` on a non-fieldset, give it `aria-label` or `aria-labelledby`.
*   **Keyboard map (must match native):** Tab enters/exits (one stop); ↑↓←→ move-and-select with wrap; Space selects the focused option. Native handles this; custom builds must implement roving tabindex (§9.16).
*   **Labels wired to inputs** (`<label for>` or wrapping) so the _whole label_ is clickable and named. Icon/swatch radios still need a text name.
*   **State to screen readers:** native radios expose checked/unchecked automatically. Custom `role="radio"` builds must set `aria-checked="true|false"` on every option and keep it in sync. Group errors go through `aria-describedby` on the group + `role="alert"` on the message.
*   **`aria-required`** **/** **`aria-invalid`** on the group communicate "you must pick one" and "your pick is invalid."
*   **Contrast:** the selected dot and the ring must both clear 3:1 against their background (WCAG non-text contrast); label text ≥4.5:1. Don't signal selection with color alone — the dot's _presence_ is the primary signal.
*   **Target size ≥44px:** make the whole label row/tile the hit area, never the bare circle.
*   **No-default caveat:** an empty group is valid, but announce the requirement (`aria-required`) and make the empty→invalid transition audible on submit.

The radio looks solved but keeps evolving:
*   **Card/tile radios as the default** for any consequential single choice — richer than a bare circle, room for price/description/icon, still a native radio underneath.
*   **AI-suggested defaults:** the group pre-selects a recommended option _and explains why_ inline ("Express suggested — your address is 2 days from our warehouse"). Powerful, but it sharpens the no-default ethics: the suggestion must be honest and trivially reversible.
*   **Liquid Glass selected-tiles** on Apple platforms — the chosen card refracts the backdrop with a specular rim rather than a flat fill.
*   **Segmented controls with a sliding "thumb"** that animates between options (View Transitions / shared-element motion), honoring `prefers-reduced-motion`.
*   **`field-sizing`** **and container queries** letting radio _cards_ reflow from a vertical stack to a horizontal row based on available space, not a media-query breakpoint.
*   **Native** **`accent-color`** **everywhere** — the boring-but-huge win that made 90% of custom radio CSS unnecessary; brand the control in one line and keep every native behavior.
* * *

## 12\. Conversion / UX Killers (mistakes that bleed money silently)
None of these throw an error. They just quietly drop your numbers.
*   **Only the circle is clickable.** If the label text doesn't select the option, mobile users miss constantly and the form feels broken. Wire `<label for>` / wrap the input — make the _whole row_ the target.
*   **Wrong control for the job.** Radios where multiple answers are valid (users can't pick both, rage-quit), or checkboxes where only one is valid (two get checked, ambiguous submit). Radio = one, checkbox = any.
*   **A** **`<select>`** **where radios would convert better.** Burying three pricing tiers in a dropdown hides the comparison that sells. ≤5 consequential, comparable options belong _visible_.
*   **A manipulative or invisible default.** Pre-selecting the priciest plan is a dark pattern that erodes trust (and triggers chargebacks). No visible selected state at all leaves users unsure what they chose. The default is powerful — use it honestly and show it clearly.
*   **Split group from a broken** **`name`****\*\*\*\*.** Different `name`s per option silently turn one choice into N independent selectable radios — users check three "mutually exclusive" options and the submit is nonsense. Silent, common, brutal.
*   **No group label.** Three radios with no `<legend>` — sighted users infer the question from nearby text; screen-reader users get nothing. Also hurts the visual scan.
*   **Ten radios where a select belongs.** A wall of radios is as bad as a hidden one. Past ~7 options, collapse to a dropdown.
*   **Selected state that dies on your background.** A light-gray dot on a white card, or any selection signaled by color alone, fails for low-vision users and looks unfinished to everyone.

**The through-line:** these fail _quietly_. No log, no crash — just a lower completion rate that's easy to misblame. Audit your radio groups before you audit your funnel.

## 13\. Advanced Patterns
Techniques that lean on the platform and the type system instead of custom JS.
*   **`:has()`** **for whole-tile selected styling** — style the card from its input's state with zero JS: `.plan-card:has(input:checked) { border-color: var(--brand); }`. Modern, declarative, no state mirroring.
*   **Discriminated-union options** — a TS type where certain option values _require_ an extra field (e.g. an "Other" option demands a `detail` input) catches invalid configurations at compile time.
*   **Design tokens for the control** — drive ring color, dot color, size, and focus ring from three-tier tokens (`--radio-dot`, `--radio-ring`, `--radio-focus` → semantic → primitive) so a rebrand or dark mode is a token swap, not new CSS. `accent-color: var(--radio-accent)` themes native radios directly.
*   **Radio + reveal, associated properly** — when selecting "Other" reveals a text input, wire `aria-controls`/`aria-describedby` so the relationship is announced, and move focus to the revealed field.
*   **Uncontrolled +** **`FormData`** — for simple forms, skip React state entirely: let the native radios hold state and read `new FormData(form).get("name")` on submit. Less code, fewer re-renders, still fully accessible.
*   **`aria-live`** **on the dependent summary** — when the radio choice updates a price or total elsewhere, put that summary in a polite live region so the change is announced, not just repainted.

## 14\. Performance & Bundle Cost
A radio group is cheap, but a few traps exist at scale:
*   **Prefer native** **`accent-color`** **over a JS-driven custom control.** One CSS line versus a component that re-renders on every selection. The native path has _zero_ runtime cost and free a11y.
*   **One delegated** **`change`** **listener on the group**, not one per radio — matters when you render dozens of options (surveys, matrices).
*   **Don't re-render the whole group on every keystroke.** In React, keep the group's value in a single state and memoize option rows so arrowing through a long list doesn't re-render every sibling.
*   **Icon imports in card radios** — the icon-library trap from Button applies: `import { Check } from "lucide-react"`, never `import * as Icons`.
*   **Animate only** **`transform`****/****`opacity`** on the dot-fill (compositor-only, no reflow); never animate the ring's `width`/`border-width`.
* * *

## 15\. Security (the value is attacker-controllable)
A radio group sends exactly one value, and that value is _not_ constrained by your UI once it leaves the browser.
*   **Validate against a server-side allow-list (enum), always.** The three radios you rendered don't stop `curl` from POSTing a fourth value. `z.enum([...])` / a Pydantic `Enum` is the real boundary (§9.9–9.10).
*   **Authorize the** **_choice_****\*\*\*\*, not just its validity.** A valid `plan=scale` may still be forbidden for this user's tier — re-check server-side.
*   **Presence ≠ default.** A required-but-unselected group sends _no_ key. Validate presence explicitly; don't treat a missing field as an empty string that sneaks past.
*   **Escape option data before it hits** **`innerHTML`****\*\*\*\*.** Any radio group that builds its markup from a string template (the Web Component in §9.15) must HTML-escape option labels/values first — they can arrive from an attribute or a fetch and are attacker-controllable, so a raw `${o.label}` is stored/reflected XSS. Prefer `textContent`/`new Option()`, or escape as shown in §9.15.
*   **CSRF still applies.** A radio group inside a state-changing form is an attack surface like any other — anti-CSRF token + `SameSite` cookies (see Button §9.24).

## 16\. Senior-Level Checklist (ship-ready)
Before a radio group is "done":
- [ ] All options share **one** `name` (native) or the group is one roving-tabindex `role="radiogroup"`.
- [ ] Wrapped in `<fieldset>` with a `<legend>` (or `role="radiogroup"` + `aria-label`).
- [ ] Every input has a unique `id` and a wired `<label>` — the **whole label/row/tile is clickable**.
- [ ] One Tab stop; arrow keys move **and** select; wraps at ends; Space selects.
- [ ] Selected state is obvious via more than color; focus ring visible via `:focus-visible`.
- [ ] No-default vs. pre-selected decided deliberately and correctly for the field's stakes.
- [ ] Right control chosen: radios (≤5 visible, comparable) vs. select (long) vs. checkbox (multi).
- [ ] Target ≥44px; dot/ring contrast ≥3:1, label ≥4.5:1.
- [ ] Required/invalid communicated (`aria-required`, `aria-invalid`, `role="alert"` on error).
- [ ] Server validates the value against an enum and authorizes the choice per user.
- [ ] Custom-styled radios keep the real `<input>` (`opacity:0`/`sr-only`, never `display:none`).
- [ ] Markup built from templates escapes option labels/values before `innerHTML` (or uses `textContent`).
- [ ] `prefers-reduced-motion` and `forced-colors` handled.

## 17\. Visual Styles (the same group, eleven skins)
The style is skin; the shared `name`, one-Tab-stop, arrow-key move-and-select, focus ring, and 44px target are identical across all eleven.

*   **Flat:** solid ring, solid dot, crisp edges, no shadow. The default. Reads instantly.
*   **Material:** filled dot with a subtle ripple on the control, clear elevation on card radios, larger targets.
*   **Glassmorphism:** translucent tile with `backdrop-filter: blur()` over a vivid backdrop; guard the selected dot's contrast hard — glass eats it.
*   **Liquid Glass (2026):** the selected _tile_ refracts the backdrop with a specular rim and a slow sheen; pill/rounded cards. Web = honest approximation; SwiftUI emits native `.glassEffect()`. Respect reduced-motion on the sheen.
*   **Neumorphism:** the ring is an inset/extruded soft-shadow well on a same-color surface; pretty but contrast-poor — accent use only, and never as the sole selected signal.
*   **Skeuomorphism:** beveled, glossy physical radio buttons (the literal car-radio metaphor), inner highlight on the dot.
*   **Neo-Brutalism:** thick black ring, hard offset shadow on the selected tile, zero radius, clashing accent. High personality.
*   **Claymorphism:** big-radius puffy tiles, soft inner light, a fat rounded dot; friendly and toy-like.
*   **Aurora / Gradient:** animated multi-hue gradient fills the selected dot/tile on dark; honor `prefers-reduced-motion`.
*   **Minimal / Swiss:** near-zero decoration — a thin ring, a solid dot, generous whitespace, type doing the work. Excellent for dense settings.
*   **UJG Brand:** Goldenrod dot + ring on deep Eminence/Night tiles, confident radius, a warm glow on the selected card. The house default for plan/tier pickers.

**Rule that never changes:** style is skin, behavior is the skeleton. Guard glass/liquid-glass/neumorphism for the selected-state contrast; guard aurora/liquid-glass for `prefers-reduced-motion`. Full guidance in the [Design Styles (visual languages)](https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-81211/838qa-200551) doc.
* * *

**Bottom line:** the radio button is a trap that hides its real component. Stop thinking about the circle and start thinking about the _group_ — the shared `name` that enforces one-of-many, the fieldset/legend that names the question, the one-Tab-stop-plus-arrow-keys keyboard model where moving _is_ selecting, and the whole-label hit target that makes it feel built rather than bolted on. Get those four right, choose radios only when the options are few and comparison matters, decide the default deliberately, and validate the single value server-side — and you've mastered the control that quietly decides which plan gets bought.
