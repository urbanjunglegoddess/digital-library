## 1\. What a Footer Actually Is
A footer is the closing region of a page: the band at the bottom that holds site-wide secondary information — sitemap navigation, legal links, contact, social, copyright, and often a newsletter signup. That's the plain version. The word hides three things people conflate:
**The page footer (landmark):** the top-level `<footer>` that closes the whole document. This — and _only_ this — maps to the ARIA `contentinfo` landmark. Screen-reader users jump to it directly to find copyright, privacy, and contact.
**A sectioning footer (not a landmark):** a `<footer>` nested inside an `<article>`, `<section>`, `<main>`, `<aside>`, or `<nav>`. This is a card footer, an article byline/date block, a comment footer. It is _not_ `contentinfo` and carries no landmark role — it's just the closing part of that specific chunk.
**A sticky/pinned footer (layout, not semantics):** a footer forced to the bottom of the viewport on short pages, or a `position: fixed` bar that stays glued to the screen. These are _layout techniques_ applied to either of the above. "Sticky footer" almost always means the layout trick that pins the footer to the bottom of a short page — **not** `position: sticky` and **not** a fixed bar.

The single most common mistake: assuming every `<footer>` is a landmark. It isn't. **Only the page-level footer — a** **`<footer>`** **that is** **_not_** **inside** **`article`****/****`section`****/****`main`****/****`aside`****/****`nav`** **— is** **`contentinfo`****\*\*\*\*.** Nest one inside an `<article>` and it silently loses its landmark role, which is exactly what you want for a card footer and exactly the bug you ship when you wrap your whole page footer in a `<section>` for styling. Rule of thumb: **there is one** **`contentinfo`** **per page, it lives as a direct child of** **`<body>`****\*\*\*\*, and everything else called a "footer" is just a closing block.**
* * *

## 2\. Why It Matters
The footer looks like dead space. It isn't — it's the page's safety net and its trust ledger.
**It's where intent-driven users go on purpose.** People who scroll to the footer are _looking for something_: your address, your phone number, your returns policy, your careers page, "contact us." Footer links have low volume but disproportionately high intent. Burying or breaking them costs conversions from your most motivated visitors.
**It carries legal and compliance weight.** Privacy policy, terms of service, cookie preferences, accessibility statement, and copyright are not decoration — in many jurisdictions they're required, and their absence is a liability. GDPR/CCPA cookie controls, an ADA/WCAG accessibility statement, business registration details: the footer is where the law lives on your site.
**It's a second-chance conversion surface.** The newsletter signup in the footer catches users who read to the bottom and are warm but not ready. Done right (real label, inline validation, clear success), it's one of the highest-ROI forms on the site. Done wrong (an unlabeled input and a naked "Submit"), it's an accessibility failure _and_ a leaky funnel.
**It sets the trust tone at the exit.** A rich, organized footer signals a real, established business; a thin `© 2026` with a broken social icon signals a side project. The footer is the last thing a hesitant buyer sees before they decide whether you're legit.
* * *

## 3\. Anatomy of a Footer
A well-built page footer is a composition of predictable regions. Not every footer uses all of them, but every one draws from this set:
**Brand block:** logo/wordmark, a one-line positioning statement, sometimes the tagline. Anchors identity at the exit.
**Navigation columns (the sitemap):** grouped link lists under headings — Product, Company, Resources, Legal. This is the "fat footer" core (see §6).
**Newsletter / email capture:** a labeled input + submit, with validation and a success state. The footer's one interactive form.
**Social links:** icon links to social profiles. Each is a link with an accessible name — never a bare icon.
**Contact block:** address (in an `<address>` element), phone, email, hours. High-intent info.
**Legal / compliance links:** Privacy Policy, Terms of Service, Cookie Preferences, Accessibility Statement, and often "Do Not Sell My Info" (CCPA). The non-negotiable row.
**Copyright / colophon:** `© 2026 Company` plus company registration, "made with," or credits. Usually the last line.
**Locale / region switcher:** language and currency selectors, common on international sites.
**Back-to-top control:** a link/button that returns to `#top`. On long pages it's a real usability win.
**Utility row:** app-store badges, trust seals, payment logos, certifications.

The structural spine underneath: one `<footer>` landmark → an optional grid of columns → one or more `<nav aria-label="…">` for the link groups → a legal row → the copyright line. Each _navigation_ group is a `<nav>` with its own accessible name; the link lists inside are real `<ul>`/`<li>` so screen readers announce "list, 6 items."
* * *

## 4\. Sizes, Density & Layout Scale
Footers scale from a single line to a five-column wall. Match the weight to the site.

| Type | Height (desktop) | Columns | Contents | Use |
| ---| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| Minimal bar | 48–72px | 0 (one row) | Copyright + 3–4 legal links | Landing pages, apps, focused funnels |
| Standard | 160–240px | 2–3 | Brand + a couple nav groups + legal | Small sites, portfolios |
| Fat footer | 320–480px | 4–6 | Full sitemap + newsletter + social + legal | Marketing sites, e-commerce, SaaS |
| Mega footer | 480px+ | 6+ | Fat footer + region switcher + app badges + trust seals | Enterprise, large retail, multi-brand |

Values that matter:
**Column gutters** land around 24–48px on desktop; tighten to a single stacked column under ~640px.
**Vertical rhythm:** group-heading to first-link gap ~12–16px; link-to-link ~8–12px. Cramped footers read as an afterthought.
**Touch targets stay ≥44px** even though footer links _look_ small and dense — expand hit area with padding, and don't crowd two links onto one tap zone on mobile.
**Max content width** should match the site's container (e.g. 1200–1440px) so the footer aligns with the body above it, not run bleedge-to-edge while the content is boxed.
**Contrast is a size concern too:** footers are usually dark, and the small, muted "secondary" link color is exactly where teams fail AA (see §10).
* * *

## 5\. States
A footer is mostly static, but the parts that _do_ have states are the ones teams forget.
**Link — default / hover / focus-visible / active / visited.** Footer links are small and often low-contrast; the hover and especially the **focus-visible ring** must be clearly visible against the dark background. Don't kill `:visited` styling if it helps orientation in a big sitemap.
**Newsletter input — empty / focused / typing / invalid / valid.** The input needs a visible focus state and an inline error state, not just a browser default.
**Newsletter submit — default / hover / focus / loading / disabled.** Same button discipline as anywhere: disable + spinner on submit, guard double-submit.
**Newsletter form — idle / submitting / success / error.** The whole form transitions: after a valid submit it shows "You're in — check your inbox," announced via `aria-live`. Error state ("That email doesn't look right" / "Something went wrong, try again") is announced too.
**Accordion column (mobile) — collapsed / expanded.** When columns collapse to accordions on small screens, each header is a real `<button aria-expanded>` toggling its panel.
**Back-to-top — hidden / visible / focused.** Often revealed only after the user scrolls down a viewport; must still be keyboard-reachable when shown.
**Region/locale switcher — closed / open / selected.** A menu or select with its own state.

The through-line: the footer as a container has no state, but it holds a form, a set of disclosure toggles, and a wall of links — and _those_ carry the full state matrix. Missing the newsletter's loading/success/error states is the #1 footer state bug.
* * *

## 6\. Types & Variants
Footers vary by information density and job, not by "emphasis" the way buttons do.
**Minimal / bar footer:** one line — copyright plus a few legal links. Right for apps, checkout flows, and focused landing pages where a fat footer would leak attention out of the funnel.
**Standard footer:** a brand block and two or three navigation groups over a legal row. The default for small-to-mid sites.
**Fat footer (the IA workhorse):** four to six columns of grouped links acting as a **secondary sitemap**. Born from a real finding — users started treating the footer as a navigation directory — the fat footer surfaces deep pages (support, careers, individual product lines, legal) that the header nav can't hold. It's the footer that does IA work, not just legal housekeeping. Pair it with a newsletter and social block and it becomes the site's utility hub.
**Mega footer:** a fat footer plus region/language switching, app-store badges, trust/security seals, and payment logos. Enterprise and large retail.
**CTA footer:** leads with a big "Ready to start?" call-to-action band _above_ the utility rows. Common on SaaS marketing sites — the footer does one more conversion push before the legal fine print.
**Sticky/pinned footer:** any of the above, but held to the bottom of the viewport on short pages via layout (the flexbox/grid technique in §9), or a persistent `position: fixed` utility bar (cookie consent, "sign up" nudge). The fixed variant is layout-only and must never trap focus or cover content.
**App/sectioning footer:** the non-landmark `<footer>` inside a card, article, or dialog — byline, timestamp, action row. Semantically a closing block, not `contentinfo`.
* * *

## 7\. When to Use One (and When Not To)
Use a full page footer on essentially **every content and marketing page** — it's where legal, contact, and secondary nav belong. But right-size it:
**Fat footer** when you have real IA depth to expose (e-commerce, SaaS, media, anything with careers/support/multiple product lines). It rewards the intent-driven scroller.
**Minimal bar** in focused funnels — checkout, signup, onboarding, a single landing page — where a wall of links would bleed users out of the flow. Keep only legal + copyright.
**No footer** inside truly app-like, full-height tool surfaces (a canvas editor, a map, a dashboard shell) where there's no "bottom of the page" — put legal/contact in a menu instead.

Don't:
**Don't dump the whole sitemap** into the footer as a dodge for bad primary navigation. The footer is a _secondary_ directory; if users can only find something in the footer, the header IA is broken.
**Don't put your only copy of critical actions in the footer.** Primary CTAs, essential nav — those live above too.
**Don't fix a fat footer to the viewport.** A 400px `position: fixed` footer eats the screen. Fixed is for slim bars only.

Placement heuristics: legal links go **last**, in a distinct row, in a consistent order across the site. The newsletter sits high in the footer where it's seen. Contact info is easy to find — often its own column. Back-to-top anchors the far corner.

## 8\. Footers Across Design Systems
Same region, different house rules. Knowing them makes you fluent in any codebase.
**Material Design (Google):** No single "footer" component — Material treats it as a layout region built from its type scale, list, and grid. Bottom app bars and navigation bars are _app chrome_, distinct from a web page's `contentinfo` footer. On the web, you compose it yourself with Material tokens.
**Apple HIG:** Native apps use tab bars and toolbars at the bottom, not a web footer. On the web ([apple.com](http://apple.com)), the footer is a dense fat-footer of grouped links with a fine-print legal block and region selector — restrained typography, generous spacing.
**Fluent (Microsoft):** Enterprise footers are utilitarian — link groups, language/region, "Your Privacy Choices" (CCPA), and a compact legal row. Subtle neutral surface.
**Ant Design / enterprise:** Ships a `Layout.Footer` — a plain styled container, usually a centered copyright line. Ant assumes admin apps, so the footer is minimal by default; you build the fat footer yourself for marketing pages.
**Bootstrap:** No dedicated footer component, but documents the footer pattern heavily — a `<footer>` with grid columns, and the classic **sticky-footer** example using flexbox (`min-height: 100vh` on the body, `margin-top: auto` on the footer). This is where a generation of devs learned the sticky-footer trick.
**Tailwind:** No prescribed footer; you compose it from utilities (`grid`, `gap`, `mt-auto`). Tailwind UI sells ready-made fat-footer blocks that codify the pattern.
**shadcn/Radix:** No `Footer` primitive (it's not an interactive widget), but the footer's _parts_ — the newsletter form, the mobile accordion, the region select — are built from shadcn's Form, Accordion, and Select. You own the layout; Radix owns the interactive bits' accessibility.

## 8b. Visual Styles (the same footer, eleven aesthetics)
Design systems are _whose_ rules; visual styles are _which look_. The same semantic footer — one `contentinfo` landmark, labeled nav, a real newsletter form — can wear any of these skins. Full guidance lives in the 🎨 Design Styles (visual languages) doc (Private ([https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-87871/838qa-200551](https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-87871/838qa-200551))).
**Flat:** solid dark band, crisp column rules, no shadow. Reads instantly, scales fast. The web default.
**Material:** subtle elevation separating footer from body, medium-emphasis link color, uppercase group headings.
**Glassmorphism:** translucent footer with `backdrop-filter: blur()` over a vivid page background. Gorgeous over imagery — but footer text is small and muted, so this is the #1 place glass fails contrast. Guard it.
**Liquid Glass (the 2026 trend):** Apple's iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe material — translucent, refractive, a specular rim and moving sheen. As a sticky utility bar it's stunning; as a full fat-footer it's overkill. Reserve for the slim pinned bar and honor `prefers-reduced-motion`.
**Neumorphism:** soft same-color extruded panels. Pretty, contrast-poor — a bad fit for a link-dense footer. Accent only.
**Skeuomorphism:** bevels and gradient fills, a "carved plate" footer. Retro; rarely right for a modern sitemap.
**Neo-Brutalism:** thick borders between columns, hard offset shadows, mono headings, zero radius. High personality — works for editorial/portfolio footers.
**Claymorphism:** big radius, soft inner light, puffy newsletter field. Friendly consumer brands.
**Aurora / Gradient:** an animated multi-hue gradient band closing the page. Premium — honor `prefers-reduced-motion` and keep text contrast on the darkest stop.
**Minimal / Swiss:** typography and a strict grid do everything; a hairline rule, generous whitespace, near-zero decoration. Ages well.
**UJG Brand:** Goldenrod headings and links over a deep Eminence/Night band, a warm glow on the newsletter field's focus ring, confident radius. The house default — the dark, saturated backdrop is exactly where a Liquid Glass _accent_ (the back-to-top pill, say) sings.

**Rule that never changes:** the style is skin. The footer's landmark semantics, the labeled `<nav>`s, the real form labels, the focus rings, and the 44px targets stay identical across all eleven. Change the paint, never the skeleton.

Key HTML rules:
**The page footer must not be nested** inside `<section>`, `<article>`, `<main>`, `<aside>`, or `<nav>`, or it stops being the `contentinfo` landmark. One `contentinfo` per page, as a child of `<body>`.
**Each link group is a** **`<nav>`** **with an accessible name** (`aria-labelledby` pointing at its heading, or `aria-label`). Multiple `<nav>`s on a page _must_ be distinguishable by name, or a screen-reader user hears "navigation, navigation, navigation."
**Links live in** **`<ul>`****/****`<li>`** so assistive tech announces the list and its length.
**Cookie Preferences is a** **`<button>`**, not a link — it opens a control, it doesn't navigate to a URL.
**Contact goes in** **`<address>`****\*\*\*\*.** That's what the element is for (contact info for the nearest article or the whole document).
**Social icons are decorative** (`aria-hidden="true"`); the accessible name comes from visually-hidden text or `aria-label` on the link.

### 9.2 CSS — the sticky footer, done two honest ways
"Sticky footer" is the layout trick that pins the footer to the bottom of the viewport **when the page content is too short to fill the screen**, while letting it sit naturally below the content on long pages. It is _not_ `position: sticky` and _not_ `position: fixed`. Two modern techniques:

**A. Flexbox (the Bootstrap classic).** Make the body a full-height column; let the main content grow to absorb the slack.

```css
html, body { height: 100%; }
body {
  min-height: 100vh;          /* fill the viewport at minimum */
  min-height: 100svh;         /* svh: avoids mobile browser-chrome jump */
  display: flex;
  flex-direction: column;
  margin: 0;
}
main { flex: 1 0 auto; }      /* grow to eat the empty space... */
.site-footer { flex-shrink: 0; }  /* ...so the footer is pushed to the bottom */
```

**B. Grid (fewer moving parts).** One row that takes all available space, one that's auto-sized. Cleaner if the layout is already grid.

```css
body {
  min-height: 100svh;
  display: grid;
  grid-template-rows: auto 1fr auto;   /* header, content(grows), footer */
  margin: 0;
}
/* header = auto, main = 1fr (absorbs slack), .site-footer = auto */
```

Both share the same idea: **give the middle region the leftover space** so a short page still pushes the footer to the bottom, and a long page lets it flow after the content. Prefer `svh`/`dvh` over `vh` — the old `100vh` overshoots on mobile because it counts the area behind the browser's collapsing toolbar, which shoves the footer down into a scroll.

**Distinct from** **`position: fixed`****\*\*\*\*.** A fixed footer is glued to the screen and always visible, overlapping content:

```css
/* A slim persistent utility/consent bar — NOT the page footer */
.consent-bar {
  position: fixed;
  inset-inline: 0;
  bottom: 0;
  /* Reserve space so it never covers page content or the real footer */
}
body { padding-bottom: 64px; }   /* height of the fixed bar */
```

Use fixed only for slim bars (cookie consent, an install nudge). Never fix a fat footer — it swallows the viewport and traps mobile users.

### 9.3 Responsive: columns → accordions on mobile
A five-column fat footer becomes a tall stack of links on a phone — a lot of scrolling. The pattern is to collapse each column into a **disclosure accordion**: the heading becomes a `<button aria-expanded>` that toggles its list. Semantics stay intact (still `<nav>`, still a list); only the visibility changes, and only below a breakpoint.

```html
<nav class="footer__col" aria-labelledby="f-company">
  <h2 class="footer__heading">
    <button type="button" aria-expanded="false" aria-controls="f-company-list"
            id="f-company" class="footer__toggle">
      Company
      <svg class="footer__chevron" aria-hidden="true"><!-- chevron --></svg>
    </button>
  </h2>
  <ul id="f-company-list" hidden>
    <li><a href="/about">About</a></li>
    <li><a href="/careers">Careers</a></li>
  </ul>
</nav>
```

```js
// Only wire the accordion below the breakpoint; above it, columns are always open.
```

const mq = window.matchMedia("(max-width: 640px)");

```js
function initFooterAccordion() {
  document.querySelectorAll(".footer__toggle").forEach((btn) => {
    const list = document.getElementById(btn.getAttribute("aria-controls"));
    const collapse = mq.matches;
    btn.setAttribute("aria-expanded", String(!collapse));
    list.hidden = collapse;
    btn.onclick = () => {
      const open = btn.getAttribute("aria-expanded") === "true";
      btn.setAttribute("aria-expanded", String(!open));
      list.hidden = open;
    };
  });
}
mq.addEventListener("change", initFooterAccordion);
initFooterAccordion();
```

Rules that keep this accessible: the toggle is a real `<button>` (keyboard + role for free), `aria-expanded` reflects state, `aria-controls` links button to panel, and above the breakpoint the panels are simply always visible (`aria-expanded="true"`, not hidden). Respect `prefers-reduced-motion` for the expand animation.

## 10\. Accessibility (non-negotiable)
The footer is a landmark-and-links region, and every part of it has an accessibility rule that teams routinely miss.
**One** **`contentinfo`** **per page.** The page footer must be a top-level `<footer>` (not nested in `article`/`section`/`main`/`aside`/`nav`). Screen-reader users navigate to it by landmark; two `contentinfo`s or zero both break that.
**Every footer** **`<nav>`** **needs a unique accessible name.** `aria-labelledby` on the heading, or `aria-label="Footer"` / `"Legal"` / `"Social media"`. Multiple unnamed `<nav>`s are announced identically — useless.
**Links in lists.** `<ul>`/`<li>` so AT announces "list, 6 items" and users can skim by count.
**Contrast is the #1 footer failure.** Dark footers pair muted grey links on near-black, and that "subtle secondary" color routinely lands at 3:1 or worse. Body/link text must clear **4.5:1** (WCAG AA); large headings **3:1**. Test the actual muted color, not the hover color.
**Visible focus ring on a dark field.** `outline: none` with no replacement strands keyboard users. Use a bright `:focus-visible` ring (Goldenrod on Night reads clearly).
**Icon links carry a name.** Social icons are `aria-hidden`; the link's name comes from visually-hidden text or `aria-label`. A bare `<a><svg></a>` is announced as "link" with no destination.
**The newsletter is a real form.** A `<label for>` tied to the input (placeholder is _not_ a label), `type="email"`, `autocomplete="email"`, errors via `aria-describedby` + `aria-invalid`, and results announced through an `aria-live="polite"` / `role="status"` region.
**Mobile accordions use real buttons.** Column toggles are `<button aria-expanded aria-controls>`, keyboard-operable, with the panel actually hidden (`hidden`) when collapsed.
**Cookie Preferences is a button, not a link** — it opens a control. Getting the element right gives correct role + keyboard behavior for free.
**`<address>`** **for contact info.** Semantic, and AT announces it as contact details.
**Target size ≥ 44px** even though footer links look tiny and dense. Pad them; don't stack two tap targets into one thumb zone.

## 11\. Innovative & Emerging Ideas
The footer looks frozen but keeps shifting:
**The footer as a command surface.** Site-search, an AI "ask this site" box, and quick actions moving into the footer so intent-driven scrollers get an answer without going back up.
**Server-rendered by default, islands only where needed.** The whole footer streams from the server (RSC); only the newsletter and region switcher hydrate. Less JS, faster paint.
**Consent-aware footers.** Cookie Preferences and "Your Privacy Choices" (CCPA/CPRA) rendered dynamically from the user's consent state, with a live "manage" entry point instead of a static link.
**View Transitions on sticky/pinned bars.** Native browser morphing when a fixed utility bar appears/dismisses, no heavy JS.
**Personalized sitemap columns.** The fat footer's link groups reordered by what the user actually uses (logged-in dashboards), turning dead space into a shortcut rail.
**Liquid-Glass utility bars.** The slim pinned bar (consent, "back to top," install nudge) rendered as Apple's 2026 refractive material — striking over a dark page, guarded for reduced-motion and contrast.
**Accessibility statement as a live widget.** Beyond a static page: an in-footer control that opens contrast/motion/text-size preferences, signaling real commitment.

## 12\. Conversion & Trust Killers (the silent ones)
None of these throw an error. They just quietly cost you the most motivated visitors — the ones who scrolled all the way down.
**The unlabeled newsletter.** A placeholder-only input and a naked "Submit" — no `<label>`, no validation, no success message. It fails screen readers _and_ leaves users unsure it worked, so they don't trust it. The footer's single form deserves real form discipline.
**Contrast so low the links vanish.** Grey-on-black at 3:1 reads as disabled. The highest-intent users can't find your contact link because it's the same color as the background. This is the most common footer bug, full stop.
**Missing legal links.** No privacy policy, no terms, no cookie control, no accessibility statement — a compliance liability _and_ a trust tell. A footer without these reads as unfinished or shady.
**The everything-drawer.** Dumping 60 links into the footer because the header IA is broken. The footer is a _secondary_ directory; if it's the only path to key pages, fix navigation, don't paper over it here.
**A fixed fat footer.** A 400px `position: fixed` footer that eats a third of a phone screen and can't be dismissed. Fixed is for slim bars only.
**Broken/decorative social icons.** Icon links with no accessible name (announced as "link, link, link") and no visible hover — or worse, links to dead accounts. Each social icon needs a name and a live destination.
**The** **`100vh`** **sticky-footer jump.** Using `100vh` instead of `svh`/`dvh` so the footer gets shoved below the fold on mobile by the browser toolbar, forcing a pointless scroll on a short page.
**No back-to-top on long pages.** Making users scroll a full sitemap's worth of footer back up to navigate. One anchor fixes it.

**The through-line:** footer mistakes fail _quietly_ — no crash, just a compliance gap, a screen-reader dead-end, and a slow leak of your highest-intent traffic. Audit the footer before you blame the funnel.

## 13\. Advanced Patterns
Techniques that lean on the platform and the type system instead of custom scripting.
**Sticky-footer with** **`svh`****/****`dvh`****, not** **`vh`****\*\*\*\*.** `min-height: 100svh` uses the _small_ viewport height (toolbar visible), so the footer never gets pushed under the mobile browser chrome. `dvh` (dynamic) tracks the toolbar as it collapses. This one-word change fixes the most common sticky-footer bug.
**Container queries for column collapse.** Instead of a viewport media query, use a container query so the footer reflows based on _its own_ width — correct even when it's placed in a narrow layout slot: `@container footer (max-width: 40rem) { … }`.
**Typed nav config, one source.** Drive header _and_ footer from one typed sitemap object so links never drift out of sync. `as const satisfies SiteNav` gives compile-time safety and autocompletion.
**Design tokens for the dark surface.** The footer usually inverts the page palette. Reference semantic tokens (`--surface-inverse`, `--text-on-inverse`, `--link-on-inverse`) so dark mode and rebrands are a token swap, and so the AA-safe link color is defined once, not eyeballed per footer.
**`content-visibility: auto`** **on a below-the-fold footer.** A long fat footer is off-screen at load; `content-visibility: auto` lets the browser skip rendering it until it's near the viewport, cutting initial layout cost.
**Progressive-enhancement newsletter.** A `<form action>` bound to a Server Action (§9.5) submits before JS hydrates — the footer's form works on the slowest connection.
**`aria-live`** **status shared by success and error.** One polite region for the newsletter result means one place to reason about announcements, and no double-announcing.
* * *

## 14\. Performance & Bundle Cost
The footer is on every page and mostly below the fold, so its cost should be near zero — but two traps make it heavy.
**The social-icon import trap.** Importing a whole icon library for five social glyphs, on every page. Use named/tree-shakeable imports (`import { Instagram } from "lucide-react"`) or, better, inline the five SVGs — they never change and don't need a library.
**Render it on the server.** The footer is static chrome; shipping it as a client component pays hydration cost on every page for something that never re-renders. Server-render the shell, hydrate only the newsletter/region-switcher islands.
**Defer the below-the-fold work.** `content-visibility: auto` and lazy-loading any footer imagery (payment logos, trust seals, app badges with `loading="lazy"`) keeps the footer out of the critical path.
**Don't animate layout.** A back-to-top reveal or accordion should animate `transform`/`opacity`, never `height`/`top` — footers are wide, and reflowing them each frame is expensive.
**One delegated listener for the link wall.** A fat footer can have 40+ links; if you're attaching analytics, use one delegated `data-track` listener on the footer, not 40 closures (see the Button doc's analytics pattern).

## 15\. Security
The footer's attack surface is its newsletter form and any user-influenceable content it renders. It's small but real.

**The newsletter endpoint is the primary target.** It accepts user input (an email address), writes to a database, and sends an email. That's a full state-changing endpoint that needs the same discipline as any form submission:
*   **Validate server-side.** Client regex is UX; the server's Zod/schema parse is the gate. Never trust the input.
*   **Rate-limit per IP.** Footer newsletter forms are the #1 spam-bot target on most marketing sites. Per-IP throttling (e.g., 5 requests/minute) stops drive-by abuse without a CAPTCHA.
*   **Honeypot field.** A hidden input that real users leave empty; bots fill it. Silently drop submissions where it's populated.
*   **Never leak membership.** Don't return "already subscribed" — that reveals whether an email is on your list (enumeration attack). Return a neutral success regardless.
*   **Double opt-in (GDPR).** Store the email as `pending`, send a confirm link. Only move to `active` on click. This proves consent and keeps your list clean.
*   **Idempotency.** Re-subscribing the same email must not create a duplicate row or send a second welcome email. Upsert or check-then-skip.
*   **CSRF protection.** The form submits a POST — use `SameSite` cookies, an anti-CSRF token, or bind it to a Server Action (which handles CSRF by construction in Next.js).

**Footer link content from a CMS.** If column headings or link labels are CMS-driven, render them as text (framework interpolation), never `innerHTML`. A stored-XSS payload in a footer link label runs on every page of the site because the footer is global.

**Social links and open redirects.** If social URLs are configurable (admin panel, CMS), validate they point at known social domains. An attacker who can inject an arbitrary URL into the footer's social row has a persistent phishing vector on every page.

**Cookie Preferences button.** The consent-management platform (CMP) script it triggers is a third-party dependency. Pin its version, audit its CSP compatibility, and ensure it doesn't inject tracking before consent is granted — a compliance violation that lives in the footer.

## 16\. Senior-Level Checklist (ship-ready)
Before a footer is "done":
Exactly one `contentinfo` landmark: page `<footer>` is a top-level element, not nested in `section`/`article`/`main`/`aside`/`nav`.
Every footer `<nav>` has a unique accessible name (`aria-labelledby` or `aria-label`).
Links live in `<ul>`/`<li>`; Cookie Preferences is a `<button>`; contact is in `<address>`.
Legal row present and consistent site-wide: Privacy, Terms, Cookie Preferences, Accessibility Statement (+ CCPA where required).
Link/text contrast ≥ 4.5:1 against the dark background — tested at the _muted_ color, not the hover.
Visible `:focus-visible` ring on the dark field; all links keyboard-reachable.
Social icons have accessible names; decorative SVGs are `aria-hidden`.
Newsletter is a real form: `<label for>`, `type="email"`, `autocomplete`, inline validation, `aria-live` success/error, disabled-on-submit.
Newsletter endpoint validates server-side, is idempotent, rate-limited, honeypot/double-opt-in.
Sticky-footer uses `min-height: 100svh`/`dvh` (not `vh`); footer sits at the bottom on short pages and below content on long ones.
A fat footer is NOT `position: fixed`; any fixed bar is slim and reserves its own space.
Columns collapse gracefully on mobile (single column or real `<button aria-expanded>` accordions).
Touch targets ≥ 44px; no two tap targets crammed into one thumb zone.
Rendered on the server where possible; icons tree-shaken/inlined.

## 16\. Visual Styles Reference

The rule bears repeating because footers are where it's most often violated: **the style is skin — the** **`contentinfo`** **landmark, the labeled navs, the real form labels, the AA contrast, the focus rings, and the 44px targets never change with the aesthetic.** Glassmorphism and Liquid Glass are the two skins most likely to break footer contrast (small muted text over a busy translucent surface); guard them hardest. Aurora and Liquid Glass need `prefers-reduced-motion` guards. UJG Brand — Goldenrod on deep Eminence/Night — is the house default and, not coincidentally, comfortably AA when the link color is the specified `#C9C4BC`\-and-up range rather than a dim grey.
