## 1\. What Bottom Tabs Actually Are
A bottom tab bar is a **persistent navigation control fixed to the bottom of a mobile screen that lets the user switch between the app's top-level destinations**, each represented by an icon and a label. Tap "Home," you're at Home. Tap "Search," you're at Search. It never scrolls away with the content; it's the app's spine.

Here is the distinction that separates a senior build from a junior one, and it trips up nearly everyone:

*   **Bottom Tabs = navigation (routing).** Each item is a _destination_ — a different URL/route/screen. Under the hood these are **links**, wrapped in a `<nav>`, and the current one is marked with `aria-current="page"`. Activating one changes _where you are_ in the app.
*   **Tabs (the in-page widget) = an ARIA** **`tablist`****\*\*\*\*.** Each tab shows a different _panel of content within the same view_, wired with `role="tab"`, `role="tabpanel"`, `aria-selected`, and arrow-key roving focus. Activating one swaps content _on the page you're already on_.

They look similar. They are semantically opposite. **Bottom Tabs are NOT the ARIA tabs widget.** If you slap `role="tablist"` / `role="tab"` on a bottom bar whose items navigate to different routes, you've lied to the screen reader — you've told it "these swap panels here" when they actually move the user across the app. This is the single most common bottom-tab accessibility bug, and it's invisible until someone on VoiceOver tries to use your app.

The clean mental model, three components people jam together:

*   **Bottom Tabs** — mobile-app bottom bar, 3–5 top-level _destinations_, icon + label, `<nav>` of links with `aria-current`. The mobile idiom specifically.
*   **Tab Navigation** — the _web_ routed-tab pattern (e.g. a settings page with `/settings/profile`, `/settings/billing` rendered as a horizontal tab strip). Also navigation, also links, but it's the desktop/web idiom and usually sits at the top of a content region.
*   **Tabs** — the in-page widget. `role="tablist"`. Content panels, not routes. Not navigation at all.

Bottom Tabs and Tab Navigation are cousins (both are `<nav>`s of links with `aria-current`); Tabs is the odd one out (an ARIA widget). Get this right and half the accessibility work is already done. Get it wrong and no amount of polish saves you.

One more distinction: Bottom Tabs vs. **Drawer/Hamburger navigation**. A drawer hides destinations behind a menu; the bottom bar keeps them permanently visible and one thumb-tap away. The bar wins for the 3–5 _most important_ destinations; the drawer absorbs the long tail. Many apps run both — bar for the core, drawer or a "More" tab for the rest.

## 2\. Why It Matters
The bottom tab bar is the most valuable real estate in a mobile app. It's on screen essentially always, in the exact spot the thumb rests. That gives it outsized weight:

*   **Discoverability of your whole product.** The bar is the app's table of contents. Whatever isn't in it (or in the drawer behind "More") effectively doesn't exist to most users — they never find it. Choosing the 3–5 items _is_ your information architecture, exposed. Put the wrong things there and engagement with your best features quietly craters.
*   **Thumb-reach ergonomics = the whole reason it's at the bottom.** On a modern 6.7" phone, the top of the screen is unreachable one-handed; the bottom third is the natural thumb zone. Primary navigation lives at the bottom because that's where thumbs are. Move it to the top (old Android convention) and you've added friction to every single navigation the user makes, all day.
*   **Orientation and confidence.** A persistent bar with a clearly highlighted active item answers "where am I?" at a glance, every second. Apps that hide navigation feel like a maze; apps with a steady, legible bar feel _solid_. That perceived solidity is trust, and trust is retention.
*   **It's an accessibility linchpin.** Screen-reader users navigate your app _through this bar_. If it's marked up as real navigation with a correct current-page state, they're oriented. If it's a pile of `<div onClick>`s or a mislabeled tablist, they're lost — and that's an ADA/WCAG exposure for anyone shipping to the public or to enterprise.

The bar is small. Its blast radius is the entire app.

## 3\. Anatomy of a Bottom Tab Bar
Every well-built bottom bar decomposes into predictable parts:

*   **Bar container:** the fixed strip itself. Full width, pinned to the bottom, with a background, a top hairline/elevation to separate it from content, and — critically — **bottom padding equal to the safe-area inset** so it clears the home indicator / notch (see §5 and §9).
*   **Tab item:** the tappable unit. One per destination. Contains an icon and a label stacked vertically (web/Material/Android) or side-by-side in some compact designs. The whole item is the hit target, not just the icon.
*   **Icon:** the glyph. Often has two visual states — outline when inactive, filled when active (the iOS/Material convention). Decorative to AT (`aria-hidden`) because the label carries the accessible name.
*   **Label:** the text under/beside the icon. **Present on the primary bar — do not ship icon-only** (see §7). One or two words, a noun for the destination ("Home," "Search," "Profile"), not a verb.
*   **Active indicator:** how the current destination is shown — color change, filled icon, a Material 3 "pill" behind the icon, an iOS tint. Must be more than color alone.
*   **Badge / notification dot:** an optional overlay on an item's icon signaling unread count or attention ("3", or a bare red dot). Needs its own accessible text.
*   **Safe-area inset region:** the padding zone below the items on notched/gesture devices. Part of the bar's background, not empty space — the bar's color must extend into it.
*   **"More" / overflow tab:** when destinations exceed five, the fifth slot becomes a "More" entry that opens the rest (a sheet, a menu, or a secondary screen).
* * *

## 4\. Sizes, Density & the 3–5 Item Rule
The defining constraint of this component is item count. This is not a style preference; it's an ergonomic and platform rule.

**The rule: 3 to 5 top-level items. Never more.** Both Apple's HIG and Google's Material spec cap it, for the same physical reason: a phone is ~360–430pt wide, a comfortable tap target is ≥44pt, and the label has to stay legible. Do the math and five is the ceiling before items get too narrow to tap accurately or too cramped to read.

*   **Fewer than 3:** you probably don't need a bar. Two destinations is a segmented control or a single toggle.
*   **3–5:** the sweet spot. Each item gets room to breathe and a legible label.
*   **More than 5:** the sixth destination doesn't shrink the others — it collapses the overflow into a **"More" tab**. The fifth slot opens a sheet/list containing everything that didn't make the cut. Never let the bar scroll horizontally (that hides destinations and kills the "everything's visible" promise) and never cram six tiny items in.

Real dimensional values:

| Token | iOS (HIG) | Material 3 (Android) | Web baseline | Notes |
| ---| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| Bar height (content) | 49pt | 80dp | 56–64px | Above the safe-area inset |
| Safe-area bottom | 34pt (notched) | gesture-dependent | `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)` | Added below content height |
| Icon size | 25–30pt | 24dp | 24–28px | Active may swap to filled variant |
| Label text | 10pt | 12sp | 11–12px | Medium weight, single line |
| Tab item min width | — | 48dp | ≥48px | Whole item is the target |
| Tap target | ≥44×44pt | ≥48×48dp | ≥44×44px | Non-negotiable; the label counts |
| Active indicator (M3) | tint only | 32dp pill height | — | Material 3 "pill" behind icon |

Rules that matter:

*   **The tap target is the whole item, top to bottom of the bar — including the label.** Don't make only the 24px icon tappable; that's a sub-44px target and a mis-tap generator.
*   **The bar height is content height** **_plus_** **the safe-area inset.** A 56px bar on a notched phone is really `56px + 34pt` tall. Forgetting the inset is the #1 bottom-tab bug (§5, §12).
*   **Labels stay one line.** If a label wraps or truncates, the word is too long or you have too many items. Rename or reduce.
*   **Even distribution.** Items are equal-width, evenly spaced. An off-balance bar reads as broken.

## 5\. States (every one, including the ones people skip)
A bottom bar has a richer state model than people expect, spanning the item, the bar, and the device environment.

**Per-item states:**

1. **Inactive (rest)** — the baseline for every non-current item. Muted color, usually an outline icon.
2. **Active / current** — _the_ item for the screen you're on. This is the star state: distinct color/tint, often a **filled** icon variant, sometimes a pill/indicator behind it, and — the part that matters for AT — `aria-current="page"` on the web. Exactly one item is active at a time.
3. **Pressed / active-touch** — the momentary feedback while the finger is down. Ripple on Material, subtle dim/scale on iOS. Sells the tap.
4. **Focus (keyboard / web PWA)** — reachable by Tab, shows a visible `:focus-visible` ring. Matters for PWAs and any web app rendered as an installed app.
5. **Badged** — carries a notification count or dot overlaid on the icon. Composes with active/inactive.
6. **Disabled** — rare and usually a smell for a bottom bar (a top-level destination shouldn't normally be unavailable), but occasionally used for gated features. Reduced opacity + `aria-disabled`; explain why nearby.

**Bar-level states:**

1. **Visible (default)** — pinned at the bottom, always there.
2. **Hidden / hide-on-scroll** — some apps slide the bar off-screen when the user scrolls down (to reclaim vertical space) and bring it back on scroll up. This is _contested_ — see the debate in §7. If you do it, animate it and make sure a scroll-up always restores it.
3. **Elevated / scrolled** — the bar gains a shadow or a stronger top divider once content scrolls beneath it, signaling layering.

**Environment states (the audit-catching ones):**

1. **Safe-area / notched** — on devices with a home indicator or gesture bar, the bar must pad its bottom by `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)` so items aren't overlapped by the indicator and the bar's background fills the inset. This is a _state of the device_, and it's where most bars break.
2. **Landscape / compact height** — in landscape the bar often shrinks (iOS uses a compact, label-beside-icon layout) to preserve vertical space.
3. **Reduced motion** — the hide-on-scroll slide, the pill-morph indicator, and any active-icon animation must be tamed under `prefers-reduced-motion`.
4. **Keyboard-open (mobile)** — when the on-screen keyboard appears, the bar should generally be _pushed off_ or hidden rather than floating above the keyboard. A bottom bar stranded mid-screen above a keyboard is a classic layout bug.

## 6\. Types & Variants
Same component, several flavors — chosen by platform, item count, and emphasis.

*   **iOS Tab Bar (HIG):** the classic. Icon above a small label, tint color for active, filled/outline icon swap. Translucent material background. This is `TabView` on SwiftUI. The reference idiom for the whole pattern.
*   **Material Bottom Navigation → Material 3 Navigation Bar:** Google's take. Material 3 renames it "Navigation Bar," taller (80dp), with a signature **active-indicator pill** behind the current icon and optional "labels only when active" mode. `NavigationBar` in Compose.
*   **Labeled (default) vs. label-when-active:** all labels always shown (best, most legible) vs. Material's option to show the label only on the active item to save space. The always-labeled version is safer for comprehension.
*   **3-item / 4-item / 5-item:** purely count. Four is comfortable; five is the max; odd counts (3, 5) let a center item feel primary.
*   **Center-action / FAB-docked bar:** a bottom bar with a prominent center button (often a `+` FAB) that's an _action_, not a destination — "Create," "Post," "Scan." The center item breaks the "all items are destinations" rule deliberately: it's a button, semantically, dropped into the middle of the nav. Instagram, Twitter/X-style.
*   **"More"-overflow bar:** four real destinations plus a fifth "More" that opens the long tail. The standard escape hatch past five.
*   **Badged bar:** any of the above with notification badges — the most common production addition.
*   **Floating / inset bar:** a bar that doesn't span edge-to-edge but floats as a rounded pill with a margin around it (a 2024–2026 aesthetic trend). Same semantics; different skin. Watch the safe area — the float still has to clear the home indicator.

## 7\. When to Use It (and When Not To)
Use a bottom tab bar when your app has **3–5 equally-important, frequently-visited, top-level destinations** that users switch between often, and you want them one thumb-tap away at all times.

Reach for something else when:

*   **You have more than five top-level destinations.** Use a **Drawer/hamburger** (absorbs a long list — see the _Drawer Navigation_ doc), or a bottom bar of the top four **plus a "More"** tab. Don't scroll the bar; don't cram six.
*   **You have only one or two destinations.** You don't need persistent nav. A single screen or a segmented control suffices.
*   **It's a desktop/web-first product.** The bottom bar is a _mobile_ idiom. On desktop, top nav or a sidebar reads more naturally; that's **Tab Navigation** / a nav rail territory (see the _Tab Navigation_ doc). (A responsive app often swaps a bottom bar on mobile for a sidebar on desktop.)
*   **The choices are content filters within one screen, not destinations.** That's the **Tabs** widget (an ARIA tablist), not navigation. If tapping it doesn't change the route, it isn't a bottom tab.
*   **The actions are tasks, not places.** "Share," "Delete," "Compose" are actions → a toolbar or FAB, not a nav tab. (The center-FAB pattern is the deliberate exception, and it's clearly _one_ action among destinations.)

**Icon + label — always. Never icon-only for the primary bar.** This is a hard rule, not a preference. Icons are ambiguous (whose "heart" means favorites vs. likes vs. health?), and icon-only bars measurably increase mis-navigation and slow first-time users. Nielsen Norman's research is blunt on it: **always pair the icon with a text label** on primary navigation. Icon-only is acceptable only for _universally understood_ single glyphs in secondary toolbars — never for the app's spine. The label is also what gives screen readers the destination name. Drop it and you've broken both comprehension and accessibility at once.

**The hide-on-scroll debate.** Sliding the bar away on scroll-down reclaims vertical space; it also hides the app's primary navigation, momentarily disorienting users and making destinations harder to reach. The senior position: **default to persistent.** Only hide-on-scroll in immersive, content-first contexts (a reading or media feed) where vertical space is genuinely scarce, and even then: animate it, restore it instantly on any upward scroll, and never let it get "stuck" hidden. If in doubt, keep it pinned — a stable bar is worth more than the ~56px you'd reclaim.

Placement heuristics:

*   **Bottom, edge-to-edge, thumb-reachable.** That's the entire point.
*   **Most-used destination often goes left** (first read) or center (thumb rest), with settings/profile typically rightmost.
*   **Keep the active state unmistakable** — a user should never have to think about which screen they're on.

## 8\. Bottom Tabs Across Design Systems
Same job, different house rules. Fluency here means you can drop into any codebase.

*   **Apple HIG (iOS/iPadOS):** the "Tab Bar." 3–5 items, icon + short label, tint for active, translucent material, filled/outline SF Symbol swap. On iPad it can migrate to a top tab bar or sidebar. `TabView` in SwiftUI. In iOS 26 the tab bar adopts **Liquid Glass** — a translucent, refractive floating bar (see §11).
*   **Material Design 3 (Google):** the "Navigation Bar" (formerly "Bottom Navigation"). 3–5 destinations, 80dp tall, the active-indicator **pill**, optional label-when-active. Ripple on press. `NavigationBar` + `NavigationBarItem` in Compose; `NavigationBar` in Flutter's Material 3. Material also defines a **Navigation Rail** (side) for tablets and a **Navigation Drawer** for the long tail — a documented progression by screen size.
*   **Fluent (Microsoft):** less of a phone-bottom-bar culture (Windows-first), but Fluent's mobile guidance mirrors the platform bar it runs on; on the web it favors a top command bar / nav pane rather than a bottom bar.
*   **Ant Design / enterprise:** Ant Design Mobile ships a `TabBar` component — icon + title, badges, 3–5 items — squarely for admin/mobile web. Enterprise desktop UIs skip it for sidebars.
*   **Tailwind / utility-first:** no prescribed component; you compose a `<nav>` of links with utilities and handle the safe area with `pb-[env(safe-area-inset-bottom)]`. Freedom plus the responsibility to get semantics right yourself.
*   **shadcn/Radix:** no dedicated "bottom tabs" primitive (Radix's `Tabs` is the _in-page_ widget — do **not** use it for routed bottom nav). You build the bar as a `<nav>` of `next/link`s (or your router's links) with `aria-current`, optionally styling with the same tokens shadcn uses. This absence is itself the lesson: the ecosystem's "Tabs" primitive is the wrong tool for this job.
*   **React Native / Expo:** first-class. `@react-navigation/bottom-tabs` and **Expo Router's** **`Tabs`** are the standard; they render the platform-correct bar and handle the safe area for you (see §9).

The through-line: **every platform gives you a bottom bar of 3–5 destinations with an icon, a label, an active tint, and safe-area handling.** Learn the concept once; remap the API per platform.

## 9\. The Code
The biggest section. The bar is _navigation_, so every snippet below is built as links with a current-page state — never as an ARIA tablist. Web first, then the native platforms where this component truly lives.

### 9.1 HTML (the honest foundation)
A bottom tab bar is a landmark `<nav>` containing a list of links. The current destination is marked with `aria-current="page"`. That's the whole semantic contract.

```plain
<!-- Primary mobile navigation: a nav landmark of links, NOT a tablist -->
<nav class="tabbar" aria-label="Primary">
  <ul class="tabbar__list">
    <li>
      <a class="tabbar__item" href="/home" aria-current="page">
        <svg class="tabbar__icon" aria-hidden="true"><!-- home glyph --></svg>
        <span class="tabbar__label">Home</span>
      </a>
    </li>
    <li>
      <a class="tabbar__item" href="/search">
        <svg class="tabbar__icon" aria-hidden="true"><!-- search glyph --></svg>
        <span class="tabbar__label">Search</span>
      </a>
    </li>
    <li>
      <a class="tabbar__item" href="/alerts">
        <svg class="tabbar__icon" aria-hidden="true"><!-- bell glyph --></svg>
        <span class="tabbar__label">Alerts</span>
        <!-- Badge with its own accessible text -->
        <span class="tabbar__badge" aria-hidden="true">3</span>
        <span class="sr-only">, 3 unread</span>
      </a>
    </li>
    <li>
      <a class="tabbar__item" href="/profile">
        <svg class="tabbar__icon" aria-hidden="true"><!-- person glyph --></svg>
        <span class="tabbar__label">Profile</span>
      </a>
    </li>
  </ul>
</nav>
```

Key HTML rules:

*   **It's a** **`<nav aria-label="Primary">`****, not** **`role="tablist"`****\*\*\*\*.** Each item is an `<a href>` because it navigates to a route. The label distinguishes it from other nav landmarks (a header nav, a footer nav).
*   **`aria-current="page"`** marks the active destination — the _correct_ AT signal for "you are here," and semantically right (unlike `aria-selected`, which belongs to tabs).
*   **The label is real text**, not baked into the icon. The icon is `aria-hidden` because the label already names the link.
*   **Badges get accessible text.** A visual "3" means nothing to a screen reader; pair it with visually-hidden context (", 3 unread").

Why this is the senior version:

*   **Active state is** **_derived_** **from the route**, not stored in local state. The URL is the single source of truth for "where am I" — the bar just reflects it. That's what makes it real navigation.
*   **`aria-current="page"`**, not `aria-selected`. Correct semantics for a nav landmark.
*   **`startsWith(href + "/")`** so a nested route (`/search/results`) still highlights its parent tab. The most common "why isn't my tab highlighting" bug.
*   **A dev-only guard** nudges you back under the 3–5 rule instead of silently rendering a broken seven-item bar.
*   **Badge caps at "99+"** — an unbounded number breaks the layout.
*   **Filled/outline icon swap** keyed off `active`, matching the platform convention.

## 10\. Accessibility (non-negotiable)
The bar is navigation. Mark it as navigation. This is the section the whole component hinges on.

*   **Landmark:** wrap it in `<nav>` with a distinguishing `aria-label` (e.g. "Primary"). One nav landmark per navigation region; the label lets AT users jump straight to it.
*   **Links, not tabs:** each item is an `<a href>` (web) / a navigation destination (native). **Do not** use `role="tab"` / `role="tablist"` / `aria-selected` — those describe an in-page panel switcher, which this is not. Using them tells the screen reader the wrong story about what activation does.
*   **Current state:** the active item gets `aria-current="page"` (web). Native frameworks expose "selected" through their own accessibility APIs (`TabView`, `NavigationBar`) — let them; don't hand-roll it.
*   **Labels are mandatory:** every item has a visible text label _and_ that label is its accessible name. Icon-only bottom tabs fail both comprehension and AT. Decorative icons get `aria-hidden`/`contentDescription = null` so they aren't double-announced.
*   **Badges:** a visual count needs text. "Alerts, 3 unread" via visually-hidden text or the platform badge API — never a bare colored dot with no accessible equivalent.
*   **Keyboard (web/PWA):** every item is Tab-reachable in order and Enter-activates (native `<a>` behavior — free if you used real links). Unlike the Tabs widget, a nav bar does **not** use arrow-key roving focus; it's a normal sequence of links.
*   **Touch target ≥ 44×44pt / 48×48dp:** the _entire_ item, label included, is the target. Never just the 24px icon.
*   **Contrast:** active and inactive item colors both meet ≥4.5:1 against the bar, and active is signaled by more than color (filled icon / indicator), satisfying WCAG 1.4.1.
*   **Reduced motion:** hide-on-scroll slides, indicator-pill morphs, and icon animations respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
*   **Reachability:** the bottom placement is itself an accessibility feature — it puts primary nav in the one-handed thumb zone. Don't undermine it by floating the bar somewhere unreachable.

## 11\. Innovative & Emerging Ideas (2026-aware)
The bottom bar looks settled but is quietly evolving:

*   **Liquid Glass bars (Apple, iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe):** the tab bar is now a translucent, _refractive_ floating element that bends the content scrolling beneath it, with a specular rim and a subtle live sheen — glassmorphism grown up. SwiftUI's `TabView` adopts it automatically; on the web you approximate with `backdrop-filter`, layered highlights, and a floating inset bar. Contrast and reduced-motion caveats still bind.
*   **Morphing / adaptive bars:** the bar that transforms with context — collapsing to fewer items on scroll, expanding a search field inline, or morphing the center item into an action. Apple's iOS 26 tab bar can minimize on scroll and surface a search role.
*   **Material 3 Expressive navigation:** springier active-indicator transitions, bolder shape morphing on selection, larger emotional range in the pill animation.
*   **Floating / detached bars:** the edge-to-edge bar giving way to a rounded, inset "pill" that floats above content with a margin — a dominant 2024–2026 aesthetic. Same semantics, new silhouette; still must clear the safe area.
*   **Responsive nav that** **_becomes_** **a rail/sidebar:** one component that renders as a bottom bar on phones, a navigation rail on tablets, and a sidebar on desktop — Material's documented progression, increasingly automated by frameworks.
*   **Haptic + spatial feedback:** a tab switch fires a subtle haptic (native) and, on spatial/visionOS-influenced UIs, a soft depth transition rather than an instant swap.
*   **Contextual / AI-surfaced tabs:** an item that adapts to predicted intent, or a "for you"/agentic entry that streams rather than routes. Handle with care — a bar whose destinations _move_ undermines the muscle memory that makes the pattern valuable.

## 12\. Conversion & UX Killers (the silent bleeders)
None of these throw an error. They just quietly erode navigation, comprehension, and trust.

*   **Icon-only bars.** The classic. Users can't decode ambiguous glyphs, mis-navigate, and slow down — and screen readers get nothing. _Always pair icon + label._
*   **Too many items.** Six, seven, eight tiny tabs: cramped targets, unreadable labels, mis-taps. Cap at five; overflow into "More."
*   **Forgetting the safe area.** The single most common bottom-tab bug: labels eaten by the home indicator, or the bar floating under the gesture bar. `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)` + `viewport-fit=cover`. On every notched device this is glaringly broken and instantly reads as "cheap app."
*   **Content hidden behind the bar.** No bottom padding on the scroll container, so the last item/CTA sits _under_ the fixed bar and can't be tapped. Pad `main` by bar height + safe area.
*   **Ambiguous or missing active state.** If the user can't tell which tab they're on at a glance, the bar's core job (orientation) fails. Make active unmistakable and never color-only.
*   **Wrong semantics (tablist instead of nav).** Invisible in the UI, catastrophic for AT users, and it drifts you toward arrow-key focus behavior that fights the platform. Nav of links with `aria-current`, full stop.
*   **Nested routes that de-highlight the parent.** Drill into `/search/results` and suddenly no tab is active — the user feels unmoored. Match on the route _prefix_.
*   **Overusing hide-on-scroll.** Reclaiming 56px by hiding the app's spine trades a tiny space win for real disorientation. Default to persistent; hide only in genuinely immersive views, and restore instantly.
*   **Actions masquerading as destinations.** Putting "Share" or "Compose" as a nav tab confuses "places" with "tasks." If it's an action, it's a button/FAB, marked as such — not a fifth destination.
*   **Bar stranded above the keyboard.** On mobile web especially, a `position: fixed` bar can float mid-screen above the on-screen keyboard. Hide or push it when the keyboard opens.

**The through-line:** these fail _quietly_. No crash, just users who can't find features, mis-tap, or feel lost — and blame "the app" without knowing why. Audit the bar before you audit the funnel.

## 13\. Advanced Patterns
The senior-move versions of the basics.

**Per-tab navigation stacks (state preservation).** Each tab should remember its own scroll position and drill-down history. Tap Search → open a result → switch to Home → come back to Search: you should land exactly where you left, not reset to the top. React Navigation and native `TabView`/`NavigationBar` preserve per-tab stacks by default; on the web you replicate it with per-route scroll restoration and cached route state. Losing tab state is one of the most-felt "this app feels cheap" bugs.

**Re-tap to pop-to-root / scroll-to-top.** Tapping the _already-active_ tab should scroll its content to the top (or pop its stack to the root). It's an unwritten platform convention users rely on without articulating it — implement it and the app feels native.

**The "More" overflow, done right.** Past five destinations, the fifth slot is "More," opening a sheet or list of the remainder. Keep the _same four_ primary tabs stable across the whole app; only the "More" contents vary. Stability of the primary four is what builds muscle memory.

**Center-action / docked FAB.** A center item that's an _action_, not a destination, is legitimate — but mark it as a `<button>` (web) / action, not a nav link, and give it a distinct visual weight so users read it as "do" not "go."

**Design tokens + cross-platform sync.** Drive the bar's colors, active tint, indicator, and heights from tokens (primitive → semantic → component), so the web bar, the SwiftUI bar, and the Compose bar share one Goldenrod-on-Night palette exported once via Style Dictionary — no hand-copied hex across platforms.

**TS-enforced item count.** A tuple type can _require_ 3–5 items at compile time, catching an over-stuffed bar before runtime:

```plain
type Tab = { href: string; label: string; icon: React.ReactNode };
// Enforce 3–5 items structurally
type Tabs =
  | [Tab, Tab, Tab]
  | [Tab, Tab, Tab, Tab]
```

| \[Tab, Tab, Tab, Tab, Tab\];

```plain
function BottomTabs(props: { items: Tabs }) { /* ... */ }
// A sixth item now fails to typecheck — the rule is enforced, not remembered.
```

**Responsive escalation.** One nav model, three renders: bottom bar (phone) → navigation rail (tablet) → sidebar (desktop). Share the same item list and active-route logic; swap only the presentation at breakpoints.

## 14\. Performance & Bundle Cost
The bar is cheap to render but easy to make expensive.

*   **The icon-import trap.** Importing an entire icon set for four glyphs is the #1 bloat source. `import { Home, Search } from "lucide-react"` (tree-shakeable), never `import * as Icons`.
*   **Prefetch destinations, not everything.** Warm the 3–5 tab targets (`next/link prefetch`, route preloading) so switches feel instant — but don't eagerly load _all_ screen data on mount; lazy-load each tab's payload on first visit, then cache.
*   **Keep tab state alive, but bounded.** Preserving per-tab stacks is great UX; keeping _every_ visited screen mounted forever is a memory leak. Keep the active tab and recently-used ones warm; reclaim the rest.
*   **Animate only** **`transform`****/****`opacity`****\*\*\*\*.** The active-indicator pill and any hide-on-scroll slide must use compositor-only properties — never animate `height`/`bottom`, which reflow every frame.
*   **One component, rendered once.** The bar lives in the layout and renders a single time across page changes; only the active-state derivation re-runs. Don't remount the whole bar on every navigation.

## 15\. Security
Bottom tabs are navigation, not a form or endpoint, but they touch real security surfaces because each tab is a route the server renders.

**The tab bar is not an authorization boundary.** Hiding a tab (e.g. "Admin") from non-admins is UX. The route behind it must independently re-check auth on every request. A user can type the URL, deep-link, or hit the API directly regardless of whether the tab rendered for them.

**Don't leak gated destinations in the client bundle.** If your tab config includes admin-only routes and ships to every client, you've leaked route structure. Filter the config server-side before it reaches the browser, or accept that route _existence_ is public and lock down the routes themselves.

**Badge counts are data.** An "Inbox 3" badge fetches from an API. That endpoint must authorize per-user, never leak counts across tenants, and respect rate-limits. A badge that shows another user's unread count is a data leak living in chrome.

**Deep-link params are attacker-controllable.** A shared link like `/alerts?id=xyz` carries IDs the user can edit. The server must validate ownership, not trust the param because it came from your tab link.

**Open-redirect via returnTo params.** If any tab destination carries a `?returnTo=` or `?next=` query param, validate it against your own domain server-side before redirecting. An unvalidated redirect in a nav link is a phishing vector on every screen of the app.

**Clickjacking (web PWAs).** A bottom bar with a high-value action (the center FAB "Buy Now") can be targeted by a transparent iframe overlay. Defend with `Content-Security-Policy: frame-ancestors 'none'` at the HTTP level.

- [ ] Tap target ≥44×44pt / 48×48dp across the whole item; contrast ≥4.5:1.
- [ ] Badges have accessible text; counts cap at "99+".
- [ ] Keyboard-reachable (PWA) with a visible focus ring; no fake roving-tabindex.
- [ ] Per-tab state/scroll preserved; re-tap pops-to-root / scrolls-to-top.
- [ ] Hide-on-scroll (if used) animates, respects reduced motion, and restores instantly.
- [ ] Bar hides/pushes when the mobile keyboard opens; doesn't float mid-screen.

## 17\. Visual Styles (the same bar, eleven skins)
Design systems are _whose_ rules; visual styles are _which look_. The same semantic bottom bar — `<nav>` of links, `aria-current`, ≥44px targets, safe-area padding — can wear any of these. **Style is skin; behavior is the skeleton.** None of the semantics, keyboard behavior, focus ring, target size, or safe-area handling changes across any style below.

*   **Flat:** solid Night bar, crisp top hairline, no shadow, flat icons. Active = a color swap. Reads instantly, scales everywhere. The safe default.
*   **Material:** the M3 Navigation Bar — 80dp, tonal surface, the active-indicator **pill** behind the current icon, ripple on tap, label-when-active option.
*   **Glassmorphism:** translucent bar with `backdrop-filter: blur()` over vivid content. Looks premium; guard contrast hard — muted labels on a blurred backdrop fail AA fast.
*   **Liquid Glass (the 2026 trend):** Apple's iOS 26 language — a floating, refractive translucent bar that bends the content beneath it, with a specular rim and live sheen. Native via SwiftUI `TabView`; an honest approximation on the web. Guard for contrast _and_ `prefers-reduced-motion`.
*   **Neumorphism:** the bar and items as soft same-color extrusions with dual light/dark shadows. Pretty, contrast-poor — accent only, never the whole primary bar unless you fix the active-state contrast.
*   **Skeuomorphism:** a tactile "tray" bar with bevels, inner highlights, and a subtly recessed active slot. The old iOS 6 dock feel.
*   **Neo-Brutalism:** hard black top border, zero radius, a thick offset shadow under the bar, an unmistakable boxed active item. High personality, loud.
*   **Claymorphism:** puffy rounded floating bar, soft inner top-light and bottom-shadow, big radius, gentle glow. Friendly, app-store-cute.
*   **Aurora / Gradient:** animated multi-hue gradient bar or active indicator on a dark field. Premium; honor `prefers-reduced-motion` (kill the animation) and keep label contrast.
*   **Minimal / Swiss:** near-invisible bar — hairline divider, monochrome icons, typographic labels doing the work, active shown by weight/opacity. Restraint as the aesthetic.
*   **UJG Brand:** Afro-Futurist house style — muted Platinum icons on a deep **Night** (`#0A0A0A`) bar, the active item in **Goldenrod** (`#DCA424`) with a filled icon and a soft warm glow, an optional **Eminence** (`#5F2C82`) indicator pill (Material mode) or **Dark Green** (`#042D1D`) recess, badges in **Spanish Orange** (`#E86100`). Confident radius on the floating variant. The refraction of a Liquid Glass bar sings over the dark Eminence/Night backdrop — lean there for hero apps, keep Flat/Swiss for dense/utility screens.

**Rule that never changes:** the bar is navigation in every skin. Guard glass / liquid-glass / neumorphism for contrast; guard aurora / liquid-glass for reduced motion; keep the 44px target and the safe-area inset no matter how on-trend the surface. Full guidance in the Private ([https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-87871/838qa-200551](https://app.clickup.com/8495850/docs/838qa-87871/838qa-200551)).
* * *

**Bottom line:** the bottom tab bar is the smallest surface with the biggest orientation job in a mobile app. Build it as what it actually is — a `<nav>` of links with `aria-current`, 3–5 destinations, icon _and_ label, honoring the safe area and the thumb zone — and resist the one universal temptation to mark it up as an ARIA tablist. Get those bones right and every platform, from SwiftUI's `TabView` to Expo Router's `Tabs`, is just a different syntax for the same correct idea. Get them wrong and you've broken the one control your users touch on every single screen.
