## 1\. What a Hero Actually Is
A hero is the large, first-viewport section of a page whose job is to say — in about three seconds — _what this is, who it's for, and what to do next._ It's the handshake. Everything after it is the conversation, but only if the handshake lands.

The word gets stretched over several things people conflate:
*   **Hero (marketing):** the above-the-fold pitch. Headline + subhead + one primary CTA + supporting media. This is what we mean by "hero" 95% of the time and what this doc is about.
*   **Banner / announcement bar:** a thin strip ("Free shipping over $50," "We raised a round"). Informational, not the page's thesis. Not a hero.
*   **Masthead / page header:** the top region of _any_ page (logo + nav). The hero usually sits _below_ the masthead, inside the same first viewport.
*   **Carousel / slider hero:** multiple heroes rotating. Almost always a mistake (see §7 and §12) — it dilutes the single message and tanks LCP.
*   **Cover / splash:** a full-bleed image with minimal text, common in editorial. A hero with the pitch dialed down and the mood dialed up.

The single most common conceptual mistake: **treating the hero as decoration instead of a decision.** A hero is not "the pretty picture at the top." It's a conversion instrument with one headline, one primary action, and one job. If you can't say what action the hero is driving, you don't have a hero — you have a screensaver. Rule of thumb: **a hero is a promise plus a next step; if either is missing, it isn't doing its job.**

---

## 2\. Why It's So Important
The hero carries more weight per pixel than anything else you'll build.
*   **It's the bounce line.** Visitors decide in seconds whether to stay. A muddy value proposition or a slow-painting hero and they're gone before they've read a word of your real content. The hero converts the visitor or loses them — there's rarely a second chance.
*   **It's almost always your Largest Contentful Paint element.** The big headline or the background image in the hero is, by definition, the largest thing painted in the first viewport. That makes the hero _the_ performance section of the page — Google's LCP Core Web Vital is, in practice, "how fast does your hero show up?" Get this wrong and you pay in rankings and in bounce. This is the through-line of the whole doc (see §9.5 and §14).
*   **It sets the trust tone.** Text you can't read over a busy photo, a video that autoplays with sound, a headline that reflows and shoves the CTA down half a second after load — each one reads as _amateur_ and quietly erodes confidence before the visitor reaches your actual offer.
*   **Conversion lives here.** The hero's headline and primary CTA are among the most A/B-tested elements in existence. The gap between "Powerful analytics for teams" and "See your revenue in 60 seconds" is measured in signups.

For UJG specifically: the hero is where the Afro-Futurist promise either feels premium and intentional or feels like a template. It's the first and loudest brand impression.

## 3\. Anatomy of a Hero
Every well-built hero is composed of a predictable set of parts, layered front to back:
*   **Eyebrow (kicker):** a short label above the headline ("New," a category, "For logistics teams"). Optional. Sets context in ~2–4 words. Should _not_ be an `<h2>` sitting above your `<h1>` — use a `<p>` or a styled span so heading order stays clean.
*   **H1 headline:** the single most important line on the page. The value proposition in one breath. **Exactly one** **`<h1>`** **per page**, and on a landing page it almost always lives here.
*   **Subhead (dek):** one or two supporting sentences that pay off the headline's promise and add the specifics the headline was too short to carry.
*   **Primary CTA:** the one action you most want. Filled, high-contrast, verb-first ("Start free," "Book a call"). See the Button reference for the button itself.
*   **Secondary CTA (optional):** a lower-emphasis alternative ("Watch demo," "See pricing") as a ghost/outline button or text link. Never a second _primary_.
*   **Supporting media:** the background or side image, video, gradient, or product screenshot. Carries mood and context. This is usually the LCP element (see §9).
*   **Scrim / overlay:** the semi-transparent layer between media and text that guarantees legibility. The part juniors forget (see §5 and §9.3).
*   **Trust signals:** logos ("Trusted by…"), a rating, a stat ("10,000+ shipments tracked"), or a short testimonial. Reduces the risk of committing to the CTA.
*   **Scroll cue (optional):** a chevron or "Scroll" hint on tall full-viewport heroes so users know there's more below.
*   **Landmark container:** the semantic wrapper. A hero is a `<section>` (often with an `aria-labelledby` pointing at the H1), _inside_ `<main>` — not the page's `<header>` (that's the masthead/nav).

## 4\. Sizes & Scale
Heroes scale by intent and viewport. Height is the first decision and the most abused one.

| Hero height | Value | Reads as | Use |
| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| Compact | 40–60vh / ~360–520px | Efficient, content-forward | Docs, apps, blog landing, returning users |
| Standard | 60–80vh | Balanced pitch + peek of content below | Most marketing pages |
| Full-viewport | 100svh | Immersive, brand-first | Campaigns, launches, portfolios |
| Oversized | \> 100vh | Editorial / scroll-driven | Story pages, agencies — use sparingly |

Type scale inside the hero (fluid, `clamp()`\-driven):

| Element | Mobile | Desktop | Notes |
| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| Eyebrow | 12–13px | 14px | Uppercase, tracked |
| H1 | 28–34px | 48–72px | The showpiece; `clamp()` between them |
| Subhead | 16px | 18–20px | Max ~60ch measure for readability |
| CTA | 16px | 16–18px | ≥44px tap target, always |

Rules that matter:
*   **Use** **`100svh`****, not** **`100vh`****, for full-viewport heroes.** `vh` on mobile includes the area behind the browser's collapsing toolbar, so a `100vh` hero gets _clipped_ by the address bar on load and jumps when it retracts. `svh` (small viewport height) is measured with the toolbar shown — no clip, no jump. `dvh` (dynamic) resizes live and can cause layout thrash; `svh` is the safe default.
*   **Fluid type with** **`clamp()`** so the H1 scales smoothly between phone and desktop instead of snapping at breakpoints: `font-size: clamp(2rem, 6vw + 1rem, 4.5rem);`.
*   **Cap the text measure** at ~60 characters even when the hero is full-bleed. A headline that runs the full width of a 1440px monitor is unreadable.
*   **Reserve the media's box** with `aspect-ratio` so the hero doesn't reflow when the image loads (see §9.5 — this is a Cumulative Layout Shift fix _and_ protects your LCP).

## 5\. States (the part juniors forget)
A hero isn't as stateful as a button, but "it just sits there" is exactly the assumption that ships broken heroes. The states here are mostly about _loading, media, and environment._
1. **Initial paint (the one that's graded).** Before anything else, the browser paints the hero. Your entire job is to make the H1 and the media appear as fast as possible. This is the LCP moment — see §9.5. If the headline is a web font that hasn't loaded, you get invisible text (FOIT) or a swap-jump (FOUT); if the image lazy-loads, LCP tanks.
2. **Media loading.** The background image/video is still fetching. The box must already be reserved (`aspect-ratio` + a solid or gradient placeholder color, or a blurred LQIP) so text is readable _immediately_ and nothing shifts when the media arrives.
3. **Media loaded.** Full-quality media in place. For video, this is when playback starts (muted).
4. **Media failed.** The image 404s or the video can't play. The hero must _still_ be legible and on-brand — a gradient or solid brand-color fallback under the text, never white-text-on-white-nothing. Set a `background-color` on the media container as the floor.
5. **Reduced motion.** User has `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`. Video heroes must _not_ autoplay; show the poster frame instead. Aurora/animated-gradient heroes freeze. (See §9.4.)
6. **Reduced data / slow connection.** On `prefers-reduced-data` or a `2g` effective connection, skip the video entirely and serve the poster image. Don't push 8MB of hero video down a metered phone connection.
7. **Small viewport / art-direction.** On mobile, a wide cinematic crop becomes an unreadable sliver. The hero swaps to a portrait/square crop via `<picture>` (see §9.6). This is a _state_ of the same hero, not a different component.
8. **Scrolled / sticky variants.** Some heroes pin, parallax, or hand off to a compact sticky header as you scroll past. Optional and easy to overdo.

Missing states four, five, and six are the difference between a hero that _demos_ well and one that survives real users on real devices.

## 6\. Types & Variants
Heroes vary along two axes: **layout** and **media treatment.**

**By layout:**
*   **Centered:** headline, subhead, CTA stacked and centered over a full-bleed background. The default for campaigns and brand moments. Maximum focus, minimum nuance.
*   **Split (50/50):** copy on one side, media on the other. The SaaS standard — lets you show a product screenshot at full fidelity next to a readable, high-contrast text column (no scrim needed). Reads left-to-right into the CTA.
*   **Asymmetric / off-grid:** text weighted to one third, media bleeding off the opposite edge. More editorial and expressive; harder to keep balanced and responsive.
*   **Text-only:** no media at all — big type, generous space, maybe a gradient. Fastest possible LCP, very premium when the type does the work. The headline _is_ the LCP element here.
*   **Stacked (mobile-first):** copy on top, media below, in a single column. What almost every layout collapses to on a phone anyway.

**By media treatment:**
*   **Background image:** classic full-bleed photo behind text. Needs a scrim (see §9.3). The image is your LCP element — optimize ruthlessly.
*   **Background video:** looped, muted, autoplaying clip for atmosphere. Powerful and _expensive_ — see §9.4 for the poster/reduced-motion/mobile-data rules. The poster image (not the video) should be your LCP.
*   **Product / screenshot hero:** the actual UI shown in a split or framed layout. Highest-converting for SaaS because it shows the thing.
*   **Gradient / illustrative:** a CSS gradient or vector art. Near-zero weight, infinitely crisp, never a legibility problem, no image request at all. UJG's Eminence-to-Night gradients live here.
*   **3D / interactive:** WebGL, Spline, canvas. Stunning, heavy, and a serious LCP and battery liability. Reserve for launches, and always ship a static fallback.

## 7\. When to Use One (and When Not To)
Use a big hero when the page has **one primary message and one primary action** — landing pages, product pages, campaigns, portfolios, home pages. That's most marketing surfaces.

Don't reach for a full hero when:
*   **The page is a tool or a dashboard.** An app's home screen wants the user _working_, not admiring a photo. A compact header or none at all beats a 100vh brand moment that pushes the actual UI below the fold.
*   **The content is the point** (docs, a blog post, search results). A giant hero is a wall between the user and what they came for.
*   **You have three equally-important messages.** That's the impulse behind the rotating carousel hero — and it's the wrong answer. Auto-rotating hero sliders test _worse_: users rarely see slide 2+, each slide fights the others, and every slide's image loads, wrecking LCP. Pick the one message that matters. If you truly must rotate, see the Carousel reference and make it user-driven, not auto-advancing.

Placement & hierarchy heuristics:
*   **Primary CTA above the fold, always.** If the visitor has to scroll to find the action, you've added friction to the highest-intent moment.
*   **One primary CTA.** A secondary is fine as a ghost/text button; a second _primary_ means you've told the user nothing is important.
*   **Put the value proposition, not your company name, in the H1.** "Urban Jungle Goddess" is not a headline. "Afro-Futurist brand systems that make you unmissable" is.
*   **Keep the H1 and CTA in the first paint.** Media can stream in behind them; the promise and the action cannot wait on a 3MB JPEG.

## 8\. Heroes Across Design Systems
Same section, very different house philosophies. Knowing these makes you fluent anywhere.
*   **Material Design (Google):** No single "Hero" component; built from a full-bleed surface + display-scale type + a filled/tonal button. Material 3 "Expressive" pushes bolder shape and springier motion into hero moments. Elevation and dynamic color do the work.
*   **Apple HIG (marketing web):** Apple's own product pages are the reference hero: enormous restrained type, generous negative space, one crisp product image, often a scroll-driven reveal. Minimal chrome, maximal focus. On-device, the equivalent is a large-title screen.
*   **Fluent (Microsoft):** Marketing heroes lean on acrylic/mica materials and a calm, information-forward tone; product surfaces skip the hero for a command-bar layout.
*   **Ant / enterprise:** Admin products rarely use a marketing hero at all — a `PageHeader` (title + breadcrumb + actions) is the closest analog. When the marketing site needs one, it's a straightforward centered or split block.
*   **Tailwind (+ Tailwind UI / Hero patterns):** No prescribed hero, but Tailwind UI ships a dozen canonical hero layouts (centered, split, with screenshot, with gradient blob). The de-facto modern-web hero vocabulary.
*   **shadcn/Radix:** No hero primitive — it's a composition (typography + Button + `next/image`). You own the markup; the system gives you the button and tokens.
*   **Bootstrap:** Ships the **Jumbotron** heritage — now just a `.container` with `.py-5`, display headings (`.display-1`), and `.lead` text. The original "hero as a framework class."
*   **Brand/expressive (agencies, launches):** WebGL, scroll-jacking, custom cursors, aurora gradients. Rules relax for spectacle — but LCP, contrast, and reduced-motion do not.

## 8b. Visual Styles (the same hero, eleven aesthetics)
Design systems are _whose_ rules; visual styles are _which look_. The same semantic hero — H1, subhead, one primary CTA, media, scrim — can wear any of these skins. See them all in the Design Styles (visual languages) guide.
*   **Flat:** solid or single-gradient background, crisp type, no shadow. Fastest LCP, reads instantly.
*   **Material:** elevated content card floating over the media, bold display type, one filled CTA with a ripple.
*   **Glassmorphism:** a frosted `backdrop-filter` panel holding the copy over a vivid photo — doubles as the scrim, but _watch contrast_ over busy areas.
*   **Liquid Glass (the 2026 trend):** Apple's iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe language. The copy sits on a translucent slab that _refracts_ the media behind it with a specular rim and a slow moving sheen, not a static blur. Gorgeous over UJG's deep Eminence media; guard contrast and reduced-motion.
*   **Neumorphism:** soft same-color surface with dual shadows. Weak for heroes — contrast is too low for a headline that has to shout. Accent panels only.
*   **Skeuomorphism:** textured "real material" background (paper, metal, fabric) with beveled type. Editorial, tactile, heavy.
*   **Neo-Brutalism:** hard offset shadows, thick borders, clashing color blocks, zero radius, oversized type. Maximum personality, zero subtlety — great for a bold campaign hero.
*   **Claymorphism:** puffy rounded panels, soft glow, friendly big radius. Playful product heroes.
*   **Aurora / Gradient:** animated multi-hue gradient mesh behind big type. Premium and cheap-to-load — honor `prefers-reduced-motion` (freeze the animation).
*   **Minimal / Swiss:** a grid, one line of huge type, one CTA, near-zero decoration. Lets the words carry everything.
*   **UJG Brand:** Goldenrod headline accents and CTA over a deep Eminence-to-Night gradient or a scrimmed jungle image, confident radius, warm glow. Afro-Futurist and premium — the house default.

**Rule that never changes:** the style is skin. The hero's single-H1 semantics, landmark, keyboard focus on the CTA, ≥44px target, scrim contrast, and reduced-motion handling stay identical across all eleven. Guard glass / liquid-glass / neumorphism for contrast; guard aurora / liquid-glass for `prefers-reduced-motion`.

## 9\. The Code
### 9.1 HTML (the semantic foundation)
The hero is a labeled landmark section, not a `<div>` soup. The order of parts and the single `<h1>` are the whole game.

```html
<!-- Hero lives INSIDE <main>, below the <header>/nav masthead -->
<section class="hero" aria-labelledby="hero-title">
  <!-- Media: the LCP element. Note the priority hints (see 9.5). -->
  <picture class="hero__media">
    <source media="(max-width: 640px)" srcset="/hero-mobile.avif" type="image/avif">
    <source media="(max-width: 640px)" srcset="/hero-mobile.webp" type="image/webp">
    <source srcset="/hero-desktop.avif" type="image/avif">
    <source srcset="/hero-desktop.webp" type="image/webp">
    <img src="/hero-desktop.jpg" alt=""
         width="1920" height="1080"
         fetchpriority="high" decoding="async">
  </picture>

  <!-- Scrim: guarantees text legibility over ANY image -->
  <div class="hero__scrim" aria-hidden="true"></div>

  <!-- Content -->
  <div class="hero__content">
    <p class="hero__eyebrow">For logistics teams</p>
    <h1 id="hero-title" class="hero__title">
      See every shipment in one place, in real time
    </h1>
    <p class="hero__subhead">
      Stop stitching spreadsheets together. Track, forecast, and flag delays
      from a single dashboard your whole team can read.
    </p>
    <div class="hero__actions">
      <a href="/start" class="btn btn--primary btn--lg">Start free</a>
      <a href="/demo" class="btn btn--ghost btn--lg">Watch 2-min demo</a>
    </div>
    <ul class="hero__trust" aria-label="Trusted by">
      <li>10,000+ shipments tracked daily</li>
      <li>4.9/5 average rating</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</section>
```

Key HTML rules:
*   **Exactly one** **`<h1>`****, and it's the value proposition** — not the logo, not the eyebrow. The eyebrow is a `<p>`, never an `<h2>` above the H1 (that breaks heading order).
*   **Decorative hero image gets** **`alt=""`****.** If the image carries meaning the text doesn't, describe it; if it's mood behind readable copy, empty alt so screen readers skip it.
*   **`width`****/****`height`** **on the** **`<img>`** so the browser reserves the box and you get zero layout shift (CLS).
*   **`fetchpriority="high"`** **on the hero image** and **never** `loading="lazy"` on it. This is the single most impactful line in the whole doc — see §9.5.
*   **`<section aria-labelledby>`** ties the landmark to the H1 so assistive tech announces the region by its headline.

### 9.2 CSS (layered stack, scrim, responsive, aspect)
The hero is a single positioning context with media, scrim, and content stacked. CSS Grid stacking (everything in one cell) beats `position: absolute` for the layers.

```css
.hero {
  position: relative;
  display: grid;
  grid-template-areas: "stack";
  min-height: 80svh;            /* svh, NOT vh — no mobile toolbar clip */
  isolation: isolate;          /* new stacking context for z-index sanity */
}
.hero > * { grid-area: stack; } /* media, scrim, content share one cell */

.hero__media {
  z-index: 0;
  overflow: hidden;
  background: #042D1D;          /* fallback floor if the image fails */
}
.hero__media img {
  width: 100%;
  height: 100%;
  object-fit: cover;            /* fill the box, crop gracefully */
  object-position: center;
}

/* The scrim: a gradient, not a flat wash — darkest where the text sits */
.hero__scrim {
  z-index: 1;
  background: linear-gradient(
    to right,
    rgba(10, 10, 10, 0.82) 0%,
    rgba(10, 10, 10, 0.55) 45%,
    rgba(10, 10, 10, 0.15) 100%
  );
}

.hero__content {
  z-index: 2;
  align-self: center;
  max-width: 42rem;            /* cap the measure even full-bleed */
  padding: clamp(1.5rem, 5vw, 6rem);
  color: #E8E6E1;
}
.hero__eyebrow {
  text-transform: uppercase;
  letter-spacing: 0.08em;
  font-size: 0.875rem;
  color: #DCA424;             /* Goldenrod accent */
}
.hero__title {
  font-size: clamp(2rem, 6vw + 1rem, 4.5rem);  /* fluid, no breakpoint snap */
  line-height: 1.05;
  text-wrap: balance;         /* even ragged lines, no orphan word */
  margin: 0.5rem 0 1rem;
}
.hero__subhead {
  font-size: clamp(1rem, 1vw + 0.75rem, 1.25rem);
  max-width: 60ch;
  opacity: 0.92;
}
.hero__actions { display: flex; flex-wrap: wrap; gap: 1rem; margin-top: 2rem; }

/* Split layout on desktop: no scrim needed, media in its own column */
@media (min-width: 900px) {
  .hero--split { grid-template-areas: "content media"; grid-template-columns: 1fr 1fr; }
  .hero--split .hero__scrim { display: none; }
}

/* Reserve the media box up front so nothing reflows on load */
.hero__media { aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; }

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  .hero * { animation: none !important; transition: none !important; }
}
```

Why the gradient scrim and not a flat overlay: a flat `rgba(0,0,0,.4)` dims the _whole_ image evenly, muddying the pretty parts to rescue the text corner. A directional gradient keeps the media vivid where there's no text and heavy where the copy sits. **Never rely on the image happening to have a dark corner** — art gets swapped, CMS editors upload bright photos, and your white headline vanishes. The scrim is the guarantee.

### 9.3 Text-over-image legibility (the contrast section)
This is where most heroes silently fail accessibility. White text over a photo passes on your monitor and fails over the one bright cloud in the top-left. Bulletproofing:
*   **A scrim you control, sized to the text region.** Gradient (above), or a blur-behind panel (glass style), or a solid text column (split layout). One of these is non-negotiable over any photographic hero.
*   **Test contrast against the** **_lightest_** **pixels under the text**, not the average. WCAG AA is ≥4.5:1 for the subhead and ≥3:1 for large H1 text — but hit 4.5:1 everywhere and stop worrying.
*   **`text-shadow`** **as belt-and-suspenders**, not the primary fix: `text-shadow: 0 1px 3px rgba(0,0,0,.6);` buys a little insurance but can't rescue text over a genuinely busy image.
*   **A** **`text-box`** **/ paint-order trick for stubborn cases:** a subtle blurred dark box that only appears behind the text using `box-decoration-break`, or a `background: rgba()` on the content block itself.

```css
/* When a gradient isn't enough — a soft readable panel behind the copy */
.hero--photo .hero__content {
  background: linear-gradient(rgba(10,10,10,.55), rgba(10,10,10,.55));
  border-radius: 12px;
}
.hero__title, .hero__subhead { text-shadow: 0 1px 3px rgba(0,0,0,.5); }
```

### 9.4 Background video hero (the expensive one)
Video heroes are atmospheric and _costly_. Do them right or not at all.

```html
<section class="hero" aria-labelledby="hero-title">
  <video class="hero__media" autoplay muted loop playsinline
         poster="/hero-poster.jpg" preload="none"
         aria-hidden="true">
    <source src="/hero.webm" type="video/webm">
    <source src="/hero.mp4"  type="video/mp4">
  </video>
  <div class="hero__scrim" aria-hidden="true"></div>
  <div class="hero__content"><!-- H1, subhead, CTA --></div>
</section>
```

```js
// Respect reduced motion AND reduced data: don't even fetch the video.
const video = document.querySelector(".hero video");
const noMotion = matchMedia("(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)").matches;
const saveData = navigator.connection?.saveData
  || ["slow-2g", "2g"].includes(navigator.connection?.effectiveType);

if (noMotion || saveData) {
  video.removeAttribute("autoplay");
  video.load();                 // keeps the poster, skips the download
} else {
  video.preload = "auto";
  video.play().catch(() => {}); // some browsers still block; poster stays
}
```

The non-negotiables for a video hero:
*   **`muted`** **+** **`playsinline`** **+** **`autoplay`** — autoplay is blocked without `muted`, and iOS fullscreens the video without `playsinline`.
*   **A real** **`poster`** — this is what paints first and what should be your LCP element. The video itself must _never_ be the thing the user waits on.
*   **`preload="none"`** **by default**, upgraded to `auto` only after the reduced-motion/data checks pass. Don't push megabytes at a phone on cellular.
*   **`prefers-reduced-motion`** **stops autoplay** — motion-sensitive users get the still poster, full stop.
*   **The video is decorative** (`aria-hidden`); all meaning lives in the H1/subhead/CTA. Never put essential info only in the video.
*   **LCP cost reality:** a video hero's LCP is the _poster_, so optimize the poster like a background image (§9.5). The video weight then hits your total load budget and mobile data — budget for it or ship a gradient instead.

### 9.5 The LCP reality (THE performance section)
Read this twice. **The hero image or the H1 is almost always your Largest Contentful Paint element** — the largest thing painted in the first viewport. Google grades LCP as a Core Web Vital (good = under 2.5s at the 75th percentile). Your hero _is_ your LCP score. Everything here is about making that one element appear fast.

**The cardinal sins (each one alone can wreck LCP):**
*   **`loading="lazy"`** **on the hero image.** This is the most common and most damaging hero mistake in existence. Lazy-loading defers the fetch — but the hero is _in_ the viewport, so you've deliberately delayed your own LCP element. Hero media is `loading="eager"` (the default) and `fetchpriority="high"`, **never** lazy.
*   **No priority hint.** The browser doesn't _know_ the hero image matters more than the footer logo. Tell it: `fetchpriority="high"` on the `<img>`, and a `<link rel="preload">` so the fetch starts before the CSS/JS even parses.
*   **Hero image imported through JS/a component that hydrates first.** If the image URL only appears after a bundle runs, the fetch starts late. Put the LCP image in the initial HTML.
*   **A web-font H1 that blocks paint.** If the H1 is the LCP element and its font isn't loaded, you get invisible text until the font arrives. Use `font-display: swap` (or `optional`) and preload the hero font.

**The preload + format recipe:**

```html
<!-- In <head>: start the LCP image fetch immediately, before CSS parses -->
<link rel="preload" as="image"
      href="/hero-desktop.avif" type="image/avif"
      imagesrcset="/hero-mobile.avif 640w, /hero-desktop.avif 1920w"
      imagesizes="100vw" fetchpriority="high">

<!-- Preload the hero H1 font so text paints without a swap-jump -->
<link rel="preload" as="font" href="/fonts/methanerse.woff2"
      type="font/woff2" crossorigin>
```

**Image optimization checklist for the hero (do all of these):**
*   **Modern formats:** AVIF first, WebP fallback, JPEG floor. AVIF is typically 50%+ smaller than JPEG at the same quality.
*   **Right-size per breakpoint** with `srcset`/`sizes` — don't ship a 1920px image to a 390px phone.
*   **Compress aggressively.** A hero JPEG over ~200KB is usually lazy encoding. Target well under that with AVIF.
*   **LQIP / dominant color** as the `background-color` so the box is filled the instant the section paints, before the full image arrives.
*   **CDN + long cache headers**, ideally an image CDN that negotiates format and size per request.
*   **Reserve the box** (`width`/`height` or `aspect-ratio`) so the loaded image causes zero CLS.

Get these right and the hero — your biggest paint — becomes your _fastest_ meaningful paint. That's the whole ballgame for Core Web Vitals.

### 9.6 Responsive art-direction (`<picture>`)
A 21:9 cinematic desktop crop becomes an unreadable letterbox sliver on a phone. Art-direction serves a _different crop_ (not just a smaller file) per viewport — that's what `<picture>` with `media` is for (vs. `srcset` alone, which only swaps resolution).

```html
<picture>
  <!-- Portrait crop, subject centered, for phones -->
  <source media="(max-width: 640px)"
          srcset="/hero-portrait.avif 640w, /hero-portrait@2x.avif 1280w"
          sizes="100vw" type="image/avif">
  <!-- Wide cinematic crop for everything larger -->
  <source media="(min-width: 641px)"
          srcset="/hero-wide.avif 1280w, /hero-wide.avif 1920w, /hero-wide.avif 2560w"
          sizes="100vw" type="image/avif">
  <img src="/hero-wide.jpg" alt=""
       width="1920" height="1080"
       fetchpriority="high" decoding="async">
</picture>
```

`srcset` = "same picture, pick a size." `<picture><source media>` = "different picture per condition." Heroes usually need _both_: art-direction across the mobile/desktop break, and resolution switching within each.

### 9.7 React + TypeScript (a proper reusable Hero)
A hero component's API is layout + media source + copy + CTAs. Keep it a composition, and keep the LCP hints as first-class props.

```tsx
import { ReactNode } from "react";

type Layout = "centered" | "split" | "asymmetric";

interface HeroAction {
  label: string;
  href: string;
  variant?: "primary" | "ghost";
}

interface HeroProps {
  eyebrow?: string;
  title: string;                 // becomes the single <h1>
  subhead?: string;
  actions: HeroAction[];         // first one is the primary CTA
  media: {
    srcSet: { mobile: string; desktop: string };
    fallback: string;
    alt?: string;                // "" if decorative
  };
  layout?: Layout;
  trust?: ReactNode;
}

export function Hero({
  eyebrow, title, subhead, actions,
  media, layout = "centered", trust,
}: HeroProps) {
  return (
    <section
      className={`hero hero--${layout}`}
      aria-labelledby="hero-title"
    >
      <picture className="hero__media">
        <source media="(max-width: 640px)" srcSet={media.srcSet.mobile} type="image/avif" />
        <source srcSet={media.srcSet.desktop} type="image/avif" />
        {/* fetchpriority high + eager: this is the LCP element */}
        <img
          src={media.fallback}
          alt={media.alt ?? ""}
          width={1920}
          height={1080}
          // @ts-expect-error fetchpriority is valid HTML, typings lag
          fetchpriority="high"
          decoding="async"
        />
      </picture>

      {layout !== "split" && <div className="hero__scrim" aria-hidden="true" />}

      <div className="hero__content">
        {eyebrow && <p className="hero__eyebrow">{eyebrow}</p>}
        <h1 id="hero-title" className="hero__title">{title}</h1>
        {subhead && <p className="hero__subhead">{subhead}</p>}
        <div className="hero__actions">
          {actions.map((a, i) => (
            <a
              key={a.href}
              href={a.href}
              className={`btn btn--lg btn--${a.variant ?? (i === 0 ? "primary" : "ghost")}`}
            >
              {a.label}
            </a>
          ))}
        </div>
        {trust && <div className="hero__trust">{trust}</div>}
      </div>
    </section>
  );
}
```

Why this is the senior version, not the junior one:
*   **`title`** **is a plain string that becomes the** **`<h1>`** — the component _owns_ the single-H1 contract instead of leaving it to the caller to remember.
*   **`actions`** **is an ordered array where index 0 is primary** — encodes "one primary CTA" into the data shape.
*   **The LCP hints (****`fetchpriority`****, eager,** **`width`****/****`height`****) are baked in**, not left to whoever uses the component to remember on a deadline.
*   **`alt`** **defaults to** **`""`** so a decorative hero image doesn't accidentally get announced.

### 9.8 Next.js (App Router + `next/image` priority)
Next is where the hero's performance story is easiest to get _right_ — and easiest to break by forgetting one prop. The hero can be a **Server Component** (it's static content); only interactive bits need `"use client"`.

```tsx
// app/(marketing)/page.tsx — a Server Component, no "use client" needed
import Image from "next/image";
import Link from "next/link";

export default function Home() {
  return (
    <main>
      <section className="hero" aria-labelledby="hero-title">
        <Image
          src="/hero-desktop.avif"
          alt=""
          fill
          priority                 // THE hero prop: preloads + fetchpriority=high, disables lazy
          sizes="100vw"
          quality={70}
          className="hero__media"
          style={{ objectFit: "cover" }}
        />
        <div className="hero__scrim" aria-hidden="true" />
        <div className="hero__content">
          <p className="hero__eyebrow">For logistics teams</p>
          <h1 id="hero-title" class="hero__title">
            See every shipment in one place, in real time
          </h1>
          <p class="hero__subhead">
            Track, forecast, and flag delays from a single dashboard.
          </p>
          <div class="hero__actions">
            <Link href="/start" className="btn btn--primary btn--lg">Start free</Link>
            <Link href="/demo" className="btn btn--ghost btn--lg">Watch demo</Link>
          </div>
        </div>
      </section>
    </main>
  );
}
```

The one prop that matters: **`priority`** on the hero `<Image>`. It makes Next preload the image and set `fetchpriority="high"`, and it _disables_ the default lazy-loading. Forgetting `priority` on the hero is the #1 Next.js LCP regression — the image lazy-loads and your LCP jumps a full second. Every other image on the page stays lazy; the hero is the _one_ that gets `priority`. Pair with `sizes="100vw"` so Next generates the right responsive set, and use `fill` + `object-fit: cover` for a full-bleed background.

### 9.12 Web Component (framework-agnostic)
A hero that works in any stack, wrapping a real `<img>` (light DOM) so the browser's preload scanner can still find the LCP image — a Shadow DOM `<img>` is invisible to the preload scanner, which would hurt LCP.


**LCP (Lighthouse / WebPageVitals in CI):** assert the hero is your LCP element _and_ that it's fast. In a Lighthouse CI budget, fail the build if `largest-contentful-paint` exceeds 2500ms. In a Playwright + `web-vitals` check, capture the LCP entry and assert its `element` is the hero image/H1 — if your LCP element is something _else_, your hero isn't the biggest paint and something's mis-sized.

```typescript
// Playwright: confirm LCP element IS the hero image and it's under budget
test("hero is the LCP element and paints fast", async ({ page }) => {
  await page.goto("/");
  const lcp = await page.evaluate(() => new Promise<number>((resolve) => {
    new PerformanceObserver((list) => {
      const e = list.getEntries().at(-1) as any;
      resolve(e.startTime);
    }).observe({ type: "largest-contentful-paint", buffered: true });
  }));
  expect(lcp).toBeLessThan(2500);
});
```

What to assert (and skip):
*   **Do test:** exactly one `<h1>`, the headline text, the primary CTA is a reachable link with the right href, decorative image has `alt=""`, no axe violations, LCP under budget.
*   **Don't test:** exact scrim opacity, hex colors, pixel positions. Those are skin — they break tests without catching real bugs.
*   **Bonus:** a visual-regression snapshot (Percy/Chromatic) catches scrim/contrast regressions a unit test can't see.

## 10\. Accessibility (non-negotiable)
The hero is simple structurally, which is exactly why the a11y misses are silent.
*   **One** **`<h1>`****, and it's the value proposition.** Screen-reader users navigate by headings; the hero's headline is the page's title-in-context. Never skip from the eyebrow to an `<h2>` — the eyebrow is a `<p>`.
*   **Landmark the region.** `<section aria-labelledby="hero-title">` inside `<main>`. The hero is _not_ the page `<header>` (that's the masthead/nav).
*   **Contrast ≥ 4.5:1** for the subhead and small text over media, ≥ 3:1 for large H1 text — measured against the _lightest_ pixels under the text, not the average. The scrim exists to guarantee this. (See §9.3.)
*   **Decorative media gets** **`alt=""`** and `aria-hidden` on the scrim/video. Meaningful media gets a real, described `alt`. Don't narrate mood photos.
*   **The CTA is a real** **`<a>`****/****`<button>`** with a visible `:focus-visible` ring and a ≥44px target — see the Button reference. Focus order runs eyebrow → H1 → subhead → primary → secondary, matching visual order.
*   **`prefers-reduced-motion`** stops video autoplay and freezes animated gradients/parallax. Motion-sensitive users get a still hero, no exceptions.
*   **Don't trap meaning in the image.** If the only place a discount or a key claim appears is baked into the hero JPEG, screen readers and translators can't reach it. Live text, always.
*   **Zoom to 200%** without the headline clipping or the CTA leaving the viewport — fluid type and `svh` heights handle this if you didn't hard-code pixel heights.

The hero looks solved but keeps moving (2026-aware):
*   **Liquid Glass content slabs:** the copy panel is a refractive translucent material (iOS 26 / macOS Tahoe) floating over the media — glassmorphism grown up. Sings over UJG's deep Eminence backdrops.
*   **Scroll-driven reveals with the native API:** CSS `animation-timeline: scroll()` / `view()` drives parallax and headline reveals with zero JS and no jank — replacing the old scroll-jacking libraries.
*   **View Transitions API:** the hero image morphs smoothly into the next page's element on navigation, native and lightweight.
*   **AI-generated / personalized heroes:** headline and media swapped per segment or per referrer at the edge, so the logistics visitor and the retail visitor see different value props from the same page.
*   **Generative/streaming media:** subtle shader or WebGL ambient backgrounds — beautiful, but ship a static fallback and respect reduced-motion and battery.
*   **Spatial / depth-aware heroes** carried over from visionOS: soft 3D layering and content that reacts to device motion.
*   **`text-wrap: balance`** **/** **`pretty`** for headline typography that used to need manual `<br>` babysitting — now a one-line CSS default.

## 12\. Conversion Killers (mistakes that bleed money silently)
None throw an error. They just quietly drop your numbers — which makes them dangerous. Every one is fixable in an afternoon.
*   **A slow-painting hero.** The lazy-loaded or un-preloaded hero image pushes LCP past 2.5s; visitors bounce before the pitch renders, and Google demotes the page. This is the most expensive hero mistake and the most invisible — it never errors, it just costs you traffic and conversions at once. (See §9.5.)
*   **A headline that's about you, not them.** "Welcome to Urban Jungle Goddess" or "We're a full-service agency" wastes the one line that matters. Lead with the visitor's outcome: "Brand systems that make you unmissable."
*   **No clear primary CTA / competing CTAs.** Three equal buttons above the fold = decision paralysis = they leave. One primary, one optional ghost secondary. Never two primaries.
*   **Unreadable text over the image.** No scrim, banking on the photo having a convenient dark corner. Swap the photo and the headline vanishes. The scrim is not optional.
*   **The CTA below the fold.** If the visitor has to scroll to find the action at the highest-intent moment, you've added friction exactly where you can least afford it.
*   **Layout shift on load (CLS).** The image loads and shoves the headline and CTA down half a second after the user went to click. Reserve the box (`aspect-ratio` / `width`/`height`).
*   **Autoplaying video with sound**, or a heavy video pushed to a phone on cellular. Instant back-button, and a data-cost betrayal of the visitor.
*   **The rotating carousel hero.** Users rarely see slide 2+, the slides fight each other, and every slide's image loads and wrecks LCP. One message beats five rotating ones. (See §7.)
*   **A hero taller than the whole first screen with the value prop below it.** A 120vh brand photo where the headline only appears after a scroll is a screensaver, not a hero.

**The through-line:** these all fail _quietly_. No log, no crash — just a lower conversion number that's easy to blame on "the market." Audit your hero before you audit your ad spend; it's the cheapest conversion lift you own.

## 13\. Advanced Patterns (tokens, TS-enforced a11y, art-direction at scale)
### Design tokens for the hero system
Drive the scrim, heights, and type off tokens so a rebrand or dark-mode is a token swap, not a rewrite.

```css
:root {
  --hero-min-h: 80svh;
  --hero-scrim-from: rgba(10, 10, 10, 0.82);
  --hero-scrim-to:   rgba(10, 10, 10, 0.12);
  --hero-title-size: clamp(2rem, 6vw + 1rem, 4.5rem);
  --hero-eyebrow: #DCA424;      /* Goldenrod */
  --hero-fallback-bg: #042D1D;  /* Dark Green floor if media fails */
}
.hero { min-height: var(--hero-min-h); }
.hero__scrim { background: linear-gradient(to right, var(--hero-scrim-from), var(--hero-scrim-to)); }
.hero__title { font-size: var(--hero-title-size); }
```

### TypeScript: force the single-H1 and alt contracts at compile time
Model the hero copy so a headline is _required_ and a decorative image must explicitly declare itself decorative — catching the two most common hero a11y bugs before runtime.

```typescript
type HeroMedia =
  | { decorative: true; src: string }                 // alt="" enforced downstream
  | { decorative: false; src: string; alt: string };  // alt is now MANDATORY

interface HeroContent {
  title: string;                 // required — no headless hero
  subhead?: string;
  primaryCta: { label: string; href: string };  // exactly one primary, required
  secondaryCta?: { label: string; href: string };
  media: HeroMedia;
}
```

Now a hero without a headline, or a meaningful image without an `alt`, won't compile. The type system enforces the rules so a human on a deadline doesn't have to remember them.

### Art-direction at scale
Standardize named crops in your image pipeline — `hero-wide` (16:9), `hero-portrait` (4:5), `hero-square` (1:1) — and have the CMS/DAM generate all three from one upload. Your `<picture>` then references crop _names_, not hand-uploaded files, so editors can't ship a letterboxed mobile hero by accident.

### Progressive enhancement
The hero must be fully legible and clickable with **zero JavaScript** — it's the first thing that paints and often paints before any bundle runs. Video/parallax/animation are enhancements layered on top of a working image-or-gradient hero, never prerequisites for reading the pitch.

## 14\. Performance & Bundle Cost
The hero _is_ the performance conversation for most pages. Recap and extend:
*   **LCP is the hero.** Everything in §9.5 — preload, `fetchpriority="high"`, never lazy, AVIF/WebP, right-sizing, `next/image priority`. This is where your Core Web Vitals score is won or lost.
*   **Reserve the box** (`aspect-ratio` / `width`+`height`) so the hero contributes **zero** to CLS.
*   **Video is a weight tax.** A background video can be several MB. It doesn't count toward LCP (the poster does), but it competes for bandwidth with everything else and hits mobile data. Gate it behind reduced-motion/data checks and `preload="none"`.
*   **Fonts block the H1 paint.** If the headline is your LCP element, an unpreloaded web font delays it or causes a swap-jump. Preload the hero font and use `font-display: swap`/`optional`.
*   **Keep the hero out of the JS critical path.** Render it in static HTML / a Server Component so the LCP image URL is in the initial markup for the preload scanner — not injected after a bundle hydrates.
*   **Animate only** **`transform`****/****`opacity`** for any hero motion (parallax, sheen). Animating `width`/`top`/`background-position` forces reflow every frame.
*   **3D/WebGL heroes** are the heaviest option on the board — main-thread cost, GPU, battery. Lazy-init after first paint, and always ship a static fallback for the initial LCP.

## 15\. Security
A hero is mostly presentation, so the honest attack surface is small — but "small" isn't "none," and the holes it does have tend to ship silently. Every CTA that opens a new tab needs `rel="noopener noreferrer"` on its `target="_blank"`, and when the CTA `href` is CMS- or user-supplied, validate the scheme against an allowlist (`https:`/`mailto:`/`tel:`) so nobody slips a `javascript:` URL into your prettiest button. The background/hero image is the sneaky one: if its `src` comes from a CMS and your server proxies or optimizes it, an attacker-controlled URL turns your image pipeline into an SSRF probe against your own internal network — allowlist the image hosts before you fetch. That same off-origin media is a referrer-leak vector, so set `referrerpolicy="no-referrer"` (or at least `strict-origin-when-cross-origin`) on hero images pointing off-domain. Headline, eyebrow, and CTA copy pulled from a CMS must be rendered as text, never `innerHTML` — the hero paints first, so a stored-XSS payload there executes before anything else on the page even loads. Finally, the hero usually owns both the page's LCP and its first analytics event, so audit the tracking params you append to CTA hrefs: don't let session tokens, emails, or other PII ride along in a query string that ends up in `Referer` headers and third-party logs.

## 16\. Senior-Level Checklist (ship-ready)
Before a hero is "done":
- [ ] Exactly one `<h1>`, and it's the value proposition (not the logo/eyebrow).
- [ ] Hero region is a landmark: `<section aria-labelledby>` inside `<main>`.
- [ ] Hero image is **eager +** **`fetchpriority="high"`** **\+ preloaded** — never `loading="lazy"`. (`priority` on `next/image`.)
- [ ] Media served as AVIF/WebP, right-sized per breakpoint via `srcset`, art-directed via `<picture>` for mobile.
- [ ] Box reserved (`aspect-ratio` or `width`/`height`) — zero CLS on load.
- [ ] Measured LCP under 2.5s at p75; the hero image/H1 _is_ the LCP element.
- [ ] Scrim guarantees text contrast ≥4.5:1 against the lightest pixels — no reliance on a dark corner.
- [ ] Full-viewport heroes use `100svh`, not `100vh`.
- [ ] One primary CTA above the fold; at most one ghost secondary.
- [ ] Video hero: muted + playsinline + autoplay, real poster, `preload="none"`, reduced-motion/data gated.
- [ ] Decorative media `alt=""` + `aria-hidden` scrim; meaningful media described.
- [ ] `prefers-reduced-motion` stops autoplay/animation; a media failure still leaves a legible, on-brand hero.
- [ ] CTA has visible focus ring and ≥44px target; focus order matches visual order.
- [ ] Headline uses fluid `clamp()` type and `text-wrap: balance`; measure capped ~60ch.

## 17\. Visual Styles Reference (skin only)
The eleven languages, applied to the hero — semantics, single-H1, landmark, scrim contrast, focus, target size, and reduced-motion never change:
*   **Flat** — solid/single-gradient background, crisp type, no shadow. Fastest LCP.
*   **Material** — content on an elevated surface card, bold display type, one filled CTA with ripple.
*   **Glassmorphism** — frosted `backdrop-filter` copy panel over vivid media (doubles as scrim; guard contrast).
*   **Liquid Glass** — refractive translucent slab with specular rim + moving sheen; guard contrast + reduced-motion.
*   **Neumorphism** — soft dual-shadow surface; too low-contrast for a shouting headline, accent panels only.
*   **Skeuomorphism** — textured real-material background, beveled type; editorial and heavy.
*   **Neo-Brutalism** — hard offset shadows, thick borders, clashing blocks, oversized type; max personality.
*   **Claymorphism** — puffy rounded panels, soft glow, big radius; playful.
*   **Aurora / Gradient** — animated multi-hue mesh behind big type; freeze on reduced-motion.
*   **Minimal / Swiss** — grid, one huge line of type, one CTA, near-zero decoration.
*   **UJG Brand** — Goldenrod accents + CTA over a deep Eminence-to-Night gradient or scrimmed jungle image, confident radius, warm glow. Afro-Futurist, premium, the house default.

Full guidance in the Design Styles (visual languages) guide. Cross-reference the Image, Video, Button, and Carousel references — the hero composes all four.

---

**Bottom line:** the hero is the smallest section with the largest blast radius. It's your bounce line, your brand handshake, and — because the headline or image is almost always your Largest Contentful Paint element — it's the single most important _performance_ element on the page. Nail the three seconds: one value-proposition H1, one primary CTA above the fold, a scrim that guarantees legibility over any image, and a media layer that's eager-loaded, `fetchpriority="high"`, preloaded, and never lazy. Get the hero right and you've won the visitor before they've read a single other word — get it wrong and none of the rest of the page ever gets seen.
