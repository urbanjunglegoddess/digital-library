## 1 · Definition
The plain, precise meaning. State what the term **is**, what it is **not**
(name the thing it gets confused with), and the one-line rule of thumb for when
the term genuinely applies.

## 2 · Etymology & origin
Where the term comes from — first coinage or root, who introduced it, and how
its meaning has shifted since. Skip only if genuinely unknown (say so).

## 3 · Operation instances — Common
How the term **typically operates in practice**: the everyday, expected cases.
List each as a named instance with the condition that triggers it and what it
looks like when it happens. These are the cases a reader will meet most often.

## 4 · Operation instances — Rare
The **edge, exceptional, or little-known** instances. For each, name the
condition that produces it and say plainly why it is rare — an unusual input, a
boundary case, a historical quirk. Do not pad this with common cases.

## 5 · Research findings
What the gathered research and literature actually say. Make claims, and attach
the specific source each claim rests on (by its `sources` entry). Separate
"the evidence shows X" from "some argue X" — do not present opinion as finding.

## 6 · Proven occurrences
**Documented, verifiable** real-world occurrences — not hypotheticals. Each one
gets a date or context and a citation that proves it happened. If a claimed
occurrence cannot be sourced, it belongs in §3/§4 as an instance, not here.

## 7 · Distinctions & commonly confused with
The near-terms and how this one differs from each, and the trap that each
confusion sets. One row per confusable term.

## 8 · Usage & register
When and where to use the term — domain, formality, and audience. Give one or
two example sentences that use it correctly.

## 9 · Related terms
Cross-links to adjacent entries (mirror the frontmatter `related`), each with a
single line on how it relates to this term.

## 10 · Sources & evidence
The full citation list backing everything above — mirror the frontmatter
`sources` and add anything cited inline. Prefer primary sources; mark secondary
ones as such.
